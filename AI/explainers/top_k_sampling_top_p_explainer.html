Top-k, Top-p (Nucleus), Min-p Sampling

Controlling creativity and coherence in Large Language Model text generation
Foundational Deep Learning Generative AI O(V log V) or O(V)
1
Understanding Sampling Strategies
▼

What is it? When a Large Language Model (LLM) generates text, it predicts a probability distribution over its entire vocabulary for the next token. Simple strategies like "greedy decoding" (always picking the most probable token) often lead to repetitive or boring text. Pure random sampling from the full distribution can lead to chaotic or nonsensical output. Top-k, Top-p (nucleus), and Min-p sampling are techniques to intelligently prune this vocabulary, balancing creativity and coherence.

Analogy: Imagine you're a chef trying to pick the next ingredient for a dish.

  • Greedy: You always pick the most popular ingredient. Safe, but predictable.
  • Pure Sampling: You pick any ingredient from your pantry, weighted by popularity. Could be brilliant, could be a disaster.
  • Top-k: You only consider the k most popular ingredients. Limits chaos, but k is a fixed number, so if the popular ingredients are all very similar, you might still get repetitive results.
  • Top-p (nucleus): You consider only the smallest set of ingredients whose combined popularity reaches a certain threshold p. This adapts: if many ingredients are equally popular, you consider more; if one is dominant, you consider fewer.
  • Min-p: You only consider ingredients that are at least a certain minimum popularity. This explicitly prunes truly irrelevant options.

💡 If you remember one thing

All these methods prune the model's predicted vocabulary and re-normalize the probabilities of the remaining tokens. This allows for controlled randomness, avoiding both the dullness of greedy decoding and the incoherence of unconstrained sampling from a very long tail of low-probability tokens.

2
How It Works Internally
▼

From Logits to Probabilities

An LLM's final layer outputs a vector of "logits" — raw, unnormalized scores for each token in its vocabulary. These logits are converted into probabilities using the softmax function. A crucial parameter often applied here is temperature \(T\), which scales the logits before softmax: higher \(T\) flattens the distribution (more uniform, more diverse output), while lower \(T\) sharpens it (more peaked, more deterministic output).

$$P(w_i \mid \text{prev}) = \frac{e^{\text{logit}_i / T}}{\sum_{j=1}^{|V|} e^{\text{logit}_j / T}}$$

Pruning Strategies

Once probabilities are computed, the core idea is to filter the vocabulary based on these probabilities:

  • Top-k Sampling: Only the \(k\) tokens with the highest probabilities are considered. All other tokens are assigned a probability of zero. The probabilities of the remaining \(k\) tokens are then re-normalized so they sum to 1.
  • Top-p (Nucleus) Sampling: Tokens are sorted by probability in descending order. We then select the smallest set of top tokens whose cumulative probability sum is greater than or equal to \(p\). All other tokens are assigned zero probability, and the selected tokens' probabilities are re-normalized. This method dynamically adjusts the effective vocabulary size based on the shape of the distribution.
  • Min-p Sampling: Only tokens whose probability is greater than or equal to a threshold \(min\_p\) are considered. All tokens below this threshold are assigned zero probability, and the remaining tokens' probabilities are re-normalized. This is a simpler, absolute cutoff.

Design Decision: Why these specific methods?

These methods are heuristics designed to mitigate issues with both greedy and pure sampling. Greedy decoding is deterministic and often repetitive. Pure sampling from the full distribution (especially with a large vocabulary) often picks highly improbable tokens from the "long tail" of the distribution, leading to incoherent text. Top-k, Top-p, and Min-p aim to keep the generation diverse enough to be interesting, but constrained enough to be coherent, by focusing on the "plausible" tokens.

🔑 Key Property

The critical step after pruning the vocabulary is re-normalization. Without it, the probabilities of the remaining tokens would not sum to 1, violating the fundamental property of a probability distribution and leading to incorrect sampling behavior.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Numerically Stable Softmax with Temperature

Given a vector of logits \(z = [z_1, \dots, z_{|V|}]\), the softmax function with temperature \(T\) is:

$$P(w_i) = \frac{e^{z_i / T}}{\sum_{j=1}^{|V|} e^{z_j / T}}$$

For numerical stability, we subtract the maximum logit \(m = \max_j (z_j/T)\) before exponentiation (the log-sum-exp trick):

$$P(w_i) = \frac{e^{(z_i/T) - m}}{\sum_{j=1}^{|V|} e^{(z_j/T) - m}}$$

This ensures that the largest exponent is \(e^0=1\), preventing overflow while preserving the mathematical result.

Re-normalization After Pruning

Let \(P\) be the initial probability distribution. After a pruning strategy, we obtain a set of candidate tokens \(V_{\text{cand}}\) (e.g., top-k, top-p nucleus, or min-p tokens). The probabilities of tokens not in \(V_{\text{cand}}\) are set to zero. The probabilities of tokens in \(V_{\text{cand}}\) must then be re-normalized:

$$P'(w_i) = \begin{cases} \frac{P(w_i)}{\sum_{j \in V_{\text{cand}}} P(w_j)} & \text{if } w_i \in V_{\text{cand}} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

This new distribution \(P'\) is what we sample from. The denominator \(\sum_{j \in V_{\text{cand}}} P(w_j)\) is often called the "sum of kept probabilities" or "normalization constant."

⚠️ Common Pitfall

If the chosen sampling parameters (e.g., too small \(k\), too small \(p\), or too high \(min\_p\)) result in an empty set \(V_{\text{cand}}\), the normalization sum will be zero, leading to division by zero and `NaN` probabilities. Implementations must handle this by falling back to a safer strategy (e.g., greedy) or expanding the candidate set.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use which method:

  • Greedy Decoding (T=0, k=1, p=0): For highly deterministic tasks where exactness is paramount (e.g., code generation, factual recall if the model is perfect). Leads to repetitive output.
  • Pure Sampling (T=1, k=V, p=1): For maximum diversity and exploration, but high risk of incoherence, especially with large vocabularies.
  • Temperature Scaling: A fundamental control. Use \(T < 1\) for more focused, deterministic output; \(T > 1\) for more diverse, creative output. Often combined with other methods.
  • Top-k Sampling: Simple and effective for limiting the "long tail." Useful when you want a fixed number of options to choose from, regardless of their individual probabilities. Can be less robust if the distribution is very flat (many low-probability tokens in the top K).
  • Top-p (Nucleus) Sampling: Generally preferred for diverse yet coherent generation. It dynamically adjusts the effective vocabulary size based on the shape of the probability distribution, making it more robust than Top-k when distributions vary significantly.
  • Min-p Sampling: A simpler alternative to Top-p. Effective for ensuring that only tokens with a minimum level of plausibility are considered. Less adaptive than Top-p but easier to reason about.

Practical considerations:

  • Top-p is often combined with a small Top-k cutoff (e.g., `top_k=50, top_p=0.9`) to ensure a minimum number of tokens are always considered, even if the distribution is very sharp.
  • Experimentation is key! Optimal parameters for `T`, `k`, `p`, `min_p` depend heavily on the model, task, and desired output style.
  • These methods are applied *per token* during generation. The model generates one token, then re-evaluates the distribution for the next token based on the new context.
💡 Big Picture

These sampling strategies are not changing the underlying knowledge or capabilities of the LLM. They are sophisticated filtering mechanisms that allow us to steer the model's inherent randomness to produce outputs that are more aligned with human expectations for creativity, coherence, and usefulness.

Code Trace (Python/PyTorch-like)
▼
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
def sample_next_token(logits, temperature=1.0, top_k=0, top_p=0.0, min_p=0.0):
# 1. Apply temperature (and numerical stability trick)
logits = logits / temperature
probs = F.softmax(logits, dim=-1) # subtracts max logit internally for stability
# 2. Apply pruning strategy
if top_k > 0:
# Keep only top_k tokens, zero out others
values, indices = torch.topk(probs, top_k)
mask = torch.zeros_like(probs, dtype=torch.bool)
mask.scatter_(-1, indices, True)
probs = probs.masked_fill(~mask, 0.0)
if top_p > 0.0:
# Sort probabilities and find nucleus
sorted_probs, sorted_indices = torch.sort(probs, descending=True)
cumulative_probs = torch.cumsum(sorted_probs, dim=-1)
# Remove tokens above the top_p threshold
nucleus_mask = cumulative_probs - sorted_probs > top_p
sorted_probs.masked_fill_(nucleus_mask, 0.0)
# Re-scatter to original order
probs = torch.zeros_like(probs).scatter_(-1, sorted_indices, sorted_probs)
if min_p > 0.0:
# Keep only tokens above min_p threshold
probs = probs.masked_fill_(probs < min_p, 0.0)
# 3. Re-normalize probabilities (if any tokens remain)
if probs.sum() == 0: # Fallback to greedy if all pruned
return torch.argmax(logits, dim=-1)
probs = probs / probs.sum()
# 4. Sample a token
return torch.multinomial(probs, num_samples=1)
