Null & Alternative Hypothesis

The formal language of scientific claims — and how data decides between them.

Foundational Probability & Statistics Inferential Statistics Must Know
1
Understanding the Concept
▼

What is this?

Every scientific experiment starts with a claim — "this drug works," "this model is better," "coin is fair." Hypothesis testing is the mathematical framework that turns a claim into a decision backed by data. We define two competing stories about reality and then use probability to pick one.

The null hypothesis \(H_0\) is the default, skeptical story: "nothing happened, no effect, status quo." The alternative hypothesis \(H_1\) (or \(H_a\)) is the claim we want to prove: "something real is going on." We never directly prove \(H_1\); instead, we ask how surprised we'd be by our data if \(H_0\) were actually true.

Key Analogy

Think of \(H_0\) as the "innocent until proven guilty" presumption. The data is the evidence. The significance level \(\alpha\) is the standard of proof required for a conviction. We convict (reject \(H_0\)) only when the evidence is overwhelmingly improbable under innocence — not merely unlikely.

The One-Sentence Summary

Hypothesis testing quantifies how surprised our observed data should make us under the null, and rejects the null when that surprise crosses a pre-set threshold \(\alpha\).

If You Remember One Thing

We never accept \(H_0\) — we only "fail to reject" it. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. The p-value is \(P(\text{data this extreme} \mid H_0 \text{ is true})\), not the probability that \(H_0\) is true.

2
The Formal Framework
▼

Setting Up the Test

A hypothesis test for a population mean \(\mu\) has this structure. Given data \(x_1, \ldots, x_n\) i.i.d. from some population:

$$H_0: \mu = \mu_0 \quad \text{vs} \quad H_1: \mu \neq \mu_0 \quad (\text{two-tailed})$$
$$H_0: \mu \leq \mu_0 \quad \text{vs} \quad H_1: \mu > \mu_0 \quad (\text{one-tailed right})$$

The Test Statistic

We summarize the data into a single number that measures how far the sample is from what \(H_0\) predicts. For a z-test with known \(\sigma\):

$$Z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu_0}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}}$$

Under \(H_0\), \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\). We know its exact distribution — that's the key.

Significance Level \(\alpha\) and Rejection Region

The significance level \(\alpha\) (typically 0.05 or 0.01) is our pre-committed false-positive rate — the maximum probability we tolerate for wrongly rejecting a true \(H_0\). For a two-tailed test at \(\alpha = 0.05\):

$$\text{Reject } H_0 \text{ if } |Z| > z_{\alpha/2} = 1.960$$

The p-value

The p-value is the probability of observing a test statistic at least as extreme as the one computed, assuming \(H_0\) is true:

$$p = P(|Z| \geq |z_{\text{obs}}| \mid H_0) = 2\left[1 - \Phi(|z_{\text{obs}}|)\right]$$

where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal CDF. We reject \(H_0\) when \(p < \alpha\).

Common Misconception

The p-value is not the probability that \(H_0\) is true, nor the probability that your result is due to chance. It is a probability computed under the assumption that \(H_0\) is true. Bayesian inference is required to make statements about \(P(H_0 \mid \text{data})\).

Numerical Example

Suppose \(H_0: \mu=0\), \(H_1: \mu=0.5\), \(\sigma=1\), \(n=25\), \(\alpha=0.05\). Then \(\text{SE} = 1/\sqrt{25} = 0.2\), the non-centrality parameter is \(\delta/\text{SE} = 0.5/0.2 = 2.5\).

$$\beta = \Phi(z_{0.025} - \delta/\text{SE}) - \Phi(-z_{0.025} - \delta/\text{SE}) = \Phi(1.96 - 2.5) - \Phi(-1.96 - 2.5)$$
$$= \Phi(-0.54) - \Phi(-4.46) \approx 0.2946 - 0.000004 \approx 0.295$$
$$\text{Power} = 1 - \beta \approx 0.705$$
Power Formula

For a two-tailed z-test with true effect \(\delta = \mu_1 - \mu_0\), sample size \(n\), and known \(\sigma\):

$$\text{Power} = 1 - \beta = 1 - \Phi\!\left(z_{\alpha/2} - \frac{\delta\sqrt{n}}{\sigma}\right) + \Phi\!\left(-z_{\alpha/2} - \frac{\delta\sqrt{n}}{\sigma}\right)$$

Power increases with: larger \(|\delta|\), larger \(n\), larger \(\sigma\) does not help — it hurts (more noise). Lower \(\alpha\) also hurts power (stricter threshold).

Key Takeaways & Interview Tips
▼

Core Rules to Memorize

  • \(H_0\) represents the skeptical null (no effect, no difference, status quo).
  • \(H_1\) is the research claim we're trying to establish with evidence.
  • We never "accept \(H_0\)" — we only "fail to reject" it.
  • p-value \(= P(\text{data} \mid H_0)\), not \(P(H_0 \mid \text{data})\).
  • Lowering \(\alpha\) reduces Type I errors but increases Type II errors (less power).
  • Power \(= 1-\beta\) increases with larger \(n\), larger effect size, or larger \(\alpha\).

Common Interview Questions

  • Q: A/B test shows p = 0.03 at α = 0.05. Should we ship?
    A: Not just based on the p-value. Check: Was the test pre-registered? Is the effect size practically significant? Was multiple testing correction applied? What's the power?
  • Q: What's wrong with p-hacking?
    A: Running many tests or stopping when p < 0.05 inflates the true Type I error rate far above \(\alpha\). Use Bonferroni correction or sequential testing methods.
  • Q: Difference between one-tailed and two-tailed tests?
    A: One-tailed tests more power if direction is known a priori; two-tailed is safer and more conservative. One-tailed critical value at \(\alpha=0.05\) is \(z=1.645\) vs \(z=1.96\) for two-tailed.
The \(\alpha\)–\(\beta\) Trade-off

For a fixed sample size, \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) trade against each other: tighten \(\alpha\) (fewer false positives) and \(\beta\) grows (more false negatives). The only way to reduce both simultaneously is to increase \(n\).
