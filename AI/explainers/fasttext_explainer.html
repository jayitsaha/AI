1
Understanding FastText
▼

What is it? Word2Vec and GloVe give every word in the vocabulary one opaque vector, learned as if the word were a single indivisible symbol. FastText (Bojanowski, Grave, Joulin & Mikolov, Facebook AI, 2017) instead represents a word as a bag of its character n-grams — overlapping chunks of 3–6 characters — plus the whole word itself, and simply sums their vectors. The word's embedding is a byproduct of its spelling, not a lookup of a single row in a table.

Analogy: Think of a word like a smoothie made from fruit chunks (the n-grams). "Playing", "played", and "player" all share the chunks "play" and "pla" and "lay" — so their smoothies taste similar even before you've ever tasted "playing" specifically. If someone hands you a brand-new fruit combo you've never seen — say "replayable" — you can still blend it from familiar chunks and get a sensible-tasting result, instead of shrugging and saying "unknown fruit, no vector."

💡 If you remember one thing

A FastText word vector is the sum of the embeddings of its character n-grams (plus the whole-word token): $$v_w = \sum_{g \in G_w} \mathbf{z}_g$$ Because morphologically similar words share n-grams, FastText captures prefixes/suffixes automatically and can embed out-of-vocabulary (OOV) words that Word2Vec/GloVe simply cannot.

2
How FastText Works Internally
▼

The Problem With Atomic Word Vectors

Word2Vec's skip-gram model learns one \(d\)-dimensional vector per vocabulary word by predicting context words from a center word. It works beautifully for words seen often during training, but has two hard failure modes: (1) rare words get noisy, under-trained vectors, and (2) unseen words at inference time (typos, new slang, morphological variants like plurals or a language's rich inflections) have no vector at all — they're simply absent from the lookup table.

Step 1 — Add Boundary Symbols

FastText wraps each word with < and >, e.g. "playing" → <playing>. This lets the model distinguish a n-gram that occurs at a word boundary (like <pl, a likely prefix) from the same substring occurring in the middle of a longer word.

Step 2 — Extract All Character N-grams

Slide a window of length \(n\) (typically \(n \in \{3,4,5,6\}\)) across the padded word, collecting every substring. For "playing" with \(n=3\): <pl, pla, lay, ayi, yin, ing, ng>. The whole word <playing> is also added as its own special token, so the model can still memorize a word-specific signal when data is plentiful.

Step 3 — Hash Each N-gram Into a Fixed Bucket

Storing a unique vector per distinct n-gram would need tens of millions of rows. Instead FastText hashes each n-gram string into one of \(B\) buckets (typically \(B{=}2{,}000{,}000\)) with a cheap hash function (FNV-1a), and keeps one embedding row per bucket. Collisions are rare and empirically harmless — this is the same "hashing trick" used in large-scale linear models.

Step 4 — Sum to Get the Word Vector

The final representation is just elementwise vector addition of every n-gram's (bucket) embedding, plus the whole-word embedding if the word itself was frequent enough to get one:

$$v_w = \sum_{g \in G_w} \mathbf{z}_g$$

This sum is differentiable in every \(\mathbf{z}_g\), so standard backprop trains all n-gram vectors jointly with the skip-gram objective — no separate "morphology model" is needed.

🔑 Key Property

Any string — vocabulary word or not — decomposes into n-grams, and n-grams are shared across many words. So FastText can synthesize a plausible vector for any input string, including ones never seen in training, purely from pieces it has seen.

Design Decision: Why Sum, Not Concatenate or Average?

Summation keeps the output dimensionality fixed regardless of word length (concatenation wouldn't), and keeps gradients simple — each n-gram vector gets the exact same gradient signal as the full word during backprop (averaging would just rescale this uniformly, so sum vs. mean is mostly a matter of convention/learning-rate scaling).

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Skip-gram With Negative Sampling (SGNS), Recap

Standard skip-gram scores a center word \(w\) against a context word \(c\) with a plain dot product of two lookup vectors, then applies the logistic function:

$$p(c \mid w) = \sigma(u_w^\top v_c), \qquad \sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}$$

FastText's Modification: Replace the Word Vector With a Sum

FastText keeps the same skip-gram-with-negative-sampling objective, but swaps the atomic word vector \(u_w\) for the sum over its n-gram set \(G_w\):

$$s(w, c) = \Big(\sum_{g \in G_w} \mathbf{z}_g\Big)^{\top} v_c$$

The full negative-sampling loss for one (center, context) pair with \(K\) negative samples \(n_1,\dots,n_K\) drawn from a noise distribution is:

$$\mathcal{L} = -\log\sigma\big(s(w,c)\big) \;-\; \sum_{k=1}^{K} \log\sigma\big(-s(w,n_k)\big)$$

Gradient Flow Into Subword Vectors

Because \(s(w,c)\) is linear in the sum, the gradient with respect to any single n-gram embedding \(\mathbf{z}_g\) is identical to the gradient that would flow into a plain word vector, scaled by nothing extra:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{z}_g} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial s(w,c)} \cdot v_c \quad \text{for every } g \in G_w$$

Every n-gram that participates in a word gets the same update direction from that training example. Frequent, informative n-grams (like the suffix ing>) accumulate signal from thousands of different words and converge to stable, meaningful vectors — which is exactly why they generalize to unseen words.

⚠️ Common Misconception

FastText does not learn a separate vector for every word from scratch and then "add morphology on top." The whole-word vector is just one more summand in the same sum — for rare/unseen words the sum is dominated entirely by shared n-grams, which is what makes OOV inference possible.

When to use FastText: low-resource or morphologically rich languages, noisy user-generated text (typos, slang), search/autocomplete where new terms constantly appear, or when you need fast, lightweight, CPU-friendly static embeddings (e.g. as a baseline or a feature for classical ML).

When not to use it: tasks that need context-dependent meaning (e.g. "bank" the riverbank vs. "bank" the institution) — use a contextual transformer encoder instead. FastText still assigns one fixed vector per word regardless of sentence.

🎯 Decision Rule

Need speed, low resources, and robustness to unseen/rare words → FastText. Need deep contextual understanding and have compute to spare → Transformer embeddings. Need neither morphology nor OOV robustness and want the simplest baseline → Word2Vec/GloVe is still fine.

6
Key Takeaways
▼

FastText reframes a word embedding as an additive composition of character n-gram embeddings rather than an atomic lookup. This one change — sum instead of lookup — buys morphological generalization (shared prefixes/suffixes pull related words together) and true OOV robustness (any string can be decomposed, hashed, and summed) at almost no extra inference cost.

Related topics: Word2Vec / Skip-gram with Negative Sampling (the training objective FastText reuses), Byte-Pair Encoding & SentencePiece (the subword-tokenization lineage that transformers adopted next), the hashing trick in large-scale ML, and contextual embeddings (ELMo, BERT) as the next evolutionary step beyond static vectors.
