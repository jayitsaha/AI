Understanding GARCH
▼

What is it? GARCH stands for Generalized Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity. It's a statistical model used primarily in finance to forecast the volatility of financial time series (like stock returns, exchange rates, etc.). Unlike simpler models that assume constant volatility, GARCH models recognize that financial market volatility isn't fixed; it changes over time, often exhibiting "volatility clustering" where large price movements tend to be followed by large movements, and small movements by small ones.

Analogy: Imagine sailing on a sea. Sometimes the waters are calm, with small, predictable waves. Other times, a storm hits, bringing huge, erratic waves. After the storm, the sea doesn't instantly become calm again; the choppiness persists for a while. GARCH is like a sophisticated weather model that doesn't just predict if it will rain, but also how stormy the sea itself will be tomorrow, based on how stormy it was today and yesterday.

💡 If you remember one thing

GARCH models the variance of the error term (or residual) from a mean equation (e.g., an ARMA model) as an ARMA-like process itself. This means the current period's variance \(\sigma_t^2\) depends on past squared error terms \(\varepsilon_{t-i}^2\) (ARCH term) and past conditional variances \(\sigma_{t-j}^2\) (GARCH term). It's a model for the *predictability of unpredictability*.

2
How GARCH Works Internally
▼

The Two-Equation System

A GARCH model is typically specified as a two-equation system:

  1. Mean Equation: This describes the expected value of the time series \(r_t\) (e.g., asset returns). It's often an ARMA (Autoregressive Moving Average) model, but can be a simple constant mean or zero mean for returns: $$r_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t$$ where \(\varepsilon_t\) is the error term.
  2. Variance Equation: This is the GARCH part. It describes how the conditional variance of the error term, \(\sigma_t^2\), evolves over time. The key is that \(\varepsilon_t\) is assumed to be conditionally normally distributed with mean zero and time-varying variance \(\sigma_t^2\): $$\varepsilon_t = z_t \sigma_t \quad \text{where } z_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$$

The variance equation is where the magic happens. A GARCH(p,q) model states that the current conditional variance \(\sigma_t^2\) is a linear function of:

  • A constant term \(\omega\) (related to the long-run average variance).
  • Past squared error terms \(\varepsilon_{t-i}^2\) (the ARCH component, capturing the impact of past shocks).
  • Past conditional variances \(\sigma_{t-j}^2\) (the GARCH component, capturing the persistence of volatility).

GARCH vs. ARCH

The GARCH model is a generalization of the earlier ARCH (Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity) model proposed by Engle (1982). An ARCH(p) model only includes past squared error terms: \(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \sum_{i=1}^p \alpha_i \varepsilon_{t-i}^2\). GARCH(p,q) adds the autoregressive component for the variance itself, allowing for more parsimonious models (e.g., a GARCH(1,1) can often capture volatility dynamics that would require a much higher-order ARCH model) and better capturing the long memory of volatility.

Design Decision: Why Model Volatility Directly?

Why not just use robust standard errors or assume a fixed variance? Because in finance, volatility itself is a critical quantity. It's a measure of risk. Forecasting future volatility is essential for:

  • Risk Management: Calculating Value-at-Risk (VaR).
  • Option Pricing: Black-Scholes formula requires volatility.
  • Portfolio Optimization: Allocating assets based on risk-return profiles.
  • Trading Strategies: Volatility often correlates with trading opportunities.

GARCH provides a dynamic, data-driven way to estimate and predict this crucial risk measure.

🔑 Key Property

The core idea is that volatility is predictable from its own past behavior. Large (absolute) returns tend to be followed by large (absolute) returns, and small by small. GARCH formalizes this "volatility clustering" by making the current variance a function of past squared shocks and past estimated variances.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

The GARCH(p,q) Equation

The conditional variance equation for a GARCH(p,q) model is:

$$\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \sum_{i=1}^p \alpha_i \varepsilon_{t-i}^2 + \sum_{j=1}^q \beta_j \sigma_{t-j}^2$$

Where:

  • \(\sigma_t^2\) is the conditional variance at time \(t\).
  • \(\omega\) is a constant term (\(\omega > 0\)).
  • \(\varepsilon_{t-i}^2\) are past squared error terms (residuals from the mean equation). These capture the impact of past "shocks" to the system on current volatility. \(\alpha_i \ge 0\).
  • \(\sigma_{t-j}^2\) are past conditional variances. These capture the persistence of volatility, meaning that high (or low) volatility periods tend to be followed by high (or low) volatility periods. \(\beta_j \ge 0\).

The most common and often sufficient model is GARCH(1,1):

$$\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha_1 \varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta_1 \sigma_{t-1}^2$$

Here, \(\sigma_t^2\) depends on a constant, the previous period's squared error, and the previous period's conditional variance.

Stationarity and Long-Run Variance

For the GARCH process to be weakly stationary (meaning its unconditional variance is finite and constant over time), the sum of the ARCH and GARCH coefficients must be less than 1:

$$\sum_{i=1}^p \alpha_i + \sum_{j=1}^q \beta_j < 1$$

If this condition holds, the long-run (unconditional) variance, \(\sigma^2\), to which the conditional variance mean-reverts, can be calculated as:

\(\sigma^2 = \frac{\omega}{1 - (\sum \alpha_i + \sum \beta_j)}\)

If \(\sum \alpha_i + \sum \beta_j = 1\), the model is an Integrated GARCH (IGARCH), implying that shocks to volatility persist indefinitely. If the sum is greater than 1, the process is explosive and non-stationary.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

GARCH models assume that positive and negative shocks of the same magnitude have the same effect on future volatility. In financial markets, negative shocks (e.g., market crashes) often increase volatility more than positive shocks (e.g., market rallies) of equal size. This is known as the "leverage effect." Standard GARCH cannot capture this; variants like EGARCH or GJR-GARCH are needed.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use GARCH:

  • Financial Time Series: Stock returns, exchange rates, commodity prices, interest rates. Any series where volatility clustering is observed.
  • Risk Management: Calculating Value-at-Risk (VaR) or Expected Shortfall (ES) for portfolios.
  • Option Pricing: Providing dynamic volatility inputs for models like Black-Scholes.
  • Portfolio Optimization: Incorporating time-varying risk into asset allocation decisions.
  • Forecasting Volatility: For trading strategies or market analysis.

When NOT to use GARCH:

  • Data with Constant Volatility: If your time series exhibits homoskedasticity (constant variance), simpler models like ARIMA are sufficient and more parsimonious.
  • Non-Stationary Series: The basic GARCH model assumes stationarity of the variance. If \(\sum \alpha_i + \sum \beta_j \ge 1\), the model might be unstable or indicate an IGARCH process where shocks persist indefinitely.
  • Leverage Effect: If negative shocks have a stronger impact on volatility than positive shocks, standard GARCH is insufficient. Consider asymmetric GARCH variants (EGARCH, GJR-GARCH).
  • Long-Term Forecasting: GARCH models excel at short-to-medium term volatility forecasting. For very long horizons, the conditional variance tends to revert to the unconditional (long-run) variance, making the forecast less dynamic.
💡 Hierarchy

ARCH models (\(\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \sum \alpha_i \varepsilon_{t-i}^2\)) are a special case of GARCH models (when \(q=0\)). GARCH(1,1) is the most popular and often sufficient. There are many extensions: EGARCH (Exponential GARCH) for leverage effects, GJR-GARCH (Glosten, Jagannathan, Runkle GARCH) also for asymmetry, GARCH-in-Mean (GARCH-M) where volatility affects the mean return, and multivariate GARCH models for co-volatility.

Code Trace (GARCH(1,1) Simulation)
▼
import numpy as np
def simulate_garch11(T, omega, alpha, beta, initial_sigma2=None, seed=None):
if seed is not None:
np.random.seed(seed)
# Ensure stationarity conditions
if alpha + beta >= 1:
raise ValueError("alpha + beta must be < 1 for stationarity")
if omega <= 0 or alpha < 0 or beta < 0:
raise ValueError("omega > 0, alpha >= 0, beta >= 0")
# Initialize arrays for errors (returns) and conditional variances
eps = np.zeros(T)
sigma2 = np.zeros(T)
# Set initial conditional variance (often long-run variance)
if initial_sigma2 is None:
sigma2[0] = omega / (1 - alpha - beta) # Unconditional variance
else:
sigma2[0] = initial_sigma2
eps[0] = np.random.normal(0, np.sqrt(sigma2[0])) # First error term
# GARCH(1,1) recursion
for t in range(1, T):
# Variance equation: sigma_t^2 = omega + alpha * eps_{t-1}^2 + beta * sigma_{t-1}^2
sigma2[t] = omega + alpha * (eps[t-1]**2) + beta * sigma2[t-1]
# Error term: eps_t = z_t * sigma_t, where z_t ~ N(0,1)
eps[t] = np.random.normal(0, np.sqrt(sigma2[t]))
return eps, sigma2
