Understanding NDCG

▼

What it is: NDCG is a metric that measures how good a ranked list is, where positions near the top matter much more than positions near the bottom. It answers the question: "How close is our system's ranking to the theoretically perfect ranking?"

The Google Analogy. Imagine search engine results — a highly relevant result at position 1 is far more valuable to the user than an equally relevant result buried at position 10. Users rarely scroll past the first few results. NDCG quantifies exactly this intuition: it rewards systems that surface the most relevant items first, and penalizes systems that bury them.

One-sentence definition: NDCG normalizes DCG (Discounted Cumulative Gain) by the best possible ranking's DCG (IDCG — Ideal DCG), yielding a score in \([0, 1]\) where 1.0 is a perfect ranking.

Key insight about the logarithm: The discount \(\frac{1}{\log_2(i+1)}\) is not arbitrary. At position 1, the discount is exactly 1.0 (no penalty). At positions 2 and 3, the discounts are ≈ 0.631 and 0.5 respectively. This means that position 1 is worth more than positions 2 and 3 combined. By position 6, the discount has already halved from position 1. This steep decay mirrors how user attention behaves in real ranking scenarios.

Why not just use Precision@k? Precision@k treats all relevant documents identically — a somewhat relevant document counts the same as a highly relevant one. NDCG uses graded relevance: a document scored 3 (highly relevant) contributes exponentially more than one scored 1 (somewhat relevant). This makes NDCG far more sensitive and realistic for systems where relevance comes in degrees.

How It Works — Step by Step

▼

Computing NDCG for a ranked list involves four clear phases:

  1. 1 Assign relevance scores to each document in your ranked list. Typical grading scale: 0 = completely irrelevant, 1 = marginally relevant, 2 = moderately relevant, 3 = highly relevant. The scale can be any non-negative integers, though scales of 0–3 or 0–4 are most common in information retrieval benchmarks like TREC.
  2. 2 Compute DCG@k (Discounted Cumulative Gain at rank k). For each position \(i\) from 1 to k, compute the gain \(2^{rel_i} - 1\), then divide by the positional discount \(\log_2(i+1)\). Sum all these values. The exponential gain formula amplifies the difference between high and low relevance scores.
  3. 3 Compute IDCG@k (Ideal DCG). Sort all documents in your pool by relevance in descending order, then apply the exact same DCG formula to this re-sorted list. This is the maximum achievable DCG — the score your system would get if it were perfect.
  4. 4 Divide: NDCG = DCG / IDCG. Since DCG is always ≤ IDCG by construction, NDCG always lies in \([0, 1]\). A score of 1.0 means your ranking is perfect. A score near 0 means relevant documents are ranked at the very bottom. If IDCG = 0 (no relevant documents exist), NDCG is undefined.
The @k notation: NDCG@k only considers the top k positions. This is important — NDCG@10 asks "how good are my top 10 results?" while NDCG@100 evaluates deeper into the list. For web search, NDCG@10 is usually the gold standard since most users never go past the first page.

The Math

▼

DCG (Exponential gain variant — standard in ML competitions and search):

$$\text{DCG@}k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{2^{rel_i} - 1}{\log_2(i+1)}$$

DCG (Original formulation — Järvelin & Kekäläinen 2002):

$$\text{DCG@}k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{rel_i}{\log_2(i+1)}$$

The exponential gain variant (\(2^{rel_i} - 1\)) is more commonly used today because it creates a stronger penalty for placing highly relevant documents lower in the list, amplifying the differences between relevance grades. When \(rel_i \in \{0,1\}\) (binary relevance), both formulas become equivalent.

NDCG:

$$\text{NDCG@}k = \frac{\text{DCG@}k}{\text{IDCG@}k}$$

Position discount:

$$\text{discount}(i) = \frac{1}{\log_2(i+1)}$$

The discount at position \(i\) decays as \(\frac{1}{\log_2(i+1)}\): position 1 gets discount 1.0, position 2 gets ≈ 0.631, position 3 gets 0.5, position 4 gets ≈ 0.431, and so on. The denominator grows without bound — theoretically documents at very deep ranks contribute almost nothing to DCG, which is exactly the desired behavior.

Where IDCG comes from:

If \(\pi^*\) is the permutation that sorts documents by relevance in descending order, then:

$$\text{IDCG@}k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{2^{rel_{\pi^*(i)}} - 1}{\log_2(i+1)}$$

This is simply DCG applied to the best possible ranking. Because \(\pi^*\) places the highest-relevance documents at the lowest position indices (where the discount is largest), no other permutation can achieve a higher DCG.

Key Takeaways & Comparisons

▼

When to use NDCG:

  • Search engines evaluating result page quality
  • Recommender systems (movie/product/content recommendations)
  • Any system that outputs a ranked list where relevance comes in degrees
  • Learning-to-Rank model evaluation (e.g., LambdaRank, LambdaMART directly optimize NDCG)

NDCG vs other ranking metrics:

Metric Relevance Type Position Aware? Key Limitation
NDCG Graded (0, 1, 2, 3, ...) Yes — log discount Requires relevance judgments
Precision@k Binary No Treats all relevant docs equally; ignores relevance grade
MAP (Mean Average Precision) Binary Yes — reward early recall Cannot handle graded relevance; binary only
MRR (Mean Reciprocal Rank) Binary Yes — only first hit Only cares about the single first relevant item; ignores the rest of the list
ERR (Expected Reciprocal Rank) Graded Yes — cascade model More complex; models user stopping probability; less widely adopted
Critical Gotcha — NDCG@k undefined when IDCG@k = 0: If there are no relevant documents in the top k of the ideal ranking (i.e., the entire document pool has zero relevant items), then IDCG@k = 0 and NDCG@k is undefined (division by zero). In practice, queries with no relevant documents are either filtered out before evaluation or assigned NDCG = 0 by convention.
Practical tip — averaging across queries: NDCG is almost always reported as the mean over many queries (Mean NDCG). A single-query NDCG of 0.95 is great, but the important number is the average across thousands of queries, which smooths out easy/hard query variation.
