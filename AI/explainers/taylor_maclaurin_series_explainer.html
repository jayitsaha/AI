Taylor & Maclaurin Series

Approximating smooth functions as infinite polynomials — and bounding the error
Intermediate Calculus & Analysis Math Foundations Remainder: O(h^{n+1})
1
Understanding Taylor & Maclaurin Series
▼

What is it? Any smooth function — like \(e^x\), \(\sin x\), or \(\ln(1+x)\) — can be perfectly described near a point using an infinite sum of simple polynomial terms. A Taylor series centered at point \(a\) writes \(f(x)\) as:

$$f(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^n = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) + \frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^2 + \cdots$$

A Maclaurin series is simply the special case where \(a = 0\): we expand around the origin. This gives the iconic formulas:

$$e^x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^n}{n!}, \quad \sin x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n x^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}, \quad \cos x = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^n x^{2n}}{(2n)!}$$

Analogy: Think of a Taylor series like a GPS that gives you directions one step at a time. The first term tells you where you are (the function's value). The second term tells you which way you're heading (slope). The third tells you how fast you're turning (curvature). Each extra term refines the approximation further. Truncating after \(N\) terms gives a polynomial approximation — useful, fast, and with a provable error bound called the remainder term.

💡 If you remember one thing

A Taylor polynomial of degree \(N\) matches \(f\) and its first \(N\) derivatives at \(x = a\). The Lagrange remainder \(R_N(x) = \frac{f^{(N+1)}(\xi)}{(N+1)!}(x-a)^{N+1}\) for some \(\xi\) between \(a\) and \(x\) gives an exact, provable bound on the error.

2
How It Works Internally
▼

The Construction Principle

The key idea is matching derivatives. We want a polynomial \(P_N(x)\) such that at \(x = a\):

$$P_N^{(k)}(a) = f^{(k)}(a) \quad \text{for } k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, N$$

If \(P_N(x) = c_0 + c_1(x-a) + c_2(x-a)^2 + \cdots + c_N(x-a)^N\), differentiating \(k\) times and evaluating at \(a\) gives \(P_N^{(k)}(a) = k!\, c_k\). Setting this equal to \(f^{(k)}(a)\) uniquely determines \(c_k = f^{(k)}(a)/k!\). This is the only polynomial of degree \(\leq N\) that matches all \(N+1\) derivatives at \(a\) — a unique construction.

Three Classic Maclaurin Series (Numerically Verified)

For \(e^x\): all derivatives of \(e^x\) at 0 are 1, so every coefficient is \(1/n!\). At \(x=1\): \(1 + 1 + 1/2 + 1/6 + 1/24 + \cdots \to e \approx 2.71828\).

For \(\sin x\): odd-degree terms only, with alternating signs: \(x - x^3/6 + x^5/120 - \cdots\). At \(x=\pi/4\): the 5-term sum is \(0.70711\), matching \(\sin(\pi/4) = 1/\sqrt{2}\) to 5 decimal places.

For \(\cos x\): even-degree terms, alternating signs: \(1 - x^2/2 + x^4/24 - \cdots\). At \(x=0\), the first term alone is exact.

🔑 Key Property

The Taylor series of \(f\) converges to \(f(x)\) on the interval of convergence. For \(e^x\), \(\sin x\), \(\cos x\) this is all of \(\mathbb{R}\). For \(\ln(1+x)\) it is \((-1, 1]\). Outside this interval, partial sums diverge no matter how many terms you add.

The Lagrange Remainder — Exact Error Bound

Truncating after \(N\) terms leaves an error. Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange form of the remainder says: for every \(x\) there exists some \(\xi\) strictly between \(a\) and \(x\) such that:

$$R_N(x) = f(x) - P_N(x) = \frac{f^{(N+1)}(\xi)}{(N+1)!}(x-a)^{N+1}$$

We can't know \(\xi\) exactly, but if \(\left|f^{(N+1)}\right| \leq M\) on \([a,x]\), then \(|R_N(x)| \leq \frac{M}{(N+1)!}|x-a|^{N+1}\). For \(e^x\) near 0 with \(x \in [-1,1]\), \(M = e\) and the bound shrinks factorially fast — each new term buys you roughly one more decimal digit of accuracy.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

The Lagrange remainder gives a bound on the maximum possible error, not the actual error. The actual error \(|R_N(x)|\) is usually much smaller. Also, the remainder bound grows with \(|x - a|\) — far from the center, you need many more terms to achieve the same accuracy.

3
Why It Works — The Full Derivation
▼

Derivation via Repeated Integration by Parts

Start from the fundamental theorem of calculus: \(f(x) = f(a) + \int_a^x f'(t)\,dt\). Apply integration by parts to the integral, with \(u = f'(t)\) and \(dv = dt\), writing \(v = -(x-t)\) (choosing the antiderivative to vanish at \(t=x\)):

$$f(x) = f(a) + f'(a)(x-a) + \int_a^x f''(t)(x-t)\,dt$$

Repeat: integrate by parts on the remaining integral, each time gaining one more derivative term:

$$f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{N} \frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k + \underbrace{\frac{1}{N!}\int_a^x f^{(N+1)}(t)(x-t)^N\,dt}_{R_N(x) \text{ — Integral form}}$$

This is the integral form of the remainder. Applying the mean value theorem for integrals to \(R_N(x)\) (since \((x-t)^N\) does not change sign on \([a,x]\)) yields the Lagrange form:

$$R_N(x) = \frac{f^{(N+1)}(\xi)}{(N+1)!}(x-a)^{N+1} \quad \text{for some } \xi \in (a, x)$$

Convergence: Why Does the Series Equal the Function?

The Taylor series converges to \(f(x)\) if and only if \(R_N(x) \to 0\) as \(N \to \infty\). For \(e^x\): \(\left|f^{(N+1)}(\xi)\right| = e^{|\xi|} \leq e^{|x|}\) (a constant for fixed \(x\)), and \(\frac{e^{|x|}}{(N+1)!}|x|^{N+1} \to 0\) by the ratio test. So the Maclaurin series of \(e^x\) converges to \(e^x\) for all \(x \in \mathbb{R}\).

∑ Key Formulas

Taylor polynomial of degree \(N\) at \(a\): \(P_N(x) = \displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{N}\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k\)

Lagrange remainder: \(R_N(x) = \dfrac{f^{(N+1)}(\xi)}{(N+1)!}(x-a)^{N+1}\), \(\xi \in (a,x)\)

Error bound: \(\displaystyle|R_N(x)| \leq \frac{M \cdot |x-a|^{N+1}}{(N+1)!}\) where \(M = \max_{t\in[a,x]}|f^{(N+1)}(t)|\)

Key Takeaways & Applications in ML
▼

When Taylor Series Appear in ML

Gradient descent theory: Second-order Taylor expansion of the loss \(\mathcal{L}(\theta)\) around current parameters \(\theta_0\) gives \(\mathcal{L}(\theta) \approx \mathcal{L}(\theta_0) + \nabla\mathcal{L}^\top\delta\theta + \frac{1}{2}\delta\theta^\top H\,\delta\theta\), where \(H\) is the Hessian. Newton's method solves the quadratic approximation exactly.

Attention & softmax analysis: The softmax function's behavior for small logit differences is often analyzed via its Taylor expansion.

Numerical integration: Many numerical methods (Euler, Runge-Kutta) are derived by truncating the Taylor series of the solution to an ODE after 1, 2, or 4 terms respectively.

Activation functions: GELU is approximated as \(0.5x(1 + \tanh(\sqrt{2/\pi}(x + 0.044715x^3)))\), where the \(\tanh\) itself uses its Maclaurin series.

When to Use Which Expansion

Situation Recommendation
Expanding near \(x = 0\) Maclaurin series (simpler coefficients)
Expanding near \(x = a \neq 0\) Taylor series at \(a\) (better local accuracy)
Need error bound Compute Lagrange remainder with known \(|f^{(N+1)}|\) bound
\(x\) far from center Choose a closer center; more terms needed otherwise

Code — From Scratch Implementation
▼

The code below computes the Taylor polynomial and Lagrange remainder bound for \(e^x\), \(\sin x\), and \(\cos x\).

import math
def taylor_exp(x, N):
"""Maclaurin series of e^x: sum x^n / n! for n=0..N"""
total, term = 0.0, 1.0 # term_0 = x^0/0! = 1
for n in range(1, N + 1):
total += term # accumulate n-1 term
term *= x / n # term_n = term_{n-1} * x/n
total += term
return total
def lagrange_bound_exp(x, N):
"""Upper bound on |R_N(x)| for e^x on [0, x]."""
# |f^{(N+1)}(ξ)| = e^ξ ≤ e^x (M = e^x)
M = math.exp(abs(x))
return M * abs(x) ** (N + 1) / math.factorial(N + 1)
def taylor_sin(x, N_terms):
"""Maclaurin series of sin x: sum (-1)^n x^{2n+1}/(2n+1)!"""
total, term = 0.0, x # first term: x^1/1!
for n in range(1, N_terms):
total += term
term *= -x*x / ((2*n)*(2*n+1)) # recurrence
total += term
return total
# Quick verification:
print(taylor_exp(1.0, 10)) # → 2.7182818011...
print(lagrange_bound_exp(1.0, 10))# → 7.49e-08 (error bound)
print(taylor_sin(math.pi/4, 5)) # → 0.70710678...

The recurrence \(\text{term}_n = \text{term}_{n-1} \times x/n\) avoids recomputing factorials from scratch — each new term is O(1) to compute from the previous one.
