1
Understanding GNN-Based Recommendation
▼

What is it? Classical collaborative filtering (matrix factorization) gives every user and every item a flat learned vector and trains them to reconstruct the interactions you've already observed. It treats each interaction as an independent (user, item) pair — it never explicitly asks "who else likes what this user likes, and what do those people like?" Graph Neural Network (GNN) recommenders — NGCF, LightGCN, PinSage — fix this by treating the interaction log as a literal graph: users and items are nodes, an edge exists for every click/purchase/save, and embeddings are refined by message passing — repeatedly averaging each node's embedding with its neighbors' embeddings.

Analogy: word-of-mouth taste refinement. Your "taste vector" isn't just about what you clicked — it gets pulled toward the taste of everyone who liked the same items as you. And their vectors get pulled toward the tastes of people who liked what they liked. After a few rounds of this gossip, your embedding encodes multi-hop collaborative signal — "friends of friends" — not just your own direct history.

💡 If you remember one thing

Stack \(K\) rounds of neighbor-averaging on the bipartite user–item graph, combine the embeddings from every round, then rank items for a user by the dot product of their final embeddings: $$\hat{y}_{ui} = e_u^{\top} e_i, \qquad e_u = \operatorname{combine}\big(e_u^{(0)}, e_u^{(1)}, \dots, e_u^{(K)}\big)$$ LightGCN, NGCF and PinSage are three different answers to "exactly how should each round of averaging work?"

How the Three Models Work Internally
▼

NGCF (2019) — the "full" graph convolution

Neural Graph Collaborative Filtering explicitly models two things at every propagation layer: (1) a learnable linear transform \(W_1\) applied to neighbor embeddings, and (2) a bi-interaction term \(W_2(e_i \odot e_u)\) — an element-wise product between the neighbor and the ego node, capturing "how similar are our tastes on each latent dimension" — followed by a LeakyReLU nonlinearity. It is essentially a GCN with an extra interaction-aware term borrowed from factorization-machine intuition.

LightGCN (2020) — strip it down to what matters

The LightGCN authors ran ablations and found that NGCF's feature transforms and nonlinearities barely help — and sometimes hurt — pure collaborative filtering, while adding parameters and overfitting risk. LightGCN's layer is just a symmetric-normalized weighted sum of neighbor embeddings: no \(W\), no activation, no self-loop, no bias. The final embedding for each node is the (weighted) mean of its embeddings across all \(K\) layers, which behaves like a residual/skip connection and prevents over-smoothing as layers stack.

PinSage (2018) — the same idea at Pinterest's billion-node scale

PinSage cannot materialize the full graph or do exact neighbor aggregation — a popular pin can have millions of neighbors. Instead, for every node it runs short random walks starting from that node and counts how often each candidate neighbor is visited. Neighbors are aggregated with importance pooling — a weighted sum where the weight is each neighbor's normalized visit count — and only the top-\(K\) most-visited neighbors are kept per node. It trains with a max-margin ranking loss on (query, positive, hard-negative) triples mined from the same random walks, and runs inference as an offline MapReduce-style batch job.

🔑 Key Property

All three are instances of message passing: \(e^{(k+1)}_v = \text{AGG}\big(\{e^{(k)}_u : u \in \mathcal{N}(v)\}\big)\). They differ only in the aggregation function \(\text{AGG}\), whether neighbors are transformed/weighted by learnable matrices, and whether the full neighborhood or a sampled subset is used.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

LightGCN propagation rule

For user \(u\) and item \(i\), with \(\mathcal{N}(u)\) the items \(u\) interacted with and \(\mathcal{N}(i)\) the users who interacted with \(i\):

$$e_u^{(k+1)} = \sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}(u)} \frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{N}(u)|\,|\mathcal{N}(i)|}}\, e_i^{(k)}, \qquad e_i^{(k+1)} = \sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{N}(i)|\,|\mathcal{N}(u)|}}\, e_u^{(k)}$$

The symmetric normalization \(1/\sqrt{\deg(u)\deg(i)}\) is exactly the one used in the original GCN (Kipf & Welling) — it prevents high-degree "hub" nodes from dominating. After \(K\) layers, the final representation is the layer-combination:

$$e_u = \sum_{k=0}^{K} \alpha_k\, e_u^{(k)}, \quad \text{typically } \alpha_k = \frac{1}{K+1} \text{ (simple mean)}$$

NGCF propagation rule

$$e_u^{(k+1)} = \text{LeakyReLU}\!\left(\sum_{i \in \mathcal{N}(u)} \frac{1}{\sqrt{|\mathcal{N}(u)||\mathcal{N}(i)|}} \Big(W_1 e_i^{(k)} + W_2\big(e_i^{(k)} \odot e_u^{(k)}\big)\Big)\right)$$

Setting \(W_1 = I\), \(W_2 = 0\), and removing the nonlinearity recovers exactly the LightGCN rule — LightGCN is a strict simplification of NGCF, not a different family.

PinSage importance pooling

$$e_u^{(k+1)} = \sigma\!\left(W \cdot \text{CONCAT}\Big(e_u^{(k)},\; \gamma\big(\{e_i^{(k)} \cdot \pi(u,i) : i \in \mathcal{N}_S(u)\}\big)\Big)\right)$$

where \(\mathcal{N}_S(u)\) is the sampled top-\(K\) neighborhood, \(\pi(u,i)\) is the normalized visit count from random walks started at \(u\) (higher = more topically related), and \(\gamma\) is a weighted aggregator (weighted mean/sum). Training uses a max-margin loss over mined triples \((u, i^+, i^-)\): \(\mathcal{L} = \max(0,\; e_u^\top e_{i^-} - e_u^\top e_{i^+} + \Delta)\).

⚠️ Common Pitfall

Stacking too many layers over-smooths the graph — after enough hops, every node's embedding converges toward the same graph-wide average and items become indistinguishable. In practice \(K=2\text{–}4\) layers is typical; the layer-combination step (mean over 0..K) is precisely what protects LightGCN from this collapse, since layer 0 (the un-smoothed embedding) is always kept in the mix.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to reach for which model:

  • LightGCN — default choice for pure collaborative-filtering benchmarks; fewer parameters, faster to train, usually matches or beats NGCF.
  • NGCF — when you want the model to also learn nonlinear feature interactions from side information baked into the initial embeddings, and can afford the extra parameters/tuning.
  • PinSage — when the graph has hundreds of millions to billions of nodes/edges and full-neighborhood aggregation is computationally impossible; the random-walk sampling + importance pooling + MapReduce inference pipeline is built for that scale.

Shared caveats:

  • Over-smoothing: too many propagation layers make all embeddings converge — always keep the layer-combination / residual step.
  • Cold start remains hard: a user or item with zero edges has no neighbors to aggregate from, so these graphs still need side-feature fallbacks.
  • The graph must be rebuilt or incrementally updated as new interactions arrive — full-graph propagation is not naturally streaming.
💡 Hierarchy

LightGCN (no \(W\), no \(\sigma\)) \(\subset\) NGCF (\(W_1, W_2, \sigma\), full neighborhood) — and PinSage is the same message-passing idea as NGCF/LightGCN, re-engineered with random-walk sampling and importance pooling so it survives web-scale graphs.

import torch
# --- LightGCN: light graph convolution, no W, no activation ---
def lightgcn_layer(E, adj_norm):
# E: (num_nodes, dim) embedding table
# adj_norm: symmetric-normalized bipartite adjacency
return adj_norm @ E
def lightgcn_forward(E0, adj_norm, K=2):
layers = [E0]
for k in range(K):
layers.append(lightgcn_layer(layers[-1], adj_norm))
return torch.stack(layers, dim=0).mean(dim=0) # layer combination
# --- NGCF adds a learnable transform + bi-interaction ---
def ngcf_layer(E, adj_norm, W1, W2):
agg = adj_norm @ E
bi = adj_norm @ (E * E) # elementwise interaction
return leaky_relu(agg @ W1 + bi @ W2)
# --- PinSage: importance-pooled sampled neighborhoods ---
def pinsage_layer(node, sampled_neighbors, visit_weights, E, W):
agg = sum(w * E[n] for n, w in zip(sampled_neighbors, visit_weights))
return relu(W @ torch.cat([E[node], agg]))
# --- Training: BPR pairwise ranking loss ---
def bpr_loss(e_u, e_pos, e_neg):
return -torch.log(torch.sigmoid((e_u * e_pos).sum() - (e_u * e_neg).sum()))
