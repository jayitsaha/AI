Understanding Time Series Decomposition
▼

What is it? Time series decomposition is a statistical method that breaks down a time series into several component parts, each representing an underlying pattern category. The primary goal is to understand the individual drivers of variation within the data, which often include:

  • Trend (\(T_t\)): The long-term progression or direction of the series (e.g., increasing sales over years).
  • Seasonality (\(S_t\)): Regular, predictable fluctuations that recur over a fixed period (e.g., higher ice cream sales in summer, holiday shopping spikes).
  • Residual (\(R_t\)): The irregular, unpredictable component left after accounting for trend and seasonality (e.g., random noise, unexpected events).

Analogy: Imagine a complex song. The melody is the overarching trend, slowly evolving. The chorus is the seasonality, repeating predictably every few verses. The improvisation or unexpected flourishes are the residuals, adding unpredictable variation. Decomposition is like separating these elements to understand each one's contribution to the whole song.

💡 If you remember one thing

The choice between additive and multiplicative decomposition depends on how the seasonal component behaves: if the amplitude of seasonality is roughly constant regardless of the trend level, use additive. If the amplitude increases (or decreases) proportionally with the trend, use multiplicative.

2
How Decomposition Methods Work Internally
▼

Classical Decomposition (Moving Averages)

The most straightforward approach involves a sequence of smoothing operations:

  1. Estimate Trend: A centered moving average (CMA) is applied to the raw data. This smooths out both seasonal and irregular components, leaving primarily the trend.
  2. Detrend the Series: The estimated trend is subtracted (additive) or divided (multiplicative) from the original series, leaving the seasonal and residual components combined.
  3. Estimate Seasonality: For each period (e.g., each month in a year), the average of the detrended values is calculated. These averages are then normalized (to sum to 0 for additive, or average to 1 for multiplicative) to form the seasonal component.
  4. Estimate Residuals: The estimated trend and seasonal components are removed from the original series, leaving the residuals.

STL Decomposition (Seasonal-Trend decomposition using Loess)

STL is a more modern and robust decomposition method. Instead of simple moving averages, it uses Loess (Locally Estimated Scatterplot Smoothing), a non-parametric regression technique. Its key advantages include:

  • Robustness to Outliers: Loess is less affected by extreme values than moving averages.
  • Handles Varying Seasonality: The seasonal component can change over time, which classical methods struggle with.
  • User-Controlled Smoothing: Parameters allow fine-tuning the smoothness of both trend and seasonal components.
  • Iterative Process: STL repeatedly refines the trend and seasonal components in an inner and outer loop, making it more accurate.

Design Decision: Why STL is generally preferred

While classical decomposition is intuitive and easy to implement, its reliance on fixed-window moving averages makes it sensitive to outliers and unable to capture evolving seasonal patterns. STL, by contrast, offers greater flexibility and robustness, making it the go-to choice for many real-world applications where data might be noisy or seasonality isn't perfectly static. The main drawback is its computational complexity and more parameters to tune.

🔑 Key Property

The core of STL is Loess smoothing, applied iteratively. This local regression approach allows STL to produce smoother components and handle series where the seasonal pattern's shape or amplitude might evolve gradually over time, a common occurrence in many datasets.

3
Why It Works — The Mathematical Models
▼

Additive Decomposition Model

In an additive model, the components are summed together. This model is appropriate when the magnitude of the seasonal fluctuations does not depend on the level of the time series. For example, if monthly sales vary by about \(\pm \$100\) regardless of whether total sales are \$1,000 or \$10,000.

$$Y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t$$

Where:

  • \(Y_t\) is the observed value of the time series at time \(t\).
  • \(T_t\) is the trend component at time \(t\).
  • \(S_t\) is the seasonal component at time \(t\).
  • \(R_t\) is the residual (or irregular) component at time \(t\).

For an additive model, the seasonal component \(S_t\) typically sums to zero over one full seasonal period.

Multiplicative Decomposition Model

In a multiplicative model, the components are multiplied together. This model is suitable when the magnitude of the seasonal fluctuations increases or decreases proportionally with the level of the time series. For example, if monthly sales vary by \(\pm 10\%\) of the total sales, so the absolute variation is larger when total sales are higher.

$$Y_t = T_t \times S_t \times R_t$$

Where \(T_t\), \(S_t\), and \(R_t\) have the same interpretations as above. For a multiplicative model, the seasonal component \(S_t\) typically averages to one over one full seasonal period.

To apply methods like moving averages (which are additive operations) to a multiplicative series, it is common to first take the logarithm of the series:

$$\log(Y_t) = \log(T_t) + \log(S_t) + \log(R_t)$$

This transforms the multiplicative relationship into an additive one, allowing standard additive decomposition techniques to be applied. The components are then exponentiated back to their original scale.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

Both additive and multiplicative models assume a single, fixed seasonal period. If your data exhibits multiple seasonalities (e.g., daily and weekly patterns in the same series), or if the relationship between components is more complex and non-linear, these simple decomposition models may not be sufficient. More advanced methods like Prophet or state-space models might be necessary.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use Additive vs. Multiplicative:

  • Additive: Choose when the seasonal fluctuations remain roughly constant in magnitude, regardless of the overall level of the time series. This is common for series with relatively stable or small values.
  • Multiplicative: Choose when the seasonal fluctuations vary in proportion to the level of the time series (e.g., larger trend means larger seasonal swings). This is typical for series with growth or decay, where absolute variations are not constant.

When to use STL vs. Classical:

  • STL: Generally preferred for its robustness to outliers and ability to handle evolving seasonal patterns. If your data is noisy or seasonality isn't perfectly static, STL provides a more reliable decomposition.
  • Classical: Useful for quick, initial inspections or when the data is very clean and seasonality is perfectly fixed. It's simpler to implement and understand but less flexible.

When NOT to use simple decomposition:

  • When a time series exhibits multiple seasonalities (e.g., daily, weekly, and yearly patterns simultaneously). These methods are designed for a single dominant seasonal period.
  • When the relationship between components is highly non-linear or interactive in complex ways not captured by simple addition or multiplication.
  • When you have very short time series, as accurate estimation of trend and seasonality requires sufficient data points per period and across multiple periods.
💡 Decomposition as a Prerequisite

Decomposition is often a crucial first step in time series analysis and forecasting. By isolating components, you can forecast each part separately (e.g., use a regression for the trend, a seasonal average for seasonality, and an ARIMA model for residuals), then recombine them. It also helps in identifying structural changes or anomalies by analyzing the residual component.

Code Trace (Python - statsmodels)
▼
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import seasonal_decompose, STL
# 1. Generate sample data (e.g., monthly sales over 5 years)
np.random.seed(42)
n_points = 60 # 5 years * 12 months
time_idx = np.arange(n_points)
# Create a trend (linear)
trend = 100 + 2 * time_idx
# Create seasonality (sine wave for 12-month period)
seasonal_pattern = 20 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * time_idx / 12)
# Add some noise
noise = np.random.normal(0, 5, n_points)
# Combine for an additive series
y_additive = trend + seasonal_pattern + noise
ts_additive = pd.Series(y_additive, index=pd.to_datetime(pd.date_range(start='2020-01-01', periods=n_points, freq='MS')))
# 2. Classical Decomposition (Additive)
result_classical_add = seasonal_decompose(ts_additive, model='additive', period=12)
print("Classical Additive Decomposition:")
print(result_classical_add.trend.head())
print(result_classical_add.seasonal.head())
print(result_classical_add.resid.head())
# 3. STL Decomposition (Additive - default)
stl_decompose = STL(ts_additive, period=12, robust=True) # robust=True for outlier handling
result_stl_add = stl_decompose.fit()
print("\nSTL Additive Decomposition:")
print(result_stl_add.trend.head())
print(result_stl_add.seasonal.head())
print(result_stl_add.resid.head())
# 4. Multiplicative Decomposition (requires positive values)
y_multiplicative = trend * (1 + seasonal_pattern/100) * (1 + noise/100)
ts_multiplicative = pd.Series(y_multiplicative, index=ts_additive.index)
result_classical_mul = seasonal_decompose(ts_multiplicative, model='multiplicative', period=12)
print("\nClassical Multiplicative Decomposition:")
print(result_classical_mul.trend.head())
print(result_classical_mul.seasonal.head())
print(result_classical_mul.resid.head())
