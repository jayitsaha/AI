1
Understanding Sim-to-Real Transfer
▼

What is it? Training a robot policy directly on real hardware is slow, expensive, and can break the robot. So we train in simulation instead — millions of cheap, fast, parallel trials. The catch: no simulator perfectly matches reality. A policy that is great in sim can fail the moment it meets a real motor with unmodeled friction, a slightly heavier payload, or different lighting. That mismatch is the reality gap, and sim-to-real transfer is the collection of techniques that close it.

Analogy: Imagine training a pilot only ever in one exact flight simulator, tuned for calm, windless weather. The first time real turbulence hits, they panic — they never rehearsed it. Domain randomization is like training that pilot across thousands of randomized simulated conditions — gusty wind, rain, engine hiccups — so that whatever real weather shows up feels like "just another day in the simulator." System identification is the flight engineer instead measuring the real plane's actual aerodynamics and re-tuning the simulator to match it exactly.

💡 If you remember one thing

Domain randomization makes the real world look like just another random sample the policy already survived in training. System identification instead narrows the simulator toward the one real configuration. Used together, they are the two complementary levers for shrinking $$\text{Gap} = \big| J_{real}(\pi) - J_{sim}(\pi) \big|$$ the performance gap between simulated and real-world reward.

2
How It Works Internally
▼

The Naive Approach and Why It Fails

Train a policy \(\pi_\theta\) inside one fixed simulator \(M_{\xi_0}\) with a best-guess set of physics parameters \(\xi_0\) (mass, friction, motor damping, sensor noise). The policy over-fits to exactly those numbers. Deployed on the real robot — whose true parameters \(\xi_{real}\) almost certainly differ — the policy walks off a cliff it never had to handle before.

Domain Randomization (DR)

Instead of one simulator, sample physics parameters \(\xi \sim p(\xi)\) from a distribution on every training episode — randomize mass, friction, link lengths, motor strength, latency, even textures and lighting for vision policies. The policy is forced to find a strategy that is robust across the whole family of simulated worlds. If \(p(\xi)\) is wide enough to contain \(\xi_{real}\), the real robot is — from the policy's point of view — just one more training sample it has already seen.

System Identification (SysID)

Rather than guessing how wide to randomize, collect a handful of real-robot rollouts and estimate the real parameters directly: run an optimizer that adjusts simulated mass/friction until the simulator's predicted trajectory matches the observed real trajectory. This gives a calibrated simulator centered near \(\xi_{real}\), which can then be used on its own or to re-center a narrower, more efficient domain-randomization distribution (the "SimOpt" style loop).

🔑 Key Property

DR trades a little in-simulation performance for robustness across a distribution (bias toward caution). SysID trades exploration breadth for precision at one estimated point. They are complementary: identify roughly where reality sits, then randomize a tight, well-aimed cloud around it.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Domain Randomization as Distributionally Robust Optimization

Standard RL maximizes expected return in one MDP. DR instead maximizes expected return under a distribution of MDPs \(M_\xi\), each a simulator instantiated with sampled physics parameters \(\xi\):

$$\theta^{*} = \arg\max_{\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{\xi \sim p(\xi)}\Big[\, \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta, M_\xi}\big[R(\tau)\big] \Big]$$

If \(\xi_{real}\) lies within the support of \(p(\xi)\), the optimal \(\theta^*\) is — by construction — a policy that has already been optimized for something statistically indistinguishable from reality.

System Identification as Least-Squares Estimation

Given real transitions \((s_t, a_t, s_{t+1}^{real})\), find simulator parameters \(\hat\xi\) whose forward dynamics \(f_\xi\) best explain what was observed:

$$\hat\xi = \arg\min_{\xi} \sum_{t} \big\| f_\xi(s_t, a_t) - s_{t+1}^{real} \big\|^2$$

This is solved by gradient descent or CMA-ES on the simulator, treating the simulator itself as a differentiable-or-black-box function of \(\xi\). Each iteration nudges \(\xi\) toward lower reconstruction error against the real rollout.

The Combined Loop (SimOpt-style)

$$p_{k+1}(\xi) \;=\; \underset{p}{\operatorname{update}}\Big(p_k(\xi),\; \mathcal{D}_{real}\Big) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \pi_{k+1} = \arg\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{\xi\sim p_{k+1}}\big[\mathbb{E}_{M_\xi}[R]\big]$$

Real rollouts \(\mathcal{D}_{real}\) collected from the current policy are used to re-estimate and re-center \(p(\xi)\) (SysID), then a new policy is trained under the updated, tighter distribution (DR) — alternating until the simulator-to-real performance gap stops shrinking.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

Randomizing too aggressively without any identification is wasteful: the policy becomes over-conservative, "planning for every world" including physically implausible ones, and in-simulation performance can degrade badly. Randomizing too narrowly without identification risks entirely missing \(\xi_{real}\). The sweet spot is a distribution just wide enough to cover reality, informed by (even rough) system identification.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to lean on Domain Randomization:

  • You cannot easily collect real-robot data before deployment (e.g. sim-only pretraining)
  • The real parameters are expected to drift or vary across many robot units / environments
  • Vision-based policies, where randomizing textures, lighting and camera pose closes the visual reality gap

When to lean on System Identification:

  • You have access to a specific real robot and can collect a modest number of calibration rollouts
  • Physics parameters are fairly stable/known-unknown (e.g. one payload mass to measure, not thousands)
  • You want to narrow an overly-conservative DR distribution without losing real-world coverage
💡 Hierarchy

Fixed single sim (fast but brittle) → Wide domain randomization (robust but conservative) → SysID-calibrated, narrow DR (both robust and efficient). Modern robot learning pipelines (e.g. OpenAI's Dactyl, SimOpt, ADR) iterate the last stage in closed loop with real rollouts.
