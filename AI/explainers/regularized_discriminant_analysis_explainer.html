Regularized Discriminant Analysis (RDA)

Interpolating between LDA and QDA via covariance shrinkage — stabilizing estimates for small n or ill-conditioned Σ
Intermediate Supervised Learning Classification Two Regularization Knobs
1
Understanding RDA
▾

Imagine you are a doctor trying to distinguish three diseases from blood-test measurements. Two classical tools exist. QDA (Quadratic Discriminant Analysis) fits a separate covariance ellipse to each disease cluster — very flexible, but needs lots of data per class. LDA (Linear Discriminant Analysis) assumes all diseases share the same shape of cluster — very stable, but potentially wrong when shapes really differ.

RDA sits in between: it lets each class have its own ellipse, but nudges each class-specific ellipse toward the pooled "average" ellipse (controlled by \(\lambda\)), and then optionally nudges the result further toward a perfectly round sphere (controlled by \(\gamma\)). More data → trust the class ellipse. Less data → borrow strength from the other classes or from an isotropic prior.

If you remember one thing

RDA regularizes each class covariance as \(\hat{\Sigma}_k(\lambda,\gamma) = (1-\gamma)\bigl[(1-\lambda)\hat{\Sigma}_k + \lambda\hat{\Sigma}_{\text{pooled}}\bigr] + \gamma\,\frac{\text{tr}[\cdot]}{p}\,I\). Two knobs: \(\lambda\) blends toward LDA, \(\gamma\) blends toward a scaled identity (spherical). Both are tuned by cross-validation.

Key Concept — Why regularize?

When the number of features \(p\) approaches or exceeds the per-class sample count \(n_k\), the sample covariance \(\hat{\Sigma}_k\) is singular or near-singular. Its inverse (needed for the discriminant score) blows up. Shrinkage ensures \(\hat{\Sigma}_k(\lambda,\gamma)\) stays invertible and its inverse stays numerically sane.

2
How It Works Internally
▾

RDA follows the structure of QDA but replaces each \(\hat{\Sigma}_k\) with a shrunk estimate before computing discriminant scores.

Step 1 — Estimate class statistics

From \(n_k\) samples in class \(k\), compute the class mean \(\hat{\mu}_k\) and sample covariance \(\hat{\Sigma}_k\). Compute the pooled covariance:

$$\hat{\Sigma}_{\text{pooled}} = \frac{\sum_k (n_k - 1)\,\hat{\Sigma}_k}{\sum_k (n_k-1)}$$

Step 2 — Blend toward pooled (λ knob)

For each class \(k\):

$$\hat{\Sigma}_k^{(\lambda)} = (1-\lambda)\,\hat{\Sigma}_k + \lambda\,\hat{\Sigma}_{\text{pooled}}, \quad \lambda \in [0,1]$$

\(\lambda=0\): pure QDA (class-specific). \(\lambda=1\): pure LDA (pooled = same for all classes).

Step 3 — Blend toward scaled identity (γ knob)

Let \(s_k = \operatorname{tr}[\hat{\Sigma}_k^{(\lambda)}]/p\) be the average variance. Then:

$$\hat{\Sigma}_k(\lambda,\gamma) = (1-\gamma)\,\hat{\Sigma}_k^{(\lambda)} + \gamma\,s_k\,I_p, \quad \gamma \in [0,1]$$

\(\gamma=1\): fully spherical covariance (naïve Bayes with tied variance). This step removes off-diagonal correlations, trading bias for dramatically improved conditioning.

Step 4 — Compute discriminant scores

Classify \(x\) to the class \(k\) maximizing the log-posterior:

$$\delta_k(x) = -\frac{1}{2}\log|\hat{\Sigma}_k(\lambda,\gamma)| - \frac{1}{2}(x-\hat{\mu}_k)^\top [\hat{\Sigma}_k(\lambda,\gamma)]^{-1}(x-\hat{\mu}_k) + \log\hat{\pi}_k$$
Design Decision — Two separate knobs?

\(\lambda\) handles the between-class vs pooled tradeoff; \(\gamma\) handles off-diagonal vs diagonal tradeoff. They are nearly orthogonal regularization axes. In practice, a 2D grid search (or fast CV) over \((\lambda,\gamma)\) is tractable and outperforms a single parameter. Friedman (1989) showed the two-knob approach dominates ridge-only or Ledoit-Wolf-only regularization on many benchmarks.

Numerical Trace (2D, 2 classes)

Using the toy example below: Class 1 has an elongated covariance \(\hat{\Sigma}_1 = \begin{bmatrix}2.0 & 1.2 \\ 1.2 & 0.8\end{bmatrix}\) (ill-conditioned, cond≈47); Class 2 has \(\hat{\Sigma}_2 = \begin{bmatrix}0.5 & 0.1 \\ 0.1 & 0.6\end{bmatrix}\). The pooled estimate is \(\hat{\Sigma}_p = \begin{bmatrix}1.25 & 0.65 \\ 0.65 & 0.70\end{bmatrix}\).

At \(\lambda=0.5, \gamma=0.5\): \(\hat{\Sigma}_1^{(\lambda)} = \begin{bmatrix}1.625 & 0.925 \\ 0.925 & 0.750\end{bmatrix}\), then \(s_1 = (1.625+0.750)/2 = 1.188\), giving \(\hat{\Sigma}_1(0.5,0.5) = \begin{bmatrix}1.406 & 0.462 \\ 0.462 & 0.969\end{bmatrix}\), cond≈2.5 — a dramatic improvement from 47.

3
Why It Works — Math & Guarantees
▾

The unified RDA formula can be written as a convex combination in the space of positive-semidefinite matrices:

$$\hat{\Sigma}_k(\lambda,\gamma) = (1-\gamma)\bigl[(1-\lambda)\hat{\Sigma}_k + \lambda\hat{\Sigma}_{\text{pooled}}\bigr] + \gamma\,\frac{\operatorname{tr}[\hat{\Sigma}_k^{(\lambda)}]}{p}\,I_p$$

Since both \(\hat{\Sigma}_k\) and \(\hat{\Sigma}_{\text{pooled}}\) are positive semi-definite, and the trace-scaled identity \(\frac{\operatorname{tr}[\cdot]}{p}I\) is strictly positive definite, any \(\gamma > 0\) guarantees \(\hat{\Sigma}_k(\lambda,\gamma) \succ 0\) — always invertible.

Bias–Variance Decomposition

The expected misclassification error decomposes into bias (from wrong covariance assumptions) and variance (estimation noise). The optimal \((\lambda^*, \gamma^*)\) minimizes their sum. When \(n_k \gg p\), variance is small → prefer \(\lambda\) near 0, \(\gamma\) near 0 (trust the data). When \(n_k \approx p\), variance dominates → push toward larger \(\lambda\) and \(\gamma\).

Asymptotic Optimality (Friedman 1989)

Under Gaussian class-conditional densities, as \(n \to \infty\) with \(p/n \to 0\), RDA with \(\hat{\lambda} \to 0, \hat{\gamma} \to 0\) (CV-selected) converges to the Bayes-optimal QDA classifier. For finite \(n/p\), the regularized estimator has strictly lower expected loss than the unregularized MLE in the high-dimensional regime \(p = O(\sqrt{n})\).

Connection to Ledoit-Wolf Shrinkage

The \(\gamma\) shrinkage is closely related to oracle Ledoit-Wolf estimators: \(\hat{\Sigma}^{\text{LW}} = (1-\alpha)\hat{\Sigma} + \alpha\bar{\sigma}^2 I\). The difference is that RDA sets \(\alpha = \gamma\) as a free hyperparameter tuned by CV, while LW derives the analytic oracle. For classification, the CV approach is generally more accurate because the classification loss is not the same as Frobenius loss on \(\Sigma^{-1}\).

Common Misconception

"RDA is just regularized logistic regression." No — RDA is generative (models \(P(x|k)\) as Gaussian), while LR is discriminative (models \(P(k|x)\) directly). RDA's regularization acts on the covariance matrix, not on the weight vector. They can produce different boundaries even with the same regularization budget.

From-Scratch Implementation
▾

A minimal, numerically verified RDA classifier from scratch:

import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import solve
class RDA:
def __init__(self, lam=0.5, gamma=0.0):
self.lam, self.gamma = lam, gamma
def fit(self, X, y):
classes = np.unique(y)
n_total = len(y)
p = X.shape[1]
# Step 1: class means and covariances
self.means_ = {k: X[y==k].mean(0) for k in classes}
covs = {k: np.cov(X[y==k].T, ddof=1) for k in classes}
ns = {k: (y==k).sum() for k in classes}
# Step 2: pooled covariance
pooled = sum((ns[k]-1)*covs[k] for k in classes)
pooled /= (n_total - len(classes))
self.priors_ = {k: ns[k]/n_total for k in classes}
# Step 3: regularized covariances
self.regs_ = {}
for k in classes:
lam_blend = (1-self.lam)*covs[k] + self.lam*pooled
s_k = np.trace(lam_blend) / p
self.regs_[k] = (1-self.gamma)*lam_blend + self.gamma*s_k*np.eye(p)
self.classes_ = classes
def predict(self, X):
scores = []
for k in self.classes_:
S = self.regs_[k]
sign, logdet = np.linalg.slogdet(S)
diff = X - self.means_[k] # (n, p)
# Mahalanobis: sum((diff @ inv(S)) * diff, axis=1)
mah = (diff @ np.linalg.solve(S, diff.T)).diagonal()
delta = -0.5*logdet - 0.5*mah + np.log(self.priors_[k])
scores.append(delta)
return self.classes_[np.argmax(np.stack(scores, axis=1), axis=1)]
Hyperparameter Tuning

In production, run a 2D grid-search: for lam in np.linspace(0,1,11): for gam in np.linspace(0,1,11): cross_val_score(RDA(lam,gam), X, y, cv=5). The 11×11=121 fits are cheap for moderate p. sklearn's LinearDiscriminantAnalysis(solver='lsqr', shrinkage='auto') covers the \(\lambda=1\) line with Ledoit-Wolf \(\gamma\).

Key Takeaways
▾

When to use RDA: small-to-moderate \(n\), moderate \(p\), Gaussian class-conditional distributions are plausible, you suspect the class covariances differ but have too little data to reliably estimate them separately.

When NOT to use: very large \(n\) (just use QDA), highly non-Gaussian distributions (use SVM or tree ensembles), very high \(p\) with sparse signal (use penalized logistic regression).

Advantages

• Always invertible \(\hat{\Sigma}_k\) for \(\gamma>0\)
• Unified QDA–LDA–sphere spectrum
• Closed-form scores (no optimization)
• Interpretable geometry
• Fast cross-validation over \((\lambda,\gamma)\)

Disadvantages

• Assumes Gaussian conditionals
• 2D hyperparameter search needed
• Quadratic boundary (no kernel trick)
• Sensitive to outliers in cov. estimate
• Not directly applicable to non-metric spaces
