1
Understanding Loss Surface Visualization
▼

What is it? A neural network's loss is a function of millions of parameters — impossible to see directly. Loss surface visualization projects this high-dimensional landscape onto 1D or 2D slices so we can understand the geometry: where are the valleys? Are they wide and flat (good for generalization) or narrow and sharp (fragile to perturbation)?

Analogy: Imagine you're a blind hiker in a mountain range with a million peaks and valleys. You can only feel the ground directly beneath your feet. Loss surface visualization gives you a topographic map — but since you can't see all dimensions, you project onto two special directions (like "looking north" and "looking east") to get a 2D slice of the terrain. Filter normalization ensures your map scale is consistent no matter which trail you start from.

💡 If you remember one thing

Without filter normalization, two models with different weight scales look artificially sharper or flatter than they are. Li et al. (2018) showed that normalizing random directions to have the same filter-wise norm as the model's weights makes sharpness measurements scale-invariant and comparable across architectures and training runs.

Why it matters: Loss surface geometry explains why some optimizers generalize better, why batch size affects performance, and why certain architectures are easier to train. Flat minima generalize better (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber 1997); skip connections flatten the loss surface (Li et al. 2018).

2
Historical Context & Core Techniques
▼

The Problem: High-Dimensional Blindness

A ResNet-56 has ~850,000 parameters. Its loss surface lives in \(\mathbb{R}^{850000}\). Visualizing it directly is impossible. Early attempts (2014–2017) plotted loss along the training trajectory projected onto 2D via PCA, but a critical flaw emerged: the scale of perturbation directions depended on weight magnitudes, making surfaces from different models incomparable.

Key Paper: Li et al. 2018 — "Visualizing the Loss Landscape of Neural Nets"

Hao Li, Zhiyuan Xu, Gavin Taylor, Christoph Studer, and Tom Goldstein (NeurIPS 2018) introduced filter normalization, solving the scale problem. They showed that skip connections (ResNets) dramatically flatten the loss surface vs. plain networks — explaining ResNet's training stability.

🔑 Filter Normalization (Li et al. 2018)

For each filter \(\theta_i\) in the network and each random direction \(d_i\), rescale: $$\hat{d}_i \leftarrow \frac{d_i}{\|d_i\|} \cdot \|\theta_i\|$$ This ensures the perturbation direction has the same norm as the corresponding filter — making sharpness measurements scale-invariant and comparable across models.

Four Core Visualization Techniques

1. 1D Loss Projections: Fix a random direction \(\delta\), plot \(L(\theta^* + \alpha \delta)\) as \(\alpha\) varies. Reveals curvature along one slice.

2. 2D Loss Surface: Pick two random filter-normalized directions \(\delta, \eta\), plot \(L(\theta^* + \alpha\delta + \beta\eta)\) as a heatmap or contour. Reveals valley shape, sharpness, saddle points.

3. PCA of Optimization Trajectory: Collect weight checkpoints \(\{\theta_t\}\), center, run PCA. The first 2 PCs capture the dominant movement during training — shows where the optimizer actually traveled.

4. Checkpoint Interpolation: Linearly interpolate between two trained models: \(\theta(t) = (1-t)\theta_A + t\theta_B\), plot \(L(\theta(t))\) vs. \(t\). Reveals loss barriers between solutions — if the path is smooth and low, models may be in the same basin.

⚠️ Critical Caveat: Random Directions Miss Most of the Space

In high dimensions, two random vectors are nearly orthogonal (concentration of measure). A 2D slice captures a tiny, potentially unrepresentative sliver of the full loss landscape. PCA-based directions are more meaningful because they reflect actual training dynamics.

3
The Mathematics of Loss Projections
▼

Formal Setup

Let \(\theta^* \in \mathbb{R}^p\) be the trained parameters (e.g., \(p = 850{,}000\) for ResNet-56). Draw two random directions \(\delta, \eta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_p)\). Apply filter normalization to get \(\hat{\delta}, \hat{\eta}\). The 2D projected loss surface is:

$$\mathcal{L}(\alpha, \beta) = L\!\left(\theta^* + \alpha\,\hat{\delta} + \beta\,\hat{\eta}\right)$$

where \(\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}\) are the coordinates on the 2D slice.

Filter Normalization — Exact Formula

For a convolutional layer with filter tensor \(\theta \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{out} \times C_{in} \times k \times k}\), treat each output filter \(\theta_i \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{in} k^2}\) separately. For direction vector \(d_i\) corresponding to filter \(i\):

$$\hat{d}_i = \frac{d_i}{\|d_i\|_2} \cdot \|\theta_i\|_2, \qquad i = 1, \ldots, C_{out}$$

Without this normalization, a model with large weights has a "zoomed out" landscape — perturbation of magnitude 1 is tiny relative to weight scale, so the surface looks artificially flat.

Sharpness via the Hessian

The local curvature at \(\theta^*\) is captured by the Hessian \(H = \nabla^2_\theta L(\theta^*)\). The curvature along direction \(\hat{d}\) is the Rayleigh quotient:

$$\kappa(\hat{d}) = \hat{d}^\top H \hat{d}$$

The sharpness metric used in practice (Keskar et al. 2017) is the maximum loss increase within an \(\epsilon\)-ball around \(\theta^*\):

$$\phi(\theta^*, \epsilon) = \frac{\max_{\|\eta\|_2 \leq \epsilon} L(\theta^* + \eta) - L(\theta^*)}{1 + L(\theta^*)}$$

PCA Trajectory Directions

Collect \(T\) weight snapshots \(\Theta = [\theta_1 - \bar{\theta}, \ldots, \theta_T - \bar{\theta}] \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times T}\) where \(\bar{\theta} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_t \theta_t\). PCA gives \(\Theta \approx U \Sigma V^\top\). The first two principal components \(u_1, u_2 \in \mathbb{R}^p\) span the dominant optimization subspace.

Interpolation & Loss Barriers

For two minima \(\theta_A, \theta_B\), the linear interpolation loss is:

$$\mathcal{L}(t) = L\!\left((1-t)\,\theta_A + t\,\theta_B\right), \quad t \in [0, 1]$$

If \(\max_{t} \mathcal{L}(t)\) is large, there is a loss barrier between the two solutions — they are in different basins. This is related to the mode connectivity literature (Garipov et al. 2018): often a curved (not linear) path can connect two minima with no barrier.

📐 Why Flat Minima Generalize Better

The PAC-Bayes bound for flat minima: if \(\theta^*\) is in a region of radius \(r\) where \(L(\theta) \leq \epsilon\) for all \(\|\theta - \theta^*\|_2 \leq r\), then the generalization gap scales as \(\mathcal{O}(p/r^2)\). Larger \(r\) (flatter) → tighter generalization bound (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber 1997, Dziugaite & Roy 2017).

When to Use Each

  • Comparing sharpness across runs/architectures → 2D random with filter normalization (Li et al. 2018)
  • Understanding optimizer behavior → PCA of trajectory (shows where optimization actually goes)
  • Checking if two models are in the same basin → Checkpoint interpolation (look for loss barrier)
  • Quick sanity check of training dynamics → 1D slice along gradient direction
💡 Key Findings from Li et al. 2018

1. Skip connections (ResNet) → dramatically flatter loss surface vs. plain networks of same depth. 2. Sharpness increases with depth for plain networks but not ResNets. 3. Large-batch SGD converges to sharper minima than small-batch (Keskar et al. 2017) — visible as a narrower valley in the 2D surface. 4. The loss surface is not convex — there are multiple basins, saddle points, and flat plateaus.

Code — From Scratch (NumPy)
▼
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
# ── Filter normalization (Li et al. 2018) ──
def filter_normalize(direction, weights):
"""Scale each filter-slice of direction to match weights norm."""
# For FC layers, treat whole weight matrix as one filter
normed = direction / (np.linalg.norm(direction) + 1e-10)
return normed * np.linalg.norm(weights) # scale to match theta
# ── 2D Loss Surface ──
def compute_loss_surface(model, theta_star, loss_fn, n=40, alpha_range=1.0):
np.random.seed(42)
d1 = np.random.randn(*theta_star.shape)
d2 = np.random.randn(*theta_star.shape)
d1 = filter_normalize(d1, theta_star) # filter normalize!
d2 = filter_normalize(d2, theta_star) # critical step
grid = np.linspace(-alpha_range, alpha_range, n)
Z = np.zeros((n, n))
for i, b in enumerate(grid):
for j, a in enumerate(grid):
theta = theta_star + a*d1 + b*d2
Z[i, j] = loss_fn(model, theta) # n² forward passes!
return grid, Z, d1, d2
# ── PCA of Optimization Trajectory ──
def pca_trajectory(checkpoints): # list of theta arrays
Theta = np.stack(checkpoints) # shape (T, p)
mean = Theta.mean(axis=0)
centered = Theta - mean # center
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(centered, full_matrices=False)
d1, d2 = Vt[0], Vt[1] # top 2 principal directions
coords = centered @ np.stack([d1, d2]).T # project trajectory
return d1, d2, coords
# ── Checkpoint Interpolation ──
def interpolate_checkpoints(theta_A, theta_B, loss_fn, model, n=50):
ts = np.linspace(0, 1, n)
losses = []
for t in ts:
theta = (1 - t) * theta_A + t * theta_B # linear interp
losses.append(loss_fn(model, theta))
return ts, np.array(losses)
# ── Plot (matplotlib) ──
def plot_surface(grid, Z):
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 6))
ax.contourf(grid, grid, np.log(Z + 0.01), levels=40, cmap='RdYlGn_r')
ax.contour(grid, grid, np.log(Z + 0.01), levels=40, colors='k', alpha=0.3, linewidths=0.5)
ax.set_xlabel('α (direction d₁)'); ax.set_ylabel('β (direction d₂)')
ax.set_title('Loss Surface (filter-normalized directions)')
