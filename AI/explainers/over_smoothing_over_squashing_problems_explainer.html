The two fundamental problems limiting depth in Graph Neural Networks

Understanding Over-smoothing & Over-squashing
▼

What are they? Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) learn node representations by iteratively aggregating information from their neighbors. As GNNs go deeper (more layers), they can suffer from two distinct but related issues:

  • Over-smoothing: Node representations in a connected component become increasingly similar, eventually converging to a single, indistinguishable vector. This causes a loss of discriminative power, as all nodes look the same regardless of their original features or local structure.
  • Over-squashing: Information from distant nodes needs to be compressed into a fixed-size vector (the node's representation) as it propagates through a narrow "bottleneck" (a single node's message passing path). This bottleneck can "squash" or lose crucial information from large receptive fields, making it hard for distant nodes to influence each other.

Analogies:

  • Over-smoothing: Imagine repeatedly mixing paints. As you add more and more layers of mixing, all distinct colors eventually blend into a uniform gray. No matter how different the initial colors were, they become indistinguishable.
  • Over-squashing: Think of trying to funnel a vast river through a tiny garden hose. While water (information) might eventually get through, most of its volume and complexity (richness of distant features) is lost or severely compressed at the bottleneck.
💡 If you remember one thing

Both over-smoothing and over-squashing fundamentally limit the effective depth of GNNs. While deeper layers theoretically capture larger receptive fields, these problems prevent GNNs from leveraging information effectively beyond a few layers (typically 2-4).

How They Work Internally
▼

Over-smoothing: The Averaging Effect

Most GNNs operate via iterative message passing, where each node updates its feature vector by aggregating information from its immediate neighbors. This aggregation often involves some form of weighted averaging (e.g., mean, sum, normalized sum). Consider a simple GCN layer:

$$H^{(l+1)} = \sigma(\tilde{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{A}\tilde{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}H^{(l)}W^{(l)})$$

The core operation \(\tilde{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\tilde{A}\tilde{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\) is essentially a graph Laplacian, which performs a local averaging. When this operation is repeated across many layers, information diffuses rapidly throughout the graph. Like heat spreading across a metal plate, initial feature differences are quickly "smoothed out." After enough layers, all nodes within the same connected component will have nearly identical feature vectors, making them indistinguishable for downstream tasks.

Over-squashing: The Information Bottleneck

Over-squashing arises when the receptive field of a node (the set of nodes that can influence its representation) grows exponentially with the number of layers, but the representation itself (a fixed-size vector) does not. To pass information from a large, distant part of the graph to a target node, this information must flow through a relatively small number of intermediate nodes, often a single "bottleneck" node in a long path.

Each intermediate node in the path must compress the vast amount of information from its "upstream" receptive field into its fixed-dimensional feature vector, which then gets passed "downstream." This repeated compression acts as an information bottleneck, leading to a significant loss of detail and distinctiveness from distant nodes. Crucial signals might be "squashed" out of existence before they reach their destination.

Design Decision: Why are GNNs shallow?

Historically, GNNs have been very shallow (2-4 layers) compared to CNNs or Transformers. This is a direct consequence of over-smoothing and over-squashing. Beyond a few layers, performance typically degrades because the node representations either become too uniform (smoothing) or lose critical long-range dependencies (squashing), making deeper models less effective than shallower ones.

🔑 Key Property

Over-smoothing is about feature convergence within a component (loss of local distinction); over-squashing is about information attenuation across long paths (loss of long-range distinctiveness). Both hinder the capacity of deep GNNs to leverage broad contextual information.

3
Why They Work — The Math
▼

Over-smoothing: Convergence to a Fixed Point

Consider a simplified linear GNN layer without non-linearities and weights, where features are simply averaged over neighbors (e.g., a normalized adjacency matrix \(\bar{A}\)):

$$H^{(l+1)} = \bar{A}H^{(l)}$$

If the graph is connected, repeated multiplication by \(\bar{A}\) (which is a stochastic matrix for row-normalized adjacency) will cause \(H^{(l)}\) to converge. Specifically, for any connected component, the rows of \(H^{(l)}\) corresponding to nodes in that component will converge to the same vector, which is proportional to the stationary distribution of a random walk on the graph. Mathematically, as \(l \to \infty\):

$$H^{(l)} \to \mathbf{1} \mathbf{v}^\top$$

where \(\mathbf{1}\) is a vector of ones and \(\mathbf{v}^\top\) is a row vector representing the converged feature. This means all nodes in the component end up with identical features, losing all discriminative power. The rate of convergence depends on the spectral gap of \(\bar{A}\).

Over-squashing: Exponential Information Loss

The information bottleneck of over-squashing can be viewed from an information-theoretic perspective. Consider a path of length \(L\) between two nodes. For information to travel from one end to the other, it must be encoded into the fixed-dimensional feature vectors of \(L-1\) intermediate nodes. If the dimension of the feature vector is \(d\), then the information capacity of a node is \(O(d)\). However, the number of nodes in a \(L\)-hop receptive field can grow exponentially, \(O(k^L)\) for a graph with average degree \(k\). The ratio of information to capacity can be seen as:

$$ \frac{\text{Information from } O(k^L) \text{ nodes}}{\text{Capacity of } O(1) \text{ bottleneck node}} = \frac{O(k^L \cdot d_{input})}{O(d_{node})} $$

When \(k^L\) becomes large, the fixed-size node representation \(d_{node}\) cannot adequately compress and transmit all the relevant information from its exponentially growing receptive field. This leads to an exponential decay of the influence of distant nodes, making it challenging for GNNs to model long-range dependencies effectively.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

Simply adding more layers to a GNN does not automatically increase its effective receptive field or improve its ability to capture long-range dependencies. Without architectural modifications, deeper layers primarily exacerbate over-smoothing and over-squashing, leading to diminishing returns or even performance degradation.

Key Takeaways
▼

When these problems are most acute:

  • Very deep GNNs: The more layers, the more severe the averaging (smoothing) and compression (squashing).
  • Dense graphs: High connectivity accelerates smoothing.
  • Long shortest paths: Long paths between nodes exacerbate over-squashing.
  • When fine-grained node-level distinctions are critical: If the task requires distinguishing between similar nodes, over-smoothing is detrimental.
  • When long-range dependencies are crucial: If information from distant parts of the graph is vital, over-squashing will hinder performance.

Common mitigation strategies:

  • Residual Connections (ResNets for GNNs): Add the input features to the output of a GNN layer (\(H^{(l+1)} = H^{(l)} + \text{GNNLayer}(H^{(l)})\)). This preserves original features, fighting smoothing and making over-squashing less severe.
  • Jumping Knowledge Networks (JKNets): Concatenate or pool node representations from multiple layers, allowing the model to selectively use features from different depths, thus retaining local information from shallow layers while benefiting from broader context from deeper ones.
  • Graph Attention Networks (GATs): Use attention mechanisms to learn weighted aggregations, allowing nodes to selectively focus on more important neighbors, potentially reducing the uniform averaging effect.
  • Positional Encodings: Inject structural or positional information (e.g., shortest path distances, eigenvectors of graph Laplacian) into node features to help distinguish nodes even after smoothing.
  • Higher-dimensional embeddings: Using larger feature vectors can increase the "bandwidth" of nodes, allowing them to carry more information and potentially mitigate over-squashing up to a point.
💡 Big Picture

Much of modern GNN research focuses on designing architectures that can effectively go deeper without succumbing to over-smoothing and over-squashing. These solutions often involve balancing local and global information and enhancing the expressive power of individual node representations.
