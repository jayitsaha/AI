Forward Diffusion Process & Noise Schedules

Gradual noise addition to data, and how linear vs. cosine schedules define its progression
Foundational Generative AI Diffusion Models O(T) steps
1
Understanding the Forward Diffusion Process
▼

What is it? The forward diffusion process is the first half of a Diffusion Model. It's a fixed, predefined Markov chain that gradually adds Gaussian noise to a clean data sample (\(x_0\)) over a series of \(T\) time steps, transforming it into pure noise (\(x_T\)). This process is crucial because the reverse (denoising) process, which is learned by the neural network, must exactly invert it.

Analogy: Imagine you have a pristine photograph. The forward process is like making a copy, then slightly blurring and adding a tiny bit of static to it. Then you make a copy of *that* slightly degraded image and add a bit more blur and static. You repeat this \(T\) times, until the final image is nothing but random static. The core idea is that each step adds only a *little* noise, making the transformation from \(x_t\) to \(x_{t-1}\) (and vice-versa) smooth and manageable.

💡 If you remember one thing

The forward process is a fixed, non-trainable, gradual noise addition defined by a noise schedule (\(\beta_1, \dots, \beta_T\)). It transforms clean data \(x_0\) into pure Gaussian noise \(x_T\) over \(T\) steps. The key is that \(x_t\) can be sampled directly from \(x_0\) and a noise term, which vastly simplifies training.

How It Works: Step-by-Step Noise Addition
▼

The Markov Chain

The forward process is a Markov chain, meaning each step \(x_t\) only depends on the previous step \(x_{t-1}\). At each step \(t \in [1, T]\), a small amount of Gaussian noise is added to \(x_{t-1}\):

$$q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(x_t; \sqrt{1-\beta_t} x_{t-1}, \beta_t I)$$

Here, \(\beta_t\) is a small positive constant (the noise schedule) that dictates the variance of the noise added at step \(t\). Let \(\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t\). The equation implies that \(x_t\) is a weighted sum of the previous state \(x_{t-1}\) (scaled by \(\sqrt{\alpha_t}\)) and a newly sampled noise term (\(\sqrt{\beta_t} \epsilon\), where \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\)).

Direct Sampling from \(x_0\)

A crucial property of this Markov chain is that we can directly sample \(x_t\) from \(x_0\) at any arbitrary time step \(t\), without needing to iterate through all intermediate steps. This is achieved by defining \(\bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{s=1}^t \alpha_s\). The equation for direct sampling is:

$$q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}(x_t; \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} x_0, (1-\bar{\alpha}_t) I)$$

This allows us to obtain any noisy version of \(x_0\) in a single step, which is vital for efficient training of the denoising network. The term \(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\) represents the amount of signal from \(x_0\) remaining at time \(t\), and \(1-\bar{\alpha}_t\) represents the total accumulated noise variance.

Noise Schedules: Linear vs. Cosine

The choice of \(\beta_t\) values (the noise schedule) impacts how quickly the signal degrades. Two common schedules are:

  • Linear Schedule: \(\beta_t\) increases linearly from a small \(\beta_{\text{start}}\) to a larger \(\beta_{\text{end}}\). This means more noise is added in later steps.
  • Cosine Schedule: \(\bar{\alpha}_t\) (the signal retention) follows a cosine curve, which means \(\beta_t\) is small at the beginning and end, and larger in the middle. This schedule has been shown to perform better in many cases as it allows for a smoother transition from signal to noise, especially at the very beginning and end of the process.
🔑 Key Property

The ability to sample \(x_t\) directly from \(x_0\) using \(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} \epsilon\) is the cornerstone of diffusion model training. It means the model can learn to denoise any level of corruption in parallel, rather than sequentially, making training much more efficient.

3
Why It Works: Deriving the Direct Sampling Formula
▼

Recursive Definition of \(x_t\)

The forward process is defined by the recursive relationship:

$$x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t} x_{t-1} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t} \epsilon_{t-1}$$

where \(\epsilon_{t-1} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) is the noise added at step \(t\), and \(\alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t\). We want to find \(x_t\) in terms of \(x_0\).

Step-by-Step Substitution

Let's substitute \(x_{t-1}\) in terms of \(x_{t-2}\):

$$x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t} (\sqrt{\alpha_{t-1}} x_{t-2} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_{t-1}} \epsilon_{t-2}) + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t} \epsilon_{t-1}$$ $$x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t \alpha_{t-1}} x_{t-2} + \sqrt{\alpha_t(1-\alpha_{t-1})} \epsilon_{t-2} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t} \epsilon_{t-1}$$

Since the sum of two independent Gaussian variables is also Gaussian, and \(\sqrt{\alpha_t(1-\alpha_{t-1})} \epsilon_{t-2} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t} \epsilon_{t-1}\) is a sum of two independent Gaussian noise terms, it can be re-expressed as a single Gaussian noise term with combined variance. Specifically, if \(Z_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_1^2)\) and \(Z_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_2^2)\), then \(Z_1+Z_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)\). Here, \(\sigma_1^2 = \alpha_t(1-\alpha_{t-1})\) and \(\sigma_2^2 = (1-\alpha_t)\). The combined variance is \(\alpha_t - \alpha_t \alpha_{t-1} + 1 - \alpha_t = 1 - \alpha_t \alpha_{t-1}\). So we can write:

$$x_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t \alpha_{t-1}} x_{t-2} + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t \alpha_{t-1}} \tilde{\epsilon}_{t-2}$$

where \(\tilde{\epsilon}_{t-2} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\).

Generalizing to \(x_0\)

By repeatedly applying this substitution all the way back to \(x_0\), we arrive at the general formula:

$$x_t = \sqrt{\prod_{s=1}^t \alpha_s} x_0 + \sqrt{1-\prod_{s=1}^t \alpha_s} \epsilon$$

Defining \(\bar{\alpha}_t = \prod_{s=1}^t \alpha_s\), we get the elegant direct sampling formula:

$$x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} \epsilon$$

This shows that \(x_t\) is a weighted average of \(x_0\) and pure Gaussian noise \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\), where the weights are determined by \(\bar{\alpha}_t\), which in turn depends on the entire noise schedule up to time \(t\).

⚠️ Importance of Noise Schedule

The choice of noise schedule (\(\beta_t\)) significantly impacts both the forward process and the subsequent training of the denoising model. A poorly chosen schedule can lead to unstable training or suboptimal generation quality, e.g., if \(\beta_t\) is too large, the signal might be destroyed too quickly, making it hard for the model to learn the reverse transitions.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use which schedule:

  • Linear Schedule: Simple and intuitive. It adds noise at a constant increasing rate. Often a good starting point for experiments.
  • Cosine Schedule: Generally preferred for its smoother transition, especially at very early and very late time steps. It ensures \(\bar{\alpha}_t\) stays close to 1 for longer at the beginning (preserving signal) and decays gracefully at the end (avoiding sudden drops to zero). This often leads to better sample quality and more stable training.

Impact on Training:

  • The forward process is fixed, but its properties (defined by the schedule) directly influence the task of the denoising network.
  • The network is trained to predict the noise \(\epsilon\) given \(x_t\) and \(t\). A well-behaved forward process makes this task easier.
  • The direct sampling formula \(x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} \epsilon\) means the network can be trained on any \(t\) independently, greatly speeding up training compared to a sequential approach.
💡 Big Picture

The forward process is the "known degradation" that the diffusion model learns to reverse. By carefully designing the noise schedule, we can control the difficulty and smoothness of this degradation, which in turn dictates how effectively the reverse (generative) process can be learned and executed.

Code Trace: Implementing Noise Schedules
▼
import numpy as np
T = 200 # Total diffusion steps
def get_noise_schedule(schedule_type='linear', T=200):
if schedule_type == 'linear':
beta_start = 0.0001
beta_end = 0.02
betas = np.linspace(beta_start, beta_end, T)
elif schedule_type == 'cosine':
s = 0.008
t = np.arange(T + 1)
alphas_cumprod = np.cos(((t / T) + s) / (1 + s) * np.pi / 2) ** 2
alphas_cumprod = alphas_cumprod / alphas_cumprod[0]
betas = 1 - (alphas_cumprod[1:] / alphas_cumprod[:-1])
betas = np.clip(betas, a_min=None, a_max=0.999) # Clip for stability
else:
raise ValueError("Unknown schedule type")
return betas
def get_noisy_image(x0, t, noise_sample, betas):
alphas = 1.0 - betas
alpha_bar_t = np.prod(alphas[:t]) # Product up to t-1 (0-indexed)
# Direct sampling formula: x_t = sqrt(alpha_bar_t)*x0 + sqrt(1-alpha_bar_t)*noise
xt = np.sqrt(alpha_bar_t) * x0 + np.sqrt(1 - alpha_bar_t) * noise_sample
return xt, alpha_bar_t
