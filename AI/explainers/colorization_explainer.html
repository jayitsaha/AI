Colorization

Predicting plausible color from grayscale — L → ab as a self-supervised pretext task
Self-Supervised Learning Computer Vision CNN Zhang et al. 2016
1
Understanding Colorization
▼

What is it? Colorization is the task of taking a grayscale photograph — just the lightness channel of an image — and predicting plausible chrominance (color) for every pixel. Working in the perceptually-uniform CIE Lab color space, an image decomposes cleanly into L (lightness, 0–100) and ab (a green↔red axis, b blue↔yellow axis). The grayscale input is the L channel; the model's job is to regress or classify the missing ab channels, which are then recombined with L and converted back to RGB.

Why does this matter for representation learning? Colorization is free: any color photo is automatically a (grayscale-input, color-target) training pair, so there is zero labeling cost. But to color an image well, a network implicitly has to recognize what's in the scene — skin, sky, grass, wood, and vehicles all live in fairly narrow, predictable color neighborhoods. That forces the network's internal features to become semantic, and those features transfer surprisingly well to downstream tasks (classification, detection, segmentation) even though the network was never shown a single class label. This is the core idea of the "pretext task" family of self-supervised learning, alongside jigsaw puzzles, rotation prediction, and inpainting.

💡 If you remember one thing

Colorization is fundamentally ambiguous — an apple could plausibly be red, green, or yellow. Treating it as a per-pixel regression problem (minimize \(\ell_2\) distance to the true \(ab\)) forces the network to average over these possibilities, producing dull, desaturated colors. Zhang et al. (2016) fix this by turning colorization into a classification problem over quantized color bins, with class-rebalancing so rare, vibrant colors aren't drowned out by common desaturated ones.

2
How Colorization Works Internally
▼

Lab space and quantized bins

Lab decouples lightness from color, so a CNN can be handed \(L\) as input and asked only to predict \(ab\) — no need to re-derive brightness. The continuous \(ab\) plane (roughly \([-110,110]^2\)) is discretized into a grid of \(Q\) bins (313 in-gamut bins on a grid of size 10 in the original paper); every pixel's true color is "soft-encoded" onto its 5 nearest bins with a Gaussian kernel, turning a regression target into a categorical distribution.

Network: CNN encoder–decoder with dilated convolutions

The architecture is a VGG-style stack of 8 conv blocks. Instead of aggressively pooling down and then deconvolving back up (which loses spatial precision), the middle blocks use dilated (atrous) convolutions — they grow the receptive field without shrinking resolution as fast, keeping color predictions spatially sharp. The final layer outputs, for every pixel, a full probability distribution \(\hat Z \in \Delta^{Q}\) over the 313 color bins rather than a single \((a,b)\) point.

From distribution back to a color: the annealed mean

A softmax distribution over bins still needs to become one final color per pixel. Taking the raw expectation reintroduces the averaging problem; taking the pure mode (argmax) is vibrant but spatially unstable, flickering between neighboring bins. The annealed mean interpolates: it re-temperatures the distribution with a parameter \(T\), pushing it toward the mode as \(T \to 0\) while keeping it smooth enough to be spatially coherent at a well-chosen \(T\) (the paper uses \(T{=}0.38\)).

🔑 Key Property

Reframing \(ab\)-regression as multinomial classification over color bins preserves the full multimodal belief about a pixel's color for as long as possible, and only collapses it to a single value at the very last step (via annealed-mean decoding) — instead of collapsing it immediately, the way an \(\ell_2\) loss does.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Why \(\ell_2\) regression desaturates

If the true color of a pixel, given its grayscale context, follows a multimodal distribution \(p(a,b)\) (e.g. red / green / yellow for "apple"), the loss-minimizing point prediction under squared error is the mean of that distribution:

$$\hat{y}_{\ell_2} = \arg\min_y \, \mathbb{E}_{(a,b)\sim p}\big[\lVert y - (a,b)\rVert^2\big] = \mathbb{E}_{p}[(a,b)]$$

Averaging red, green, and yellow lands near the achromatic origin — a muddy, low-saturation color that no real apple has. This is why naive regression colorizations look washed out.

Multinomial classification loss with rebalancing

Instead, predict a distribution \(\hat Z_{h,w} \in \Delta^Q\) per pixel and train with a class-rebalanced cross-entropy over the soft-encoded ground truth \(Z_{h,w}\):

$$\mathcal{L}_{cl}(\hat Z, Z) = -\sum_{h,w} v(Z_{h,w}) \sum_{q=1}^{Q} Z_{h,w,q}\,\log \hat Z_{h,w,q}$$

where \(v(\cdot)\) is a per-pixel scalar weight computed from the empirical color prior \(\tilde p_q\) over the training set (desaturated bins near gray are extremely common; saturated bins are rare):

$$w_q \propto \Big[(1-\lambda)\,\tilde p_q + \tfrac{\lambda}{Q}\Big]^{-1}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[w]=1,\ \lambda \approx 0.5$$

Rare, saturated bins get an inverse-frequency boost so the loss doesn't get dominated by the sea of near-gray pixels (sky, pavement, walls).

Annealed-mean decoding

$$f_T(\hat Z) = \frac{\exp(\log \hat Z / T)}{\sum_q \exp(\log \hat Z_q / T)}, \qquad \hat{Y} = \mathbb{E}\big[f_T(\hat Z)\big]$$
⚠️ Common Pitfall

Setting \(T \to 0\) (pure argmax) makes colors vibrant but spatially incoherent — neighboring pixels can jump between competing modes, producing speckled artifacts. Setting \(T{=}1\) (raw expectation) reintroduces the desaturation problem of \(\ell_2\) regression. The annealed mean at a moderate \(T\) is a deliberate compromise, not a free lunch.

Key Takeaways
▼

Why classification beats regression here:

  • Color given grayscale context is genuinely multimodal — an object's color is not a single point estimate
  • \(\ell_2\) loss is minimized by the conditional mean, which collapses multimodal beliefs into desaturated averages
  • A categorical distribution over quantized bins preserves the multiple hypotheses through the forward pass
  • Class rebalancing prevents the loss from being dominated by the abundant near-gray pixels in natural images

Evaluation is unusually hard here:

  • Pixel-wise accuracy against the "ground truth" color is the wrong metric — many plausible colorizations exist
  • The colorization Turing test (real humans judging real vs. generated color) is the paper's headline metric
  • Downstream transfer (fine-tuning colorization-pretrained features for classification/detection/segmentation) validates that the learned features are genuinely semantic, not just colorful
💡 Lineage

Naive L2 colorization ⊂ classification + rebalancing (Zhang et al. 2016) ⊂ later work adding instance awareness, GAN-based realism losses, and modern diffusion-based colorizers — each generation attacks the same core problem: representing and resolving color ambiguity.
