Understanding MME & MMMU
▼

What are they? MME and MMMU are two of the most-cited benchmarks for grading Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) — systems like GPT-4V, Gemini, or LLaVA that read an image and answer a question about it. MME ("MLLM Evaluation") is a fast, cheap diagnostic: 2,374 image-question pairs, each answerable only with "Yes" or "No," spanning 14 sub-abilities split into Perception (can you see it?) and Cognition (can you reason about it?). MMMU ("Massive Multi-discipline Multimodal Understanding") is the opposite extreme: 11,550 college-exam-level questions across 30 subjects and 6 broad disciplines — Art & Design, Business, Science, Health & Medicine, Humanities & Social Science, and Tech & Engineering — that require expert domain knowledge plus reading charts, diagrams, chemical structures, sheet music, or medical scans.

Analogy: MME is a vision screening test — quick yes/no questions like "can you read this line?" that catch basic perceptual failures (hallucinating objects, miscounting, misreading a sign). MMMU is a board-certification exam — it doesn't care if you can see the image at all unless you can also apply the right domain expertise (organic chemistry, microeconomics, anatomy) to answer correctly. A model can ace the vision test and still fail the board exam.

💡 If you remember one thing

MME grades perceptual reliability with a strict pairwise metric — Acc+ requires BOTH the "Yes" and matched "No" question about the same image to be answered correctly, punishing models that just guess "Yes" to everything. MMMU grades expert-level multimodal reasoning across 30 college subjects, and even frontier models (GPT-4V ≈ 56%) trail human experts (≈ 88.6%) by a wide margin: $$\text{Acc}_{+} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M} \mathbb{1}\!\left[\hat y_{j,1}=y_{j,1} \;\wedge\; \hat y_{j,2}=y_{j,2}\right]$$

How These Benchmarks Work Internally
▼

MME: 14 Sub-tasks, Strict Instruction Following

Every MME image gets exactly two hand-crafted questions, one whose ground truth is "Yes" and one whose ground truth is "No" (about the same image, testing the complementary claim). The 14 sub-tasks split into two groups:

Perception (10 tasks, max 200 pts each)Cognition (4 tasks, max 200 pts each)
Existence, Count, Position, Color, Poster, Celebrity, Scene, Landmark, Artwork, OCRCommonsense Reasoning, Numerical Calculation, Text Translation, Code Reasoning

The model's raw output is parsed with an exact-match rule (must contain "yes" or "no"); anything else counts as wrong. This removes grader ambiguity and makes MME cheap and fully automatic — no GPT-4 judge needed.

MMMU: Discipline-Stratified Expert Questions

MMMU questions are collected from college textbooks, quizzes, and exams across 6 disciplines and 30 subjects (e.g., Diagnostics & Laboratory Medicine, Electronics, Music, Geography). Each question pairs one or more images — figures, tables, chemical diagrams, photographs, plots, sheet music — with either a multiple-choice or open (short-answer) prompt. 183 image types are cataloged. Scoring uses exact-match for multiple-choice and rule-based / GPT-assisted matching for open answers, then macro-averages accuracy across subjects so that small, hard disciplines aren't drowned out by large easy ones.

Design Decision: Why Pair Yes/No Questions Instead of Free-form?

Free-form VQA needs a judge model and is sensitive to phrasing. MME's designers chose binary Yes/No with a paired structure specifically so they could measure whether a model is actually perceiving content or just exploiting the "answer yes to sound helpful" bias common in instruction-tuned MLLMs. A model that always says "Yes" scores 50% Acc automatically but 0% Acc+ — an extremely visible tell.

🔑 Key Property

MME isolates perceptual grounding from language-prior bias via the Acc/Acc+ gap; MMMU isolates expert-level multimodal reasoning from mere visual recognition by requiring domain knowledge no amount of "seeing" alone can supply.

Why It Works — The Metrics, Formally
▼

MME: Per-Question and Per-Pair Accuracy

Let there be \(N\) individual Yes/No questions grouped into \(M = N/2\) image pairs. Define the plain accuracy:

$$\text{Acc} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbb{1}[\hat y_i = y_i]$$

and the stricter pairwise accuracy, which only credits an image if both of its questions are answered correctly:

$$\text{Acc}_{+} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M} \mathbb{1}\big[\hat y_{j,1}=y_{j,1}\big]\cdot \mathbb{1}\big[\hat y_{j,2}=y_{j,2}\big]$$

Each of the 14 sub-task scores is then \(\text{Score} = 100\cdot(\text{Acc} + \text{Acc}_{+})\), capped at 200. Perception totals over 10 sub-tasks (max 2000); Cognition totals over 4 sub-tasks (max 800); the headline MME score is their sum (max 2800).

MMMU: Macro-Averaged Discipline Accuracy

For discipline (or subject) \(d\) with \(n_d\) questions, per-discipline accuracy is:

$$\text{Acc}_d = \frac{1}{n_d}\sum_{k=1}^{n_d}\mathbb{1}[\hat y_k = y_k]$$

The overall MMMU score reported in the paper is a macro average across the 30 subjects (each subject weighted equally regardless of size), not a micro (pooled) average — this prevents large subjects like Biology from dominating the headline number:

$$\text{Acc}_{\text{MMMU}} = \frac{1}{30}\sum_{d=1}^{30}\text{Acc}_d$$
⚠️ Common Pitfall

A model can post a deceptively high MME Acc (~75%) by always answering "Yes," since roughly half of MME's ground-truth labels are "Yes" — but Acc+ for that same model collapses toward 0%, because it will always miss the paired "No" question. Always read Acc and Acc+ together, never Acc alone. Likewise, MMMU's macro-average can hide catastrophic failure in a single small discipline; always inspect the per-discipline breakdown, not just the headline number.

Key Takeaways
▼

When each benchmark is diagnostic:

  • Use MME to sanity-check basic visual grounding after fine-tuning or quantizing a model — it's cheap (2,374 pairs), fully automatic, and a low Acc+ instantly reveals a "yes-bias" regression.
  • Use MMMU to assess whether a model can genuinely reason with expert domain knowledge — appropriate for gauging readiness for tutoring, technical assistance, or professional-grade tasks.
  • Run both: a model that is strong on MME but weak on MMMU sees fine but can't reason like a domain expert; the reverse pattern is rare but would flag a benchmark-contamination concern.

Known limitations:

  • MME's binary format is easy to game with output-format tricks and is now near ceiling for top models — diminishing discriminative power.
  • MMMU questions demand OCR-quality image reading plus graduate-level recall; poor scores can reflect either weak perception or weak knowledge — the benchmark doesn't disentangle the two.
  • Both benchmarks are static and can leak into pre-training corpora (data contamination) as newer models are trained on more of the web.
💡 Hierarchy of Difficulty

MME (perceive it) → general VQA (describe it) → MMMU (reason like a domain expert about it). Each stage assumes the previous is solved, which is exactly why frontier labs report both: MME to prove the eyes work, MMMU to prove the brain does too.

import numpy as np
# --- MME: paired Yes/No accuracy ---
# preds/gts: lists of "yes"/"no", grouped in pairs per image
def mme_scores(preds, gts):
preds = [p.strip().lower() for p in preds]
gts = [g.strip().lower() for g in gts]
correct = [p == g for p, g in zip(preds, gts)]
acc = sum(correct) / len(correct)
pairs = [correct[i:i+2] for i in range(0, len(correct), 2)]
acc_plus = sum(all(p) for p in pairs) / len(pairs)
return acc, acc_plus
def subtask_score(acc, acc_plus):
return 100 * (acc + acc_plus) # max 200 per sub-task
# --- MMMU: macro-averaged discipline accuracy ---
def mmmu_macro_accuracy(results_by_subject):
# results_by_subject: {subject: (n_correct, n_total)}
per_subject_acc = [c / t for c, t in results_by_subject.values()]
return sum(per_subject_acc) / len(per_subject_acc)
