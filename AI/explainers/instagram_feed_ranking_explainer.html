Understanding Feed Ranking
▼

What is it? Every time you open Instagram, the app must choose roughly 50-100 posts out of many thousands of candidates (everything posted recently by accounts you follow, plus everything a recommender engine thinks you might like) and lay them out in the single scrollable order you actually see. Feed ranking is the system that makes that choice, in well under a second, for over a billion people, updated continuously as new posts arrive and as you interact.

Analogy: Think of it as a personal newspaper editor who reads every wire story that could go on your front page, silently predicts "will this reader like it, comment on it, share it, or linger on it?", converts those guesses into a single importance score, and then reshuffles the layout so you are not reading five stories from the same reporter in a row. The editor never shows you the same story order as your neighbor — the page is assembled fresh, for you, on every visit.

💡 If you remember one thing

Feed ranking is not "most recent first" — it is predicted-value first. A multi-task model estimates several action probabilities per post, a value model turns those into one number, and a re-ranking pass fixes what raw scoring alone would get wrong (repetition, staleness, policy risk). $$\text{value}(p) = \sum_k w_k \cdot \hat{P}_k(p)$$

How It Works Internally
▼

Stage 1 — Candidate Generation

Ranking every post ever made would be computationally impossible, so the pipeline first narrows billions of posts down to a few hundred candidates per request. Two sources feed the candidate pool: (1) the follow graph — recent posts from accounts you follow, retrieved cheaply by recency and author; and (2) a recommendation/retrieval engine — posts from accounts you do not follow, surfaced via embedding-similarity search (two-tower retrieval) over your interest profile. This mirrors the classic two-stage recommender pattern: cheap, high-recall retrieval followed by expensive, high-precision ranking.

Stage 2 — Multi-Task Ranking Model

Every candidate is scored by a single neural network with shared bottom layers and several independent output heads — one per predicted action: \(P(\text{like})\), \(P(\text{comment})\), \(P(\text{share})\), and \(E[\text{time-spent})]\). Sharing representations across tasks is deliberate: "share" is a rare, sparse label, and it benefits from the statistical strength of the much more common "like" label learned in the same backbone. Training and serving one multi-task model is also cheaper than maintaining four separate models.

Stage 3 — Value Model (Blending)

Four separate probabilities cannot be sorted directly — you need one number. A value model combines the per-task predictions into a single scalar using weights that encode product priorities (a comment signals more investment than a like; a share indicates the content is worth distributing further, so it typically gets the largest weight). Sorting candidates by this blended value produces the initial ranked list.

Stage 4 — Diversity & Integrity Re-Rank

The raw sort is myopic: it can place three posts from your most-engaged-with friend back to back, or three near-identical reels in a row. A final deterministic pass walks the sorted list and enforces constraints the value model does not know about — author diversity (no two consecutive posts from the same author), content-type spacing, and safety/integrity demotions — before inserting ads at fixed slot positions. The result is the feed you actually scroll.

🔑 Key Property

Every impression, like, comment, share, and skip becomes a new training label. The multi-task model is retrained on this feedback loop continuously, so the ranker you see today was shaped by the aggregate behavior of everyone who used the app yesterday.

Why It Works — The Math
▼

Feature Vector

Each candidate post \(p\) is described by a feature vector, e.g. \(x = [\text{affinity}, \text{recency}, \text{media\_boost}, \text{close\_friend}]\), where affinity is a learned relationship-strength score between viewer and author, and recency decays exponentially: \(\text{recency} = e^{-\Delta t / \tau}\).

Multi-Task Heads

Classification heads (like, comment, share) use a logistic output on top of shared hidden features \(h(x)\):

$$\hat{P}_k(p) = \sigma(w_k^\top h(x) + b_k), \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$$

The time-spent head is a regression, typically passed through a softplus to keep predictions non-negative:

$$\hat{E}[\text{time}(p)] = \text{softplus}(w_t^\top h(x) + b_t) = \ln(1 + e^{w_t^\top h(x) + b_t})$$

Value Blending

The value model is a weighted linear combination, with weights \(w_k\) tuned offline (often via A/B tests) to reflect business and integrity priorities:

$$\text{value}(p) = w_{\text{like}}\hat{P}_{\text{like}} + w_{\text{comment}}\hat{P}_{\text{comment}} + w_{\text{share}}\hat{P}_{\text{share}} + w_{\text{time}}\hat{E}[\text{time}]$$

Ranking is then \(\text{argsort}_p(-\text{value}(p))\), an \(O(N\log N)\) sort over the candidate pool, followed by an \(O(N)\) greedy pass that swaps items forward only when a diversity constraint (e.g. \(\text{author}(p_i) \neq \text{author}(p_{i-1})\)) is violated.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

The multi-task losses are usually combined as \(\mathcal{L} = \sum_k \lambda_k \mathcal{L}_k\) (cross-entropy for the classification heads, Huber/MSE for time-spent). Naively fixed \(\lambda_k\) can let one easy task (e.g. "like", which is dense and easy to predict) dominate gradients and starve rarer tasks like "share" — uncertainty-weighted or gradient-normalized multi-task losses fix this.

Key Takeaways
▼

When multi-task, value-based ranking earns its complexity:

  • You have several correlated but differently-sparse engagement signals (likes are dense, shares are rare) — a shared backbone improves sample efficiency on the rare ones.
  • The product has more than one goal (engagement today vs. long-term retention vs. creator distribution) that a single click-through-rate label cannot capture.
  • The candidate pool is large enough that a cheap retrieval stage plus an expensive ranking stage beats scoring everything.

Common pitfalls:

  • Engagement bait: over-weighting time-spent or comments alone can promote outrage or clickbait content that maximizes the metric but hurts the actual product goal.
  • Filter bubbles: skipping the diversity re-rank step lets the value model happily show ten posts from your single most-engaged-with friend.
  • Position bias: training labels collected only from what the previous ranker showed near the top under-represent posts that were buried — left uncorrected, this reinforces the old ranking policy instead of improving it.
  • Feedback loops: retraining purely on engagement can amplify whatever the current model already over-shows, narrowing the diversity of what users ever see.
💡 Hierarchy

Chronological feed ⊂ affinity-weighted feed ⊂ single-task ML ranking ⊂ multi-task value-based ranking + diversity re-rank. Each layer relaxes an assumption of the previous one — first that recency is all that matters, then that one signal is enough, then that a raw sort is a good final answer.

# Simplified feed-ranking service (one request)
def rank_feed(viewer_id):
followed = fetch_followed_candidates(viewer_id, limit=400)
recommended = retrieval_engine.search(viewer_id, limit=200)
candidates = followed + recommended
features = [extract_features(viewer_id, p) for p in candidates]
# one multi-task forward pass, four heads
preds = multitask_model.predict_batch(features)
# preds[i] = {"like":.., "comment":.., "share":.., "time":..}
for p, pred in zip(candidates, preds):
p.value = (W_LIKE * pred["like"] + W_COMMENT * pred["comment"]
+ W_SHARE * pred["share"] + W_TIME * pred["time"])
ranked = sorted(candidates, key=lambda p: -p.value)
diverse = diversity_rerank(ranked) # greedy author-spacing pass
feed = insert_ads(diverse, every=5)
return feed[:100]
def diversity_rerank(items):
remaining, out, last_author = list(items), [], None
while remaining:
idx = next((i for i, p in enumerate(remaining)
if p.author != last_author), 0)
chosen = remaining.pop(idx)
out.append(chosen)
last_author = chosen.author
return out
