Eigenvalue Applications

PCA, PageRank, Markov chains, and vibrational modes — four domains, one spectral idea
Intermediate Linear Algebra Spectral Theory 4 Applications
1
The Unifying Idea
▼

What are eigenvalues doing? Every matrix \(A\) has special directions \(\mathbf{v}\) it can't rotate — it can only stretch or shrink them. These are the eigenvectors, and the stretch factors are the eigenvalues: \(A\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}\). The dominant eigenvector (largest \(|\lambda|\)) is the direction the matrix "most wants to push things toward" — and this one idea powers four very different fields.

Analogy: Imagine repeatedly folding a piece of paper in the same direction. No matter where you start, after many folds the paper lines up with one special crease. That crease is the dominant eigenvector. Eigenvalues describe how quickly each crease "wins." PCA finds the crease in data spread; PageRank finds where web-surfer probability concentrates; Markov chains find where a random walk settles; vibrational modes find the natural rhythms of a structure.

💡 The Spectral Theorem in One Line

For a symmetric matrix \(A\), the eigendecomposition \(A = Q\Lambda Q^\top\) is a basis of principal directions — each eigenvector \(\mathbf{q}_i\) is orthogonal, each eigenvalue \(\lambda_i\) is real, and the whole matrix is just stretching along those directions. This is why four completely different applications reduce to the same computation.

🔑 The Four Applications
  • PCA: Top eigenvectors of the covariance matrix = directions of maximum variance.
  • PageRank: Stationary distribution = dominant eigenvector of the Google matrix \(G\).
  • Markov chains: Steady state \(\pi\) satisfies \(P\pi = \pi\), i.e., \(\lambda=1\) eigenvector of the transition matrix.
  • Vibrational modes: Natural frequencies \(\omega\) and mode shapes \(\mathbf{v}\) satisfy \(K\mathbf{v} = \omega^2 M\mathbf{v}\) (generalized eigenvalue problem).
2
PCA — Top Eigenvectors of the Covariance Matrix
▼

Given data matrix \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\), center it: \(\tilde{X} = X - \bar{X}\). The sample covariance is \(\Sigma = \frac{1}{n-1}\tilde{X}^\top \tilde{X}\). PCA eigendecomposes \(\Sigma\): the eigenvectors are the principal components (directions of maximum variance), and the eigenvalues tell you how much variance each direction captures.

$$\Sigma \mathbf{v}_k = \lambda_k \mathbf{v}_k, \quad \lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_d \geq 0$$

Numerical trace (4 points, 2D): With data \(X = \{(2.5,2.4),(0.5,0.7),(2.2,2.9),(1.9,2.2)\}\), the covariance is \(\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix}0.7825 & 0.785\\0.785 & 0.8967\end{bmatrix}\). Solving \(\Sigma\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}\) gives:

$$\lambda_1 = 1.627,\; \mathbf{v}_1 = [0.681,\; 0.732]^\top \quad \text{(captures 96.9\% of variance)}$$
$$\lambda_2 = 0.053,\; \mathbf{v}_2 = [-0.732,\; 0.681]^\top \quad \text{(only 3.1\%)}$$

PC1 points along the axis of greatest spread — nearly the 45° diagonal since both features correlate strongly. Projecting onto PC1 compresses the 2D cloud to 1D while preserving 96.9% of the information.

⚠️ Scale Sensitivity

PCA eigenvectors depend on feature scale. Always standardize features (subtract mean, divide by std) before PCA when features have different units — otherwise the feature with the largest numeric range will dominate the first PC regardless of its true importance.

3
PageRank & Markov Chains — Dominant Eigenvector = Long-Run Distribution
▼

Markov Chains: Steady State

A Markov chain has a column-stochastic transition matrix \(P\) (each column sums to 1). The steady-state distribution \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\) satisfies \(P\boldsymbol{\pi} = \boldsymbol{\pi}\), which is exactly the eigenvector equation for \(\lambda = 1\). By the Perron-Frobenius theorem, if \(P\) is irreducible and aperiodic, \(\lambda = 1\) is unique and the corresponding eigenvector is unique.

$$P\boldsymbol{\pi} = 1 \cdot \boldsymbol{\pi} \implies \boldsymbol{\pi} = \lim_{t\to\infty} P^t \boldsymbol{\pi}_0$$

PageRank: The Google Matrix

The web is a directed graph. Define the link matrix \(H\) where \(H_{ij} = 1/\text{out-degree}(j)\) if \(j \to i\), else 0. The Google matrix adds a damping factor \(d = 0.85\) to handle dangling nodes and make the chain ergodic:

$$G = d \cdot H + \frac{1-d}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top$$

PageRank \(\mathbf{r}\) is the dominant eigenvector of \(G\) (eigenvalue 1): \(G\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}\). It equals the stationary distribution of a random surfer who follows links with probability \(d = 0.85\) and teleports uniformly with probability \(0.15\).

Numerical trace (3-node graph):

Nodes A, B, C with edges: A→B, A→C, B→C, C→A. Link matrix \(H\) (column-stochastic): A splits equally to B,C; B points to C; C points to A.

$$H = \begin{bmatrix}0 & 0 & 1\\0.5 & 0 & 0\\0.5 & 1 & 0\end{bmatrix}, \quad G = 0.85H + \frac{0.15}{3}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top$$
$$G = \begin{bmatrix}0.050 & 0.050 & 0.900\\0.475 & 0.050 & 0.050\\0.475 & 0.900 & 0.050\end{bmatrix}$$

Power iteration converges to: \(\mathbf{r} = [0.388,\; 0.215,\; 0.397]^\top\). Node C ranks highest because it receives links from both A (half-share) and B (full share), giving it the highest incoming PageRank weight.

📐 Why Power Iteration Works

Any vector \(\mathbf{r}_0 = \sum_i c_i \mathbf{v}_i\) decomposes over eigenvectors. Applying \(G^k\): the component along \(\mathbf{v}_1\) (\(\lambda_1=1\)) is unchanged, while all other components decay as \(\lambda_i^k \to 0\). The rate of convergence is the spectral gap \(1 - |\lambda_2|\).

Vibrational Modes — Eigenvectors of the Stiffness Matrix
▼

A mechanical system of masses connected by springs is governed by Newton's second law: \(M\ddot{\mathbf{x}} + K\mathbf{x} = 0\), where \(M\) is the mass matrix (diagonal) and \(K\) is the stiffness matrix (symmetric PSD). Substituting \(\mathbf{x}(t) = \mathbf{v}e^{i\omega t}\):

$$(K - \omega^2 M)\mathbf{v} = 0 \implies K\mathbf{v} = \omega^2 M\mathbf{v}$$

This is a generalized eigenvalue problem. The eigenvalues \(\omega^2\) give the squared natural frequencies; the eigenvectors \(\mathbf{v}\) give the mode shapes — how each mass moves relative to the others in that mode.

Numerical trace (2-mass system):

Masses \(m_1 = m_2 = 1\) kg, springs \(k_1 = 2\) N/m (wall to mass 1 and between masses), \(k_2 = 1\) N/m (between masses). Stiffness matrix:

$$K = \begin{bmatrix}k_1+k_2 & -k_2\\-k_2 & k_2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}3 & -1\\-1 & 1\end{bmatrix}, \quad M = I_2$$

Since \(M = I\), this reduces to ordinary eigenvalue problem. Eigenvalues and mode shapes:

$$\omega_1^2 = 0.586 \implies \omega_1 = 0.765 \text{ rad/s}, \quad \mathbf{v}_1 = [-0.383, -0.924]^\top$$
$$\omega_2^2 = 3.414 \implies \omega_2 = 1.848 \text{ rad/s}, \quad \mathbf{v}_2 = [-0.924, +0.383]^\top$$

Mode 1 (low frequency): both masses move in the same direction (in-phase). Mode 2 (high frequency): masses move in opposite directions (out-of-phase). Any motion of the system is a superposition of these two normal modes — the eigenvectors provide the natural basis for the dynamics.

💡 Why Eigenvectors Decouple the System

In the eigenvector basis (modal coordinates), the coupled ODEs \(M\ddot{\mathbf{x}} + K\mathbf{x} = 0\) transform into \(n\) independent simple harmonic oscillators \(\ddot{q}_i + \omega_i^2 q_i = 0\). This decoupling is precisely the spectral theorem at work — \(Q^\top KQ = \Lambda\) is diagonal.

Key Takeaways & Interview Prep
▼

The common thread: In each application, repeated matrix multiplication \(A^k \mathbf{x}\) selects out the dominant eigenvector. PCA uses this via SVD; PageRank via power iteration; Markov chains via the same; vibrations via the generalized eigensolver.

  • PCA: \(\Sigma = Q\Lambda Q^\top\); explained variance ratio \(= \lambda_k / \sum_i \lambda_i\).
  • PageRank: \(d = 0.85\) balances influence vs. teleportation; changing \(d\) shifts how fast convergence occurs.
  • Markov steady state: Exists and is unique iff the chain is irreducible and aperiodic (Perron-Frobenius).
  • Vibration: Symmetric \(K\) guarantees real, non-negative \(\omega^2\); non-symmetric systems can have complex frequencies (damped modes).

Common interview Q — "Why does PCA use eigenvectors of \(\Sigma\) and not \(X\)?"
The covariance \(\Sigma\) is symmetric PSD, so it has a clean orthogonal eigendecomposition. In practice, PCA is computed via SVD of \(\tilde{X}\) (numerically more stable than forming \(\Sigma\)), but the result is equivalent: left singular vectors of \(\tilde{X}\) = eigenvectors of \(\tilde{X}\tilde{X}^\top\), right singular vectors = eigenvectors of \(\Sigma\).

🔑 Perron-Frobenius Theorem (the guarantee)

For a non-negative irreducible matrix: (1) there is a unique largest real eigenvalue \(\lambda_1 > 0\); (2) it has a unique positive eigenvector (up to scale); (3) all other eigenvalues satisfy \(|\lambda_i| < \lambda_1\). This is why PageRank and Markov steady states are well-defined and unique.
