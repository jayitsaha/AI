Weighted Least Squares (WLS)

Regression for heteroscedastic data — a special case of GLS with diagonal weights \(W = \text{diag}(1/\sigma_i^2)\)
Intermediate Classical ML Stats O(n·p²) solve
1
Understanding WLS
▼

What is it? Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) treats every data point as equally trustworthy. But when some measurements are inherently noisier than others — a phenomenon called heteroscedasticity (unequal variance) — OLS is inefficient: it wastes statistical power by giving too much weight to unreliable, high-noise points. Weighted Least Squares (WLS) fixes this by assigning each point a weight inversely proportional to its noise level.

Analogy: Imagine two thermometers measuring the same room: one is a precise lab instrument (accurate to ±0.1°C), the other is a cheap kitchen gadget (accurate to ±5°C). When averaging their readings, you should trust the lab instrument 2500× more (since variance scales with squared error). WLS does exactly this — it discounts noisy measurements so the fitted line is pulled toward the reliable data.

💡 If you remember one thing

WLS is GLS with a diagonal weight matrix: \(W = \text{diag}(1/\sigma_1^2, 1/\sigma_2^2, \ldots, 1/\sigma_n^2)\). The estimator $$\hat{\beta}_{WLS} = (X^\top W X)^{-1} X^\top W y$$ is identical to the GLS formula \(\hat{\beta}_{GLS} = (X^\top \Omega^{-1} X)^{-1} X^\top \Omega^{-1} y\) with \(\Omega = W^{-1} = \text{diag}(\sigma_i^2)\). OLS is the further special case \(W = I\).

The Funnel Shape: Classic Heteroscedasticity

A common signature of heteroscedasticity is a "funnel" in the residual plot: variance fans out as \(x\) increases. This happens in economics (income vs. spending), biology (body size vs. metabolic rate), and engineering (load vs. deformation). OLS gives equal weight to the wide, noisy right end of the funnel as to the tight, precise left end — pulling the fitted line off course.

🔑 Relation to GLS

In the GLS framework, the error covariance matrix is \(\Omega = \text{diag}(\sigma_1^2, \ldots, \sigma_n^2)\). WLS uses \(\Omega^{-1} = W = \text{diag}(1/\sigma_i^2)\). Because \(\Omega\) is diagonal, there are no off-diagonal correlations to worry about — WLS handles heteroscedasticity only, not autocorrelation. Full GLS is needed when errors are also correlated.

2
How WLS Works Internally
▼

Step 1: Assign Weights

For each observation \(i\), assign weight \(w_i = 1/\sigma_i^2\) where \(\sigma_i^2 = \text{Var}(\varepsilon_i)\). In practice \(\sigma_i^2\) is unknown and must be estimated — common approaches include: (a) grouping observations and using group variances, (b) regressing squared residuals on predictors (feasible WLS / FWLS), or (c) using domain knowledge about the variance function.

Step 2: Minimize the Weighted Objective

WLS minimizes a weighted sum of squared residuals rather than the ordinary RSS:

$$\text{WRSS}(\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i (y_i - x_i^\top \beta)^2 = (y - X\beta)^\top W (y - X\beta)$$

where \(W = \text{diag}(w_1, \ldots, w_n)\). Points with large \(\sigma_i^2\) (noisy) get small \(w_i\), so their residuals barely contribute to the objective. Points with small \(\sigma_i^2\) (precise) get large \(w_i\), pulling the line toward them strongly.

Step 3: Solve the Weighted Normal Equations

Taking the gradient of WRSS and setting to zero:

$$\frac{\partial \text{WRSS}}{\partial \beta} = -2X^\top W(y - X\beta) = 0 \implies X^\top W X \hat{\beta} = X^\top W y$$
$$\hat{\beta}_{WLS} = (X^\top W X)^{-1} X^\top W y$$

Information Flow Through WLS

Input: Design matrix \(X\) (n×p), response \(y\) (n×1), weights \(w_i = 1/\sigma_i^2\) (n×1).

Rescale: Form \(\tilde{X} = W^{1/2}X\) and \(\tilde{y} = W^{1/2}y\) where \(W^{1/2} = \text{diag}(\sqrt{w_i})\). Now OLS on \((\tilde{X}, \tilde{y})\) gives exactly \(\hat{\beta}_{WLS}\) — this is the "transform then solve" geometric intuition.

Output: \(\hat{\beta}_{WLS}\) that is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE) under the heteroscedastic model, with covariance \(\text{Cov}(\hat{\beta}_{WLS}) = \sigma^2 (X^\top W X)^{-1}\).

⚠️ Practical Pitfall

WLS is only BLUE when the weights are the true inverse variances. Using wrong weights (e.g., estimated from a poor variance model) can make WLS worse than OLS. Feasible WLS (FWLS) iterates: estimate \(\beta\) → estimate \(\sigma_i^2\) from residuals → re-weight → repeat until convergence.

3
The Math — Full Derivation
▼

Model Setup

The linear model with heteroscedastic errors: \(y_i = x_i^\top \beta + \varepsilon_i\) where \(\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_i^2)\) are independent but have different variances. In matrix form: \(y = X\beta + \varepsilon\), \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Omega)\) with \(\Omega = \text{diag}(\sigma_1^2, \ldots, \sigma_n^2)\).

Derivation via Optimisation

The WLS objective is:

$$S(\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i (y_i - x_i^\top \beta)^2 = (y - X\beta)^\top W (y - X\beta)$$

Expand: \(S(\beta) = y^\top W y - 2\beta^\top X^\top W y + \beta^\top X^\top W X \beta\)

Differentiate with respect to \(\beta\) and set to zero:

$$\nabla_\beta S = -2X^\top W y + 2X^\top W X \beta = 0$$

Solving the weighted normal equations (assuming \(X^\top W X\) is invertible):

$$\hat{\beta}_{WLS} = (X^\top W X)^{-1} X^\top W y$$

Why This is the MLE (Under Gaussianity)

The log-likelihood under \(\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_i^2)\) independent is:

$$\ell(\beta) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi) - \frac{1}{2}\sum_i \log \sigma_i^2 - \frac{1}{2}\sum_i \frac{(y_i - x_i^\top \beta)^2}{\sigma_i^2}$$

The last term is exactly \(-\frac{1}{2}\text{WRSS}(\beta)\) with \(w_i = 1/\sigma_i^2\). Maximising \(\ell\) over \(\beta\) is equivalent to minimising WRSS — so WLS is the MLE.

Gauss-Markov (Generalised) Theorem

Among all linear unbiased estimators \(\hat{\beta} = Cy\) (for any matrix \(C\) with \(CX = I\)), \(\hat{\beta}_{WLS}\) has the minimum covariance matrix in the positive semi-definite sense:

$$\text{Cov}(\hat{\beta}_{WLS}) = (X^\top W X)^{-1} \preceq \text{Cov}(\hat{\beta}) \quad \forall \text{ linear unbiased } \hat{\beta}$$

Numerical Trace (n=12 funnel data)

With \(x \in [1, 6]\), \(\sigma_i = 0.3 x_i\), true \(\beta = [2.0,\, 1.5]^\top\):

$$X^\top W X = \begin{bmatrix} 26.887 & 50.681 \\ 50.681 & 133.333 \end{bmatrix}, \quad X^\top W y = \begin{bmatrix} 135.403 \\ 313.201 \end{bmatrix}$$
$$(X^\top W X)^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.1312 & -0.0499 \\ -0.0499 & 0.0265 \end{bmatrix}$$
$$\hat{\beta}_{WLS} = \begin{bmatrix} 2.145 \\ 1.534 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{vs} \quad \hat{\beta}_{OLS} = \begin{bmatrix} 2.665 \\ 1.367 \end{bmatrix} \quad \text{(true: } [2.0,\, 1.5]^\top\text{)}$$

WLS recovers near-true coefficients; OLS is biased toward the noisy high-\(x\) region. The point at \(x=1\) (tight noise) has weight 11.1; at \(x=6\) (wide noise) weight only 0.309 — a 36× ratio in influence.

📐 Key Formula

$$\hat{\beta}_{WLS} = (X^\top W X)^{-1} X^\top W y, \quad W = \text{diag}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\sigma_1^2}, \ldots, \tfrac{1}{\sigma_n^2}\right)$$

import numpy as np
# Data: x in [1,6], variance grows with x (funnel)
x = np.linspace(1, 6, 12)
sigma_i = 0.3 * x # σᵢ = 0.3·xᵢ
y = 2.0 + 1.5*x + np.random.normal(0, sigma_i)
# OLS: minimize Σ(yᵢ - ŷᵢ)² equally
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(12), x])
beta_ols = np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0]
# WLS weights: wᵢ = 1/σᵢ²
w = 1.0 / sigma_i**2 # [11.1, 5.3, 3.0, ..., 0.31]
W = np.diag(w) # diagonal weight matrix
# WLS: solve (XᵀWX)β = XᵀWy
XtW = X.T @ W
XtWX = XtW @ X # [[26.89, 50.68], [50.68, 133.33]]
XtWy = XtW @ y # [135.40, 313.20]
beta_wls = np.linalg.solve(XtWX, XtWy) # [2.145, 1.534]
# Compare
print(f"OLS: intercept={beta_ols[0]:.3f}, slope={beta_ols[1]:.3f}")
print(f"WLS: intercept={beta_wls[0]:.3f}, slope={beta_wls[1]:.3f}")
print(f"True: intercept=2.000, slope=1.500")

# WLS: 2.145, 1.534 ← much closer to truth

Interview Q&A + Comparisons
▼

Q1 (Easy): What is heteroscedasticity?

Heteroscedasticity means the variance of the error term \(\varepsilon_i\) is not constant across observations: \(\text{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma_i^2\) differs by \(i\). The opposite, equal variance, is homoscedasticity. OLS assumes homoscedasticity for efficiency; violation doesn't bias OLS but inflates standard errors and makes inference wrong.

Q2 (Medium): Why is OLS unbiased but inefficient under heteroscedasticity?

OLS is still unbiased because \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_{OLS}] = \beta\) regardless of \(\Omega\) (as long as \(\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon]=0\) and \(X\) is fixed). But OLS is not BLUE — the Gauss-Markov theorem requires \(\text{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2 I\). With heteroscedasticity, WLS achieves strictly lower variance: \(\text{Cov}(\hat{\beta}_{WLS}) \preceq \text{Cov}(\hat{\beta}_{OLS})\). Additionally, the OLS standard errors are incorrect — leading to wrong confidence intervals and p-values.

Q3 (Medium): How do you detect heteroscedasticity?

Common methods: (1) Breusch-Pagan test — regress squared OLS residuals on predictors; test F-stat for joint significance. (2) White test — includes cross-products and squares of predictors. (3) Visual: residual plot (residuals vs. fitted); funnel or fan shape indicates heteroscedasticity. (4) Goldfeld-Quandt test — split data, compare variances of two halves.

Q4 (Hard): What if you use wrong weights in WLS?

WLS with incorrect weights is still unbiased (since \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}_{WLS}] = \beta\) for any non-singular \(W\)) but is no longer BLUE. Depending on how wrong the weights are, WLS can have higher variance than OLS. The covariance matrix of WLS under misspecified weights is \(\text{Cov}(\hat{\beta}_{WLS}) = (X^\top W X)^{-1} X^\top W \Omega W X (X^\top W X)^{-1}\) — a sandwich estimator. When \(W = \Omega^{-1}\) (correct), this simplifies to \((X^\top \Omega^{-1} X)^{-1}\). In practice: use heteroscedasticity-robust (HC) standard errors with OLS if uncertain about the variance model.

Q5 (Hard): Explain WLS vs GLS vs FWLS to a research scientist.

GLS handles arbitrary error covariance \(\Omega\) — both unequal variances and correlations. \(\hat{\beta}_{GLS} = (X^\top \Omega^{-1} X)^{-1} X^\top \Omega^{-1} y\). WLS is GLS restricted to diagonal \(\Omega = \text{diag}(\sigma_i^2)\) — only variance, no correlation. FWLS (feasible WLS) estimates \(\sigma_i^2\) from data: fit OLS, regress \(\hat{\varepsilon}_i^2\) on predictors to get \(\hat{\sigma}_i^2\), set \(w_i = 1/\hat{\sigma}_i^2\), refit. Iterate until convergence. FWLS is asymptotically equivalent to WLS with known weights but adds estimation uncertainty.

🎯 Decision Rule

Use WLS when you can estimate \(\sigma_i^2\) reliably. Use OLS + robust SE (White/HC3) when you want efficiency without the variance model risk. Use GLS when errors are also correlated (panel data, time series). Use FWLS iteratively when the variance structure must be estimated.

Key Takeaways & Related Topics
▼

Summary

WLS is the principled fix for heteroscedasticity in linear regression. It is: (1) a special case of GLS with diagonal \(W = \Omega^{-1}\); (2) the MLE under independent Gaussian errors with known variances; (3) BLUE by the generalized Gauss-Markov theorem when weights are correct; (4) equivalent to OLS on rescaled data \(\tilde{X} = W^{1/2}X\), \(\tilde{y} = W^{1/2}y\).

Prerequisites (should know first)

OLS / Linear Regression basics; matrix algebra (rank, inverse); statistical inference (bias, variance, BLUE); the GLS framework (WLS is a special case — see the GLS page in this vault).

What to Learn Next

Key Papers
