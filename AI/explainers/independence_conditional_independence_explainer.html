Independence & Conditional Independence

When does knowing A change what you believe about B? The foundational distinction in probabilistic reasoning.
Intermediate Probability & Statistics Foundations Must Know
1
Understanding the Concept
▾

Two events are independent when knowing that one occurred gives you zero information about whether the other occurred. Flip a fair coin twice: learning that the first flip was Heads tells you absolutely nothing about the second flip. The second flip doesn't care about the first.

Conditional independence is subtler. Events that are independent can become dependent once you condition on a third variable — and events that are dependent can become independent once you condition. This is the phenomenon that powers Bayesian networks, the Naive Bayes classifier, and the d-separation algorithm.

Key Concept — Independence

Two events \(A\) and \(B\) are independent if and only if $$P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)$$ Equivalently: \(P(A \mid B) = P(A)\) — conditioning on \(B\) does not update your belief about \(A\).

Real-World Analogy

Think of independence as two separate dice. Rolling a 6 on the first die never changes the odds of any face on the second die. But conditional independence is like two smoke detectors in the same house: whether the left detector fires and whether the right detector fires are correlated (both triggered by fire). But once you already know whether there is a fire, the two detectors become independent — knowing one fired adds nothing once the fire's presence is established.

If You Remember One Thing

Conditioning can both create and destroy independence. Marginal independence does not imply conditional independence, and vice versa. Always ask: independent given what?

2
Independence — Formal Definition & Numerical Trace
▾

Formal Definition

Let \(\Omega\) be a probability space. Events \(A, B \subseteq \Omega\) are independent, written \(A \perp B\), when:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)$$

For random variables \(X\) and \(Y\), independence requires that the joint distribution factors: \(P(X=x, Y=y) = P(X=x)\,P(Y=y)\) for all \(x, y\). Equivalently, knowing \(X\) gives no information about \(Y\): \(P(Y \mid X) = P(Y)\).

Bespoke Numerical Trace — Two Coin Flips

Let \(A\) = "first coin is Heads" and \(B\) = "second coin is Heads". Both coins are fair and flipped independently.

OutcomeP
HH (A∩B)0.25
HT (A, ¬B)0.25
TH (¬A, B)0.25
TT (¬A, ¬B)0.25
$$P(A) = 0.5,\quad P(B) = 0.5,\quad P(A \cap B) = 0.25 = 0.5 \times 0.5 \checkmark$$

Conditional check: \(P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B)/P(B) = 0.25/0.5 = 0.5 = P(A)\). Knowing the second flip is Heads does not update our belief about the first. ✓

Independence of More Than Two Events

Events \(A_1, \ldots, A_n\) are mutually independent if for every subset \(S \subseteq \{1,\ldots,n\}\):

$$P\!\left(\bigcap_{i \in S} A_i\right) = \prod_{i \in S} P(A_i)$$
Warning — Pairwise ≠ Mutual Independence

Three events can be pairwise independent yet NOT mutually independent. Classic example: roll two dice, \(A\)="first die odd", \(B\)="second die odd", \(C\)="sum is odd". Each pair is independent, but \(P(A \cap B \cap C) = 0 \neq P(A)P(B)P(C) = 1/8\).

3
Conditional Independence & Explaining Away
▾

Definition of Conditional Independence

Events \(A\) and \(B\) are conditionally independent given \(C\), written \(A \perp B \mid C\), when:

$$P(A \cap B \mid C) = P(A \mid C)\,P(B \mid C)$$

Equivalently: \(P(A \mid B, C) = P(A \mid C)\) — once \(C\) is known, \(B\) adds nothing about \(A\).

The Sprinkler Model — Numerically Exact

Consider the classic Bayes Net: Rain \((R)\) and Sprinkler \((S)\) both cause Wet Grass \((W)\). Priors:

VariablePriorNotes
\(P(R=\text{T})\)0.20It rains 20% of days
\(P(S=\text{T} \mid R=\text{T})\)0.01Sprinkler rarely used when raining
\(P(S=\text{T} \mid R=\text{F})\)0.40Sprinkler more common in dry weather
\(P(W=\text{T} \mid R,S)\)0.99 / 0.80 / 0.90 / 0.001TT/TF/FT/FF
$$P(R) \approx 0.200,\quad P(S) \approx 0.322,\quad P(R \cap S) = 0.002$$
$$P(R)\cdot P(S) = 0.200 \times 0.322 = 0.0644 \neq P(R \cap S) = 0.002$$

So Rain and Sprinkler are not marginally independent (they share the common-cause structure through W — and here they are also directly linked via the conditional \(P(S \mid R)\)). Observe what happens when we condition on Wet Grass \(W=\text{T}\):

$$P(W=\text{T}) \approx 0.449,\quad P(R \mid W) \approx 0.357,\quad P(S \mid W) \approx 0.646$$
$$P(R \cap S \mid W) \approx 0.00441 \quad\text{vs}\quad P(R \mid W)\,P(S \mid W) \approx 0.2308$$

The ratio is ≈ 0.019 — far from 1. Rain and Sprinkler are strongly dependent given that the grass is wet.

Explaining Away (Inter-causal Reasoning)

The most striking consequence: once we know \(W=\text{T}\), knowing the sprinkler was on lowers our belief in rain:

$$P(R=\text{T} \mid W=\text{T},\; S=\text{T}) \approx 0.007$$
$$P(R=\text{T} \mid W=\text{T},\; S=\text{F}) \approx 0.997$$

The sprinkler explains away the wet grass, dramatically reducing the posterior on rain. This is the quintessential example of how conditioning on a common effect creates dependence between its causes — the opposite of what conditioning on a common cause does (which induces independence).

Critical Distinction

Marginal independence does NOT imply conditional independence (and vice versa). These are separate, logically unrelated properties. Never assume one from the other without checking.

d-Separation, Naive Bayes & Key Takeaways
▾

d-Separation in Graphical Models

In a directed acyclic graph (DAG), the d-separation criterion provides a sound and complete algorithm for reading off conditional independencies from graph structure alone. Three elemental patterns govern information flow:

StructureUnconditionalConditioned on middle node
Chain: \(X \to Z \to Y\)X, Y dependent\(X \perp Y \mid Z\) ✓ (blocked)
Fork: \(X \leftarrow Z \to Y\)X, Y dependent (common cause)\(X \perp Y \mid Z\) ✓ (blocked)
Collider: \(X \to Z \leftarrow Y\)\(X \perp Y\) (blocked at collider)\(X \not\perp Y \mid Z\) — opens! (explaining away)

The sprinkler example is a collider: \(R \to W \leftarrow S\). Rain and Sprinkler are independent marginally, but conditioning on \(W\) opens the collider and creates dependence.

Naive Bayes Classifier

Naive Bayes exploits conditional independence aggressively. Given class label \(Y\), it assumes all features \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) are conditionally independent:

$$P(X_1, \ldots, X_n \mid Y) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(X_i \mid Y)$$

This lets the classifier decompose an intractable \(n\)-dimensional joint into \(n\) easy 1-D conditionals. The prediction rule becomes:

$$\hat{y} = \underset{y}{\operatorname{argmax}}\; P(Y=y) \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(X_i \mid Y=y)$$

The "naive" part is that features are almost never truly conditionally independent in real data — yet the classifier is surprisingly robust because even an incorrect independence assumption often still preserves the correct ranking of class posteriors.

Key Takeaways

1. Independence: \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)\) — the product rule holds.
2. Conditional independence: \(P(A \cap B \mid C) = P(A \mid C)P(B \mid C)\).
3. Conditioning on a common cause blocks information flow (fork/chain) → creates independence.
4. Conditioning on a common effect opens a collider → destroys independence (explaining away).
5. Naive Bayes, Bayesian networks, and HMMs all leverage conditional independence structure for tractable inference.

Code Trace — Verifying Independence with Python
▾

The following Python trace computes independence and explaining-away numerically, matching the values shown in the visualization above.

import numpy as np
# ── Priors ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────
P_R = 0.20 # P(Rain = True)
P_S_given = {True: 0.01, False: 0.40} # P(Sprinkler | Rain)
P_W = {(T,F):0.99,(T,T):0.80,(F,T):0.90,(F,F):0.001} # P(W|R,S)
# Note: keys are (Rain, Sprinkler)
# ── Build full joint P(R, S, W) ──────────────────────────────────
joint = {}
for r in [True, False]:
for s in [True, False]:
pr = P_R if r else 1 - P_R
ps = P_S_given[r] if s else 1 - P_S_given[r]
for w in [True, False]:
pw = P_W[(r,s)] if w else 1 - P_W[(r,s)]
joint[(r,s,w)] = pr * ps * pw # product rule
# ── Marginals ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
P_R_m = sum(joint[(True,s,w)] for s in [T,F] for w in [T,F]) # 0.200
P_S_m = sum(joint[(r,True,w)] for r in [T,F] for w in [T,F]) # 0.322
P_W_m = sum(joint[(r,s,True)] for r in [T,F] for s in [T,F]) # 0.449
# ── Test: are R and S independent? ────────────────────────────────
P_RS = sum(joint[(True,True,w)] for w in [T,F]) # 0.002
print(P_RS, P_R_m * P_S_m) # 0.002 vs 0.0644 → NOT independent
# ── Condition on W=True (explaining away) ─────────────────────────
P_R_given_W = sum(joint[(True,s,True)] for s in [T,F]) / P_W_m # 0.357
P_RS_given_W = joint[(True,True,True)] / P_W_m # 0.00441
print(P_RS_given_W, P_R_given_W * P_S_given_W) # NOT equal → dependent given W
# ── Explaining away magnitude ─────────────────────────────────────
P_R_W_Strue = joint[(True,True,True)] / (joint[(True,True,True)] + joint[(False,True,True)])
P_R_W_Sfalse = joint[(True,False,True)] / (joint[(True,False,True)] + joint[(False,False,True)])
print(P_R_W_Strue, P_R_W_Sfalse) # 0.007 vs 0.997 — dramatic!
Numbers at a Glance

P(R|W=T, S=T) ≈ 0.007    (sprinkler explains the wetness)
P(R|W=T, S=F) ≈ 0.997    (no sprinkler → must be rain!)
P(R|W=T) ≈ 0.357    (baseline, before observing S)
