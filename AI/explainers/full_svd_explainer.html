Full SVD: A = UΣVᵀ

Singular Value Decomposition — the universal factorization of any matrix
Foundational + Intermediate Linear Algebra Part I · Section 1.5 Must Know
1
Understanding SVD
▾

Every matrix — no matter its shape — secretly performs three simple operations in sequence: a rotation, a rescaling along perpendicular axes, and another rotation. SVD (Singular Value Decomposition) makes that secret explicit by factoring any \(m \times n\) real matrix \(A\) as

$$A = U \Sigma V^\top$$

where \(U\) (\(m \times m\)) and \(V\) (\(n \times n\)) are orthogonal matrices (pure rotations / reflections), and \(\Sigma\) (\(m \times n\)) is rectangular diagonal with non-negative entries \(\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \cdots \geq 0\) called singular values.

Real-World Analogy

Think of a camera lens deforming a circular ring of light onto a sensor. No matter how the lens distorts, the distortion can always be described as: first rotate the ring, then stretch it into an ellipse, then rotate again. The semi-axes of the ellipse are the singular values. SVD finds those three steps for any linear map.

Key Concept

Every linear map \(\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m\) maps the unit sphere \(S^{n-1}\) to a (possibly degenerate) ellipsoid in \(\mathbb{R}^m\). The singular values are the lengths of the ellipsoid's semi-axes; the columns of \(V\) point to the input directions that get mapped to those axes; the columns of \(U\) point in the output axis directions.

Unlike eigendecomposition — which exists only for square, diagonalizable matrices — SVD always exists for any real \(m \times n\) matrix. It is the workhorse behind PCA, pseudoinverse computation, low-rank approximation, image compression, recommendation systems, and numerical linear algebra solvers.

Full vs Economy SVD

Full SVD: \(U\) is \(m \times m\), \(\Sigma\) is \(m \times n\) (zero-padded rows/cols), \(V^\top\) is \(n \times n\). This preserves the complete column and row spaces.

Economy (thin) SVD: keep only the \(r = \operatorname{rank}(A)\) non-zero singular values. \(U_r\) is \(m \times r\), \(\Sigma_r\) is \(r \times r\), \(V_r^\top\) is \(r \times n\). Same product, smaller matrices.

2
How the Three Factors Work
▾

The three stages of A as a geometric pipeline

Apply \(A\) to a vector \(\mathbf{x}\): \(A\mathbf{x} = U\Sigma V^\top \mathbf{x}\). This is three sequential operations:

  • Stage 1 — \(V^\top\mathbf{x}\): rotate (or reflect) in \(\mathbb{R}^n\). Because \(V\) is orthogonal, \(\|V^\top \mathbf{x}\| = \|\mathbf{x}\|\) — lengths are preserved.
  • Stage 2 — \(\Sigma(V^\top\mathbf{x})\): scale coordinate \(i\) by \(\sigma_i\) and embed into \(\mathbb{R}^m\). This stretches the unit circle into an ellipse with semi-axes \(\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \ldots\).
  • Stage 3 — \(U(\cdots)\): rotate (or reflect) in \(\mathbb{R}^m\). Orthogonal — lengths preserved again.

Numerical trace for \(A = \begin{bmatrix}3&2\\2&3\\1&-1\end{bmatrix}\)

This \(3 \times 2\) matrix has full SVD:

$$U = \begin{bmatrix}-0.7071 & 0.4082 & -0.5774\\-0.7071 & -0.4082 & 0.5774\\0 & 0.8165 & 0.5774\end{bmatrix}, \quad \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix}5 & 0\\0 & \sqrt{3}\\0 & 0\end{bmatrix}, \quad V^\top = \begin{bmatrix}-0.7071 & -0.7071\\0.7071 & -0.7071\end{bmatrix}$$

Apply to \(\mathbf{x} = [1, 0]^\top\):

  • \(V^\top\mathbf{x} = [-0.7071,\; 0.7071]^\top\) — rotated 135°
  • \(\Sigma(V^\top\mathbf{x}) = [-3.536,\; 1.225,\; 0]^\top\) — scaled (σ₁=5, σ₂=√3)
  • \(U\,(\cdots) = [3.0, 2.0, 1.0]^\top\) — first column of A ✓
Common Confusion

The full-SVD \(\Sigma\) is not square — it is \(m \times n\). For a \(3 \times 2\) matrix, \(\Sigma\) is \(3 \times 2\) with a zero row. The rank equals the number of non-zero singular values, and \(\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq 0\) always.

Outer-Product Form

Equivalently: \(\displaystyle A = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i \,\mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^\top\), a sum of rank-1 matrices weighted by singular values. Truncating to the top \(k\) terms gives the best rank-\(k\) approximation (Eckart–Young theorem).

3
The Math Behind SVD
▾

Existence proof sketch

Consider the symmetric PSD matrix \(A^\top A\) (\(n \times n\)). By the spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices, it has a full orthogonal eigendecomposition:

$$A^\top A = V \Lambda V^\top, \quad \Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n), \quad \lambda_i \geq 0$$

Define \(\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}\). For each non-zero \(\sigma_i\), set \(\mathbf{u}_i = \frac{1}{\sigma_i} A \mathbf{v}_i\). Then:

$$A^\top A \mathbf{v}_i = \lambda_i \mathbf{v}_i \implies \|A\mathbf{v}_i\|^2 = \mathbf{v}_i^\top A^\top A \mathbf{v}_i = \sigma_i^2 \implies \mathbf{u}_i = \frac{A\mathbf{v}_i}{\sigma_i}$$

The \(\mathbf{u}_i\) are orthonormal: \(\mathbf{u}_i^\top \mathbf{u}_j = \frac{1}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}\mathbf{v}_i^\top A^\top A \mathbf{v}_j = \frac{\lambda_j}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}\delta_{ij} = \delta_{ij}\). Extend to a full orthonormal basis for \(\mathbb{R}^m\) to get the full \(U\). Then \(A = U\Sigma V^\top\) holds by construction.

Key properties (provable from the definition)

  • Spectral norm: \(\|A\|_2 = \sigma_1\) (the largest singular value)
  • Frobenius norm: \(\|A\|_F = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \cdots + \sigma_r^2}\)
  • Rank: \(\operatorname{rank}(A) = \) number of non-zero singular values
  • Condition number: \(\kappa(A) = \sigma_1 / \sigma_r\) (measures numerical sensitivity)
  • Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse: \(A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^\top\) where \(\Sigma^+\) replaces each non-zero \(\sigma_i\) by \(1/\sigma_i\)
$$\text{Eckart–Young: } \underset{\operatorname{rank}(B)=k}{\operatorname{argmin}} \|A - B\|_F = U_k \Sigma_k V_k^\top$$
Four Fundamental Subspaces via SVD

\(\operatorname{col}(A) = \operatorname{span}(\mathbf{u}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{u}_r)\) — left singular vectors for non-zero σ

\(\operatorname{null}(A^\top) = \operatorname{span}(\mathbf{u}_{r+1},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_m)\) — left singular vectors for zero σ

\(\operatorname{row}(A) = \operatorname{span}(\mathbf{v}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{v}_r)\) — right singular vectors for non-zero σ

\(\operatorname{null}(A) = \operatorname{span}(\mathbf{v}_{r+1},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_n)\) — right singular vectors for zero σ

From-Scratch Code (NumPy + scipy.linalg)
▾

Pure NumPy — via eigendecomposition of AᵀA

Computing SVD from scratch follows the existence proof: diagonalise \(A^\top A\), extract singular values, build \(U\) column by column.

import numpy as np
def svd_from_scratch(A):
"""Full SVD via eigendecomposition of AᵀA."""
m, n = A.shape
# Step 1: form AᵀA (n×n symmetric PSD)
AtA = A.T @ A
# Step 2: eigendecompose (eigh guarantees real, sorted ascending)
eigenvalues, V = np.linalg.eigh(AtA)
# Step 3: sort descending (eigh returns ascending)
idx = np.argsort(eigenvalues)[::-1]
eigenvalues, V = eigenvalues[idx], V[:, idx]
# Step 4: singular values σ_i = sqrt(λ_i), clip negatives from numerics
singular_vals = np.sqrt(np.maximum(eigenvalues, 0))
# Step 5: build U columns for non-zero σ_i
r = np.sum(singular_vals > 1e-10)
U = np.zeros((m, m))
for i in range(r):
U[:, i] = (A @ V[:, i]) / singular_vals[i]
# Step 6: extend U to full orthonormal basis (Gram-Schmidt on nullspace)
if r < m:
Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(np.hstack([U[:, :r], np.eye(m)]))
U = Q[:, :m]
# Step 7: build full Σ (m×n)
Sigma = np.zeros((m, n))
Sigma[:r, :r] = np.diag(singular_vals[:r])
return U, Sigma, V.T
# Verify on our example
A = np.array([[3.,2.],[2.,3.],[1.,-1.]])
U, S, Vt = svd_from_scratch(A)
print("Reconstructed:", (U @ S @ Vt).round(4)) # should equal A

Production usage — numpy.linalg.svd

import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import svd as scipy_svd
A = np.array([[3.,2.],[2.,3.],[1.,-1.]])
# Full SVD (numpy) — U:(3,3), s:(2,), Vh:(2,2)
U, s, Vh = np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=True)
print("σ₁=", s[0], " σ₂=", s[1]) # 5.0 1.7321
# Economy SVD — only keep r = rank non-zero columns of U
U_r, s_r, Vh_r = np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)
# U_r:(3,2), s_r:(2,), Vh_r:(2,2)
# Low-rank approximation (best rank-1)
A_rank1 = s[0] * U[:, 0:1] @ Vh[0:1, :]
print("||A - A_rank1||_F =", np.linalg.norm(A - A_rank1).round(4)) # σ₂ = 1.7321
# Pseudoinverse via SVD
S_plus = np.zeros_like(A).T # (2,3)
S_plus[:len(s), :len(s)] = np.diag(1./s) # invert diagonal
A_plus = Vh.T @ S_plus @ U.T # Moore-Penrose
print("A+ @ A ≈ I?", (A_plus @ A).round(4)) # should be 2×2 identity
Gotcha — full_matrices=True vs False

np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=True) returns s as a 1-D array of length \(\min(m,n)\). You must build \(\Sigma\) manually. np.diag(s) gives \(\min(m,n) \times \min(m,n)\) — not the full \(m \times n\) shape. Use np.zeros((m,n)); Sigma[:len(s),:len(s)] = np.diag(s) instead.

Key Takeaways & Applications
▾
  • Universality: SVD exists for every real (or complex) matrix — no square or full-rank requirement.
  • Geometry first: \(V^\top\) rotates inputs, \(\Sigma\) scales along principal axes, \(U\) rotates outputs. The singular values are the axis lengths of the image ellipsoid.
  • Rank & nullspace: \(r\) non-zero singular values → \(\operatorname{rank}(A) = r\); the \((n{-}r)\) last columns of \(V\) span \(\operatorname{null}(A)\).
  • Best low-rank: Eckart–Young theorem — truncating at \(k\) singular values gives the best rank-\(k\) approximation in both \(\|\cdot\|_F\) and \(\|\cdot\|_2\).
  • Pseudoinverse: \(A^+ = V \Sigma^+ U^\top\) — flipping the map by inverting the non-zero diagonal entries.

Where you see SVD in ML

  • PCA — eigendecomposition of the sample covariance equals SVD of the centred data matrix
  • Collaborative filtering / Matrix Factorization — truncated SVD as a latent factor model
  • Least squares — \(\hat{x} = A^+ b\) via SVD when \(A\) is overdetermined
  • Image compression — store only top-\(k\) terms of the outer-product sum
  • Word embeddings (LSA) — SVD on word–document co-occurrence matrices
  • LoRA fine-tuning — SVD-style low-rank decomposition of weight updates
Interview Priority: Must Know

SVD is directly asked in FAANG ML/quant interviews. Expect questions on: how to prove existence, relationship to eigenvalues of \(A^\top A\), Eckart–Young, condition number, and computing the pseudoinverse. Know the full vs economy distinction and the outer-product form cold.
