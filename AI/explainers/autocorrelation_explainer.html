Autocorrelation (ACF) & Partial Autocorrelation (PACF)

Measuring direct and indirect dependencies in time series data
Foundational Time Series Stats O(N·Lags)
1
Understanding ACF & PACF
▼

What are they? Autocorrelation Function (ACF) and Partial Autocorrelation Function (PACF) are statistical tools used to identify patterns of dependence in time series data. They measure how much a current observation \(X_t\) is correlated with past observations \(X_{t-k}\).

  • ACF measures the linear correlation between \(X_t\) and \(X_{t-k}\) for various lags \(k\). This includes both direct and indirect dependencies. For example, if \(X_t\) is correlated with \(X_{t-1}\), and \(X_{t-1}\) is correlated with \(X_{t-2}\), then \(X_t\) will appear correlated with \(X_{t-2}\) just by virtue of the "chain reaction" through \(X_{t-1}\).
  • PACF measures the direct linear correlation between \(X_t\) and \(X_{t-k}\), after removing the influence of the intermediate observations \(X_{t-1}, X_{t-2}, \dots, X_{t-k+1}\). This helps isolate the "pure" relationship at each lag.

Analogy: Imagine a game of "telephone" with \(k\) players. ACF is like asking: "How similar is player 1's initial message to what player \(k+1\) hears?" This includes all the distortions and relays through players 2 to \(k\). PACF is like asking: "If players 2 to \(k\) were perfect, lossless relays of player 1's message, how much *additional* direct influence does player 1's initial message have on player \(k+1\)?"

💡 If you remember one thing

ACF (Autocorrelation Function) reveals the total dependence between a time series and its past values. PACF (Partial Autocorrelation Function) reveals the direct dependence, stripping away any indirect effects from intermediate lags. Together, they are crucial for identifying the order of ARIMA models.

How ACF & PACF Work Internally
▼

Calculating Autocorrelation (ACF)

For a given lag \(k\), the autocorrelation \(\rho_k\) is simply the Pearson correlation coefficient between the time series \(X_t\) and its lagged version \(X_{t-k}\). It's computed as the covariance between the series and its lagged self, divided by the variance of the series:

If a time series is stationary (constant mean and variance over time), its autocorrelation depends only on the lag \(k\), not on \(t\).

Calculating Partial Autocorrelation (PACF)

PACF is more complex. The partial autocorrelation at lag \(k\), denoted \(\phi_{kk}\), is the correlation between \(X_t\) and \(X_{t-k}\) that is not explained by the intermediate lags \(X_{t-1}, \dots, X_{t-k+1}\). Conceptually, it's like performing a linear regression of \(X_t\) on \(X_{t-1}, \dots, X_{t-k+1}\) and another regression of \(X_{t-k}\) on \(X_{t-1}, \dots, X_{t-k+1}\). The PACF at lag \(k\) is then the correlation between the residuals of these two regressions.

More formally, for a stationary process, \(\phi_{kk}\) is the last coefficient in an autoregressive (AR) model of order \(k\). That is, if we fit an AR(\(k\)) model to the data:

$$X_t = \phi_1 X_{t-1} + \phi_2 X_{t-2} + \dots + \phi_k X_{t-k} + \epsilon_t$$

then \(\phi_{kk}\) is the coefficient \(\phi_k\).

Design Decision: Why Use Both?

ACF and PACF provide complementary information. ACF shows all dependencies, which can be useful for identifying seasonal patterns or overall persistence. However, the indirect effects can obscure direct relationships. PACF "cleans up" the signal by isolating direct dependencies, making it easier to determine the true order of an autoregressive process (AR) or a moving average process (MA).

🔑 Key Property

For an AR(\(p\)) process, the PACF "cuts off" (becomes zero or near-zero) after lag \(p\), while the ACF decays exponentially or sinusoidally. For an MA(\(q\)) process, the ACF "cuts off" after lag \(q\), while the PACF decays exponentially or sinusoidally. This contrasting behavior is the primary heuristic for identifying appropriate AR and MA orders for ARIMA models.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Autocorrelation Function (ACF)

For a stationary time series \(X_t\) with mean \(\mu\), the autocorrelation at lag \(k\) is defined as:

$$ \rho_k = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X_t, X_{t-k})}{\operatorname{Var}(X_t)} = \frac{E[(X_t - \mu)(X_{t-k} - \mu)]}{E[(X_t - \mu)^2]} $$

In practice, for a sample of \(N\) observations, we estimate \(\mu\) with the sample mean \(\bar{X}\), and the covariance/variance terms with sample estimates:

$$ \hat{\rho}_k = \frac{\sum_{t=k+1}^N (X_t - \bar{X})(X_{t-k} - \bar{X})}{\sum_{t=1}^N (X_t - \bar{X})^2} $$

This formula gives a value between -1 and 1. The autocorrelation at lag 0 is always 1 (\(\rho_0 = 1\)) as a series is perfectly correlated with itself.

Partial Autocorrelation Function (PACF)

The PACF at lag \(k\), \(\phi_{kk}\), is the correlation between \(X_t\) and \(X_{t-k}\) that is not accounted for by the linear dependence on \(X_{t-1}, \dots, X_{t-k+1}\). For a stationary process, this can be found by solving the Yule-Walker equations for an AR(\(k\)) process:

$$ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho_1 & \dots & \rho_{k-1} \\ \rho_1 & 1 & \dots & \rho_{k-2} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \rho_{k-1} & \rho_{k-2} & \dots & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \phi_{k1} \\ \phi_{k2} \\ \vdots \\ \phi_{kk} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \rho_1 \\ \rho_2 \\ \vdots \\ \rho_k \end{pmatrix} $$

Here, \(\rho_j\) are the autocorrelations at lag \(j\). The solution vector \(\begin{pmatrix} \phi_{k1} & \dots & \phi_{kk} \end{pmatrix}^\top\) contains the coefficients of the AR(\(k\)) model, and \(\phi_{kk}\) is the partial autocorrelation at lag \(k\). This recursive definition (Durbin-Levinson algorithm) is often used for efficient computation.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

ACF and PACF plots are only interpretable for stationary time series. A non-stationary series (e.g., one with a trend or seasonality) will often exhibit slowly decaying ACF values, which can be misleading. Differencing is typically required to achieve stationarity before analyzing ACF/PACF for model selection.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use ACF and PACF:

  • ARIMA Model Order Selection: The most common application. ACF and PACF patterns directly suggest the order of the autoregressive (p) and moving average (q) components of an ARIMA(p,d,q) model.
  • Identifying Seasonality: Peaks at seasonal lags (e.g., lag 12 for monthly data) in the ACF plot indicate seasonality.
  • Understanding Data Persistence: Slowly decaying ACF in a non-stationary series (like a random walk) suggests the need for differencing.
  • Detecting White Noise: If both ACF and PACF are non-significant at all lags (within confidence intervals), the series is white noise, meaning no linear dependence on past values.

When NOT to use or when to be cautious:

  • Non-stationary Data: As highlighted, raw ACF/PACF plots of non-stationary series can be misleading. Always check for stationarity (e.g., with a Dickey-Fuller test) and apply differencing if necessary.
  • Non-linear Relationships: ACF and PACF only capture linear dependencies. Complex non-linear patterns might not be revealed by these tools.
  • Small Sample Sizes: Estimates of ACF/PACF can be unreliable with very short time series.
💡 Big Picture

ACF and PACF are fundamental diagnostic tools in classical time series analysis. They provide a "fingerprint" for different underlying data generation processes, guiding the selection of appropriate parametric models like ARMA and ARIMA by matching empirical patterns to theoretical expectations.

Code Trace (Python - Statsmodels)
▼
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from statsmodels.tsa.arima_process import ArmaProcess
from statsmodels.graphics.tsaplots import plot_acf, plot_pacf
# 1. Generate an AR(1) time series
ar_param = np.array([1, -0.7]) # [1, -phi1] for X_t = phi1*X_{t-1} + epsilon_t
ma_param = np.array([1]) # No MA component
ar_process = ArmaProcess(ar_param, ma_param)
np.random.seed(42)
series = ar_process.generate_sample(nsample=200)
# 2. Plot the time series
plt.figure(figsize=(12, 4))
plt.plot(series)
plt.title('Simulated AR(1) Time Series')
plt.show()
# 3. Plot ACF
fig_acf = plot_acf(series, lags=20, alpha=0.05) # alpha for 95% CI
fig_acf.suptitle('Autocorrelation Function (ACF)')
plt.show()
# 4. Plot PACF
fig_pacf = plot_pacf(series, lags=20, alpha=0.05, method='ywm') # ywm for Yule-Walker
fig_pacf.suptitle('Partial Autocorrelation Function (PACF)')
plt.show()
