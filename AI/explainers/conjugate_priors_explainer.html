Understanding Conjugate Priors
▼

In Bayesian inference, you start with a prior belief about a parameter \(\theta\), observe data, and compute a posterior — your updated belief. The posterior is proportional to the prior times the likelihood:

$$p(\theta \mid x) \;\propto\; p(x \mid \theta)\; p(\theta)$$

Usually, computing this posterior requires numerical integration. A conjugate prior is a special choice of prior that makes the posterior analytically tractable — the posterior is in the same distributional family as the prior, just with updated parameters.

Key Concept: Conjugacy

A prior family \(\mathcal{F}\) is conjugate to a likelihood \(p(x \mid \theta)\) if, for any prior \(p(\theta) \in \mathcal{F}\) and any observed data \(x\), the posterior \(p(\theta \mid x) \in \mathcal{F}\). Only the hyperparameters change — the family is preserved.

The canonical example: If you believe a coin's bias \(\theta\) follows a Beta distribution, and you observe coin flips (Bernoulli trials), then your posterior is also Beta — just with updated parameters \(\alpha' = \alpha + \text{heads}\), \(\beta' = \beta + \text{tails}\). You can keep updating sequentially without ever integrating.

Insight: Why This Matters

Conjugacy turns Bayesian updating from a potentially intractable integral into a simple parameter arithmetic: observe data, add counts. This is why conjugate priors were historically dominant before MCMC and variational inference made non-conjugate models tractable.

How Conjugate Updating Works Internally
▼

Beta-Binomial: A Complete Walkthrough

The Beta distribution parameterised by \((\alpha, \beta)\) has PDF:

$$\text{Beta}(\theta;\alpha,\beta) = \frac{\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}}{B(\alpha,\beta)}, \quad \theta \in (0,1)$$

where \(B(\alpha,\beta) = \Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)/\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)\) is the Beta function (normalising constant). The mean is \(\mu = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\) and the mode is \(\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha+\beta-2}\) for \(\alpha,\beta>1\).

Suppose we observe \(k\) successes in \(n\) Bernoulli trials. The likelihood is:

$$p(k \mid \theta, n) = \binom{n}{k}\theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k}$$

Multiplying prior × likelihood and absorbing constants into the normaliser:

$$p(\theta \mid k) \;\propto\; \theta^{(\alpha+k)-1}(1-\theta)^{(\beta+n-k)-1} \;=\; \text{Beta}(\alpha+k,\; \beta+n-k)$$
The Update Rule

Prior \(\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)\) + \(k\) successes in \(n\) trials → Posterior \(\text{Beta}(\alpha+k,\; \beta+n-k)\).
Hyperparameters act as pseudo-counts: \(\alpha\) = prior successes, \(\beta\) = prior failures.

Normal-Normal (known variance)

Prior: \(\mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \tau^2)\). Observe \(n\) iid samples \(x_1,\ldots,x_n \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\) with known \(\sigma^2\). Working in precision \(\lambda = 1/\sigma^2\), the posterior is:

$$\mu \mid \mathbf{x} \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu_n,\; \tau_n^2\right), \quad \frac{1}{\tau_n^2} = \frac{1}{\tau^2} + \frac{n}{\sigma^2}, \quad \mu_n = \tau_n^2\!\left(\frac{\mu_0}{\tau^2} + \frac{n\bar{x}}{\sigma^2}\right)$$

This is precision-weighted averaging: the posterior mean is a weighted combination of the prior mean and the MLE \(\bar{x}\), where weights are proportional to their respective precisions.

Gamma-Poisson

Prior: \(\lambda \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta)\) (rate parameterisation). Observe counts \(x_1,\ldots,x_n \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda)\). The posterior is \(\lambda \mid \mathbf{x} \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha + \sum x_i,\; \beta + n)\).

Sequential Updating Property

Because the posterior is in the same family as the prior, the posterior becomes the new prior. Process data batch-by-batch: \(\text{Beta}(2,5) \xrightarrow{3\,\text{heads}} \text{Beta}(5,7) \xrightarrow{4\,\text{heads},1\,\text{tail}} \text{Beta}(9,8)\). The final result is identical regardless of batch order.

Why It Works — The Exponential Family Connection
▼

Conjugacy is not a coincidence — it emerges from the structure of the exponential family. A distribution belongs to the exponential family if its PDF can be written as:

$$p(x \mid \boldsymbol\eta) = h(x)\exp\!\bigl(\boldsymbol\eta^\top T(x) - A(\boldsymbol\eta)\bigr)$$

where \(\boldsymbol\eta\) are natural parameters, \(T(x)\) is the sufficient statistic, \(A(\boldsymbol\eta)\) is the log-partition function, and \(h(x)\) is the base measure. Most common distributions (Bernoulli, Poisson, Normal, Gamma, Dirichlet, Beta) belong to this family.

Theorem: Every Exponential Family Has a Conjugate Prior

For a likelihood in the exponential family with sufficient statistic \(T(x)\) and natural parameter \(\boldsymbol\eta\), the conjugate prior is: $$p(\boldsymbol\eta \mid \boldsymbol\chi, \nu) \;\propto\; \exp\!\bigl(\boldsymbol\eta^\top\boldsymbol\chi - \nu A(\boldsymbol\eta)\bigr)$$ After observing \(n\) data points with \(\sum T(x_i)\) sufficient statistic, the posterior has hyperparameters \(\boldsymbol\chi' = \boldsymbol\chi + \sum T(x_i)\) and \(\nu' = \nu + n\).

For the Bernoulli: \(T(x) = x\) (count of 1s), \(\eta = \log\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}\) (log-odds). The conjugate prior in terms of \(\theta\) is the Beta distribution. The update \(\alpha' = \alpha + k\) is exactly \(\chi' = \chi + \sum x_i\).

Warning: Conjugate Priors vs. Uninformative Priors

Conjugacy is a mathematical convenience, not a statement about your actual beliefs. The Jeffreys prior (Beta(0.5, 0.5) for Bernoulli) is the principled non-informative prior, not Beta(1,1). Using a conjugate prior solely for convenience can encode unintended beliefs.

Sufficient Statistics — The Key Insight

The posterior update only requires \(\sum T(x_i)\), not the individual data points. For Binomial, \(T(x_1,\ldots,x_n) = \sum x_i = k\) — only the total count matters, not which trials were heads. This is the Fisher-Neyman factorisation theorem in action: sufficient statistics capture all the information about \(\theta\) in the data.

$$\frac{p(\boldsymbol\eta \mid x_1,\ldots,x_n)}{p(\boldsymbol\eta)} = \frac{\exp(\boldsymbol\eta^\top \textstyle\sum T(x_i))}{\exp(n A(\boldsymbol\eta))} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boldsymbol\chi_{\text{post}} = \boldsymbol\chi_{\text{prior}} + \textstyle\sum T(x_i)$$

Key Takeaways & When to Use
▼

When to Use Conjugate Priors

  • Real-time / streaming Bayesian updates (e.g., online A/B testing, bandit algorithms)
  • Models with exponential family likelihoods where you can encode domain knowledge as pseudo-counts
  • Hierarchical models at inner levels (conjugate conditionals enable Gibbs sampling)
  • When interpretability matters: posterior hyperparameters have direct pseudo-count meaning

When Not to Use Conjugate Priors

  • Your actual prior beliefs don't fit the conjugate family (MCMC or VI instead)
  • Non-exponential-family likelihoods (no closed-form conjugate exists)
  • Multimodal posteriors (conjugate priors always give unimodal posteriors)
The Big Picture

Conjugate priors sit at the intersection of computational convenience and Bayesian philosophy. They show that Bayesian updating is fundamentally about accumulating sufficient statistics — a connection that runs deep into information geometry and natural gradient descent. Modern probabilistic programming (Stan, PyMC, Pyro) handles non-conjugate models, but understanding conjugacy builds the intuition for all of Bayesian ML.
