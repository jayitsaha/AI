Law of Large Numbers (Weak & Strong)

Part I · Section 3: Probability & Statistics · 3.5 Limit Theorems
Foundational Probability Theory Statistics Must Know
1
The 30-Second Version
▼

What is this?

Flip a fair coin 10 times — you might get 3 heads or 7 heads. That's just randomness. Flip it 10,000 times and you'll get very close to 5,000 heads. The Law of Large Numbers (LLN) is the mathematical guarantee behind that everyday observation: as you take more and more independent samples, their average stabilises and converges to the true expected value.

Real-World Analogy

Imagine you're a casino owner. Any single gambler might win big or lose big tonight — that's unpredictable. But with thousands of players over months, your house edge (the theoretical mean) becomes your reliable profit margin. The casino doesn't gamble; it relies on the LLN. Insurance companies do the same: individual claims are uncertain, but average claim rates across millions of policies are stable and predictable.

💡 If You Remember One Thing

The sample mean \(\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\) converges to the population mean \(\mu = \mathbb{E}[X]\) as \(n \to \infty\). Weak LLN: convergence in probability. Strong LLN: almost sure convergence (stronger guarantee, same conclusion).

One-Sentence Summary

The LLN formalises the intuition that "averages become reliable with enough data" — the foundation of statistics, Monte Carlo methods, and scientific measurement.

Historical Context & Motivation
▼

The Problem

Before the 17th century, probability was purely philosophical — there was no mathematical justification for why gambling houses consistently profited, or why repeated measurements in physics averaged out to something "true." Gamblers and scientists knew empirically that larger samples behaved more predictably, but nobody could prove it.

Core Concepts & The Math
▼

Setup

Let \(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n\) be i.i.d. (independent, identically distributed) random variables with mean \(\mu = \mathbb{E}[X_i]\). Define the sample mean:

$$\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i$$

Weak LLN (Chebyshev Proof)

Assume additionally \(\operatorname{Var}(X_i) = \sigma^2 < \infty\). By linearity of expectation:

$$\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}_n] = \mu, \quad \operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$$

Applying Chebyshev's inequality \(P(|Y - \mathbb{E}[Y]| \geq \varepsilon) \leq \frac{\operatorname{Var}(Y)}{\varepsilon^2}\):

$$P\!\left(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| \geq \varepsilon\right) \leq \frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2} \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} 0$$

This is convergence in probability: \(\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{P} \mu\).

Strong LLN (Kolmogorov, 1933)

Under the weaker assumption that only \(\mathbb{E}[|X_i|] < \infty\) (finite mean, no variance required):

$$P\!\left(\lim_{n \to \infty} \bar{X}_n = \mu\right) = 1$$

This is almost sure convergence: for almost every possible sequence of outcomes (all except a set of probability zero), the running average converges to \(\mu\).

⚠️ Common Misconception

The LLN is not the "Gambler's Fallacy." If a fair coin lands heads 10 times in a row, the 11th flip is still 50/50 — past results don't change future probabilities. The LLN converges by dilution: those 10 unlucky heads get drowned out by future flips, not corrected.

🔑 Key Invariant

The variance of the sample mean shrinks as \(\sigma^2/n\). This is the mechanism behind LLN convergence: each new sample halves the variance contribution at the rate \(1/n\), pulling the average ever closer to \(\mu\).

Monte Carlo Connection

Monte Carlo estimation relies entirely on the LLN. To estimate \(\mathbb{E}[f(X)]\), draw \(n\) i.i.d. samples and compute \(\frac{1}{n}\sum f(X_i)\). The LLN guarantees this converges to the true expectation. Example: estimating \(\pi\) by checking if random points fall inside a unit circle. With \(n = 500\) samples, the estimate converges to \(\approx 3.14\).

Interview Deep-Dive
▼

Q1 (Easy) — What does the LLN say?

A: The sample mean \(\bar{X}_n\) converges to the population mean \(\mu\) as \(n \to \infty\). The Weak LLN says convergence in probability; the Strong LLN says almost sure convergence.

Q2 (Easy) — What's the practical implication?

A: Larger datasets give more reliable estimates. Monte Carlo methods, insurance pricing, A/B testing — all work because the LLN ensures sample averages estimate population averages accurately.

Q3 (Medium) — What's the difference between WLLN and SLLN?

A: WLLN (convergence in probability): for any \(\varepsilon > 0\), \(P(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| > \varepsilon) \to 0\). This leaves open the possibility that a specific sequence diverges — just with vanishing probability. SLLN (almost sure): the probability of the full sequence converging is 1. SLLN is strictly stronger. The SLLN requires only finite mean; the standard WLLN proof (via Chebyshev) also needs finite variance.

Q4 (Medium) — When does the LLN fail?

A: When \(\mathbb{E}[|X|] = \infty\) (infinite mean). The canonical example is the Cauchy distribution — it has no defined mean, and the sample mean of i.i.d. Cauchy variables does not converge. Also fails if samples are not i.i.d. (e.g., strongly correlated).

🔴 Follow-up Trap

"Does LLN imply CLT?" No — they're separate theorems. LLN says where the mean goes (\(\mu\)). CLT says how fast and with what distribution the fluctuations scale (\(\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\)). CLT needs finite variance; LLN (strong) only needs finite mean.

Q5 (Hard) — How does the LLN underlie Monte Carlo integration?

A: To compute \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\), rewrite as \((b-a)\,\mathbb{E}[f(U)]\) where \(U \sim \text{Uniform}(a,b)\). Draw \(n\) i.i.d. samples \(U_1, \ldots, U_n\) and estimate \(\frac{b-a}{n}\sum f(U_i)\). By SLLN, this converges a.s. to the true integral. Error scales as \(O(1/\sqrt{n})\) by CLT — dimension-independent, which makes Monte Carlo uniquely powerful in high dimensions.

Explain to a PhD Researcher

The SLLN in its Kolmogorov form holds iff \(\mathbb{E}[|X|] < \infty\); the proof uses the Borel–Cantelli lemma to show \(\sum P(|X_n| > n\varepsilon) < \infty\) implies the tail events occur only finitely often. The WLLN admits weaker conditions: e.g., pairwise independence (not full independence) suffices if \(\operatorname{Var}(X_i) < \infty\) uniformly. The distinction matters for ergodic theory — the ergodic theorem is an LLN where time averages replace ensemble averages.

System Design Angle

In ML: mini-batch gradient estimates are unbiased estimators of the full-gradient by LLN. Larger batch sizes reduce gradient variance at rate \(\sigma^2/B\). The LLN justifies why stochastic gradient descent converges — each mini-batch gradient is a noisy but unbiased estimate. A/B testing sample size calculations directly invoke LLN to bound \(P(|\hat{\mu} - \mu| > \varepsilon)\).

Key Takeaways & Related Topics
▼
✅ Core Takeaways

1. Sample mean converges to population mean as \(n \to \infty\) — this is the foundational guarantee of statistics.
2. Weak LLN (convergence in probability) requires finite variance; Strong LLN (a.s.) only needs finite mean.
3. The mechanism: \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \sigma^2/n \to 0\) makes large deviations increasingly improbable.
4. Monte Carlo, SGD, A/B testing, insurance — all rest on this theorem.
