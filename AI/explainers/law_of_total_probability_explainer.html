Law of Total Probability

Marginalizing over a partition — the bridge from conditional to unconditional probability
Foundational Lean Intermediate Probability & Statistics Math Foundations Core Theorem
1
Understanding the Concept
▼

What is this? Sometimes you can't compute the probability of an event \(A\) directly, but you can compute it in each scenario of a complete list of scenarios. The Law of Total Probability says: add up the probability of \(A\) in each scenario, weighted by how likely that scenario is. That sum gives you the overall probability of \(A\).

Analogy — The Email Inbox: Imagine your inbox receives three kinds of emails: work emails (50% of all email), newsletters (30%), and unknown-sender emails (20%). You know the spam rate is different in each type: 5% of work emails are spam, 40% of newsletters are spam, and 80% of unknown-sender emails are spam. To find the overall spam rate you can't just average 5%, 40%, 80% — you need to weight each by how common that type is. The Law of Total Probability gives you exactly this weighted average:

$$P(\text{spam}) = 0.05 \times 0.50 + 0.40 \times 0.30 + 0.80 \times 0.20 = 0.3050$$

So 30.5% of all emails are spam — not the naive average of 41.7%.

💡 If you remember one thing

To find \(P(A)\) when you know it only in parts: split the world into non-overlapping scenarios \(B_1, \ldots, B_n\) that cover everything, then sum \(P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^n P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i)\). This is marginalization — integrating out the hidden variable \(B\).

2
How It Works — Partitions & Marginalization
▼

Step 1 — Build a Partition of the Sample Space

A partition \(\{B_1, B_2, \ldots, B_n\}\) of sample space \(\Omega\) satisfies two conditions simultaneously:

  • Mutually exclusive: \(B_i \cap B_j = \emptyset\) for all \(i \neq j\) — no overlap.
  • Exhaustive: \(\displaystyle\bigcup_{i=1}^n B_i = \Omega\) — the union covers everything.

Consequence: the events \(A \cap B_1,\; A \cap B_2,\; \ldots,\; A \cap B_n\) are also mutually exclusive and their union equals \(A\) exactly. This lets us break \(A\) apart cleanly.

Step 2 — Decompose Event A

Since the \(A \cap B_i\) are disjoint:

$$A = (A \cap B_1) \cup (A \cap B_2) \cup \cdots \cup (A \cap B_n)$$

By the additivity axiom of probability:

$$P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^n P(A \cap B_i)$$

Step 3 — Apply the Multiplication Rule

For any two events, the multiplication rule states \(P(A \cap B_i) = P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i)\) (assuming \(P(B_i) > 0\)). Substituting:

$$\boxed{P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i)}$$

This is the Law of Total Probability. It converts conditional probabilities (easier to assess or measure) into the unconditional probability of \(A\).

Information Flow

  • Input: A partition \(\{B_i\}\) and conditional probabilities \(P(A \mid B_i)\).
  • Computation: Weight each conditional by \(P(B_i)\), the prior probability of that scenario.
  • Output: \(P(A)\) — the marginal probability of \(A\), with the scenario variable \(B\) "integrated out."
🔑 Key Property — Marginalization

The Law of Total Probability is the discrete analogue of marginalizing a joint distribution: \(P(A) = \sum_i P(A, B_i)\). In the continuous case it becomes \(p(x) = \int p(x \mid z)\,p(z)\,dz\). This identity underlies Bayesian inference, mixture models, and the evidence lower bound (ELBO).

⚠️ Common Mistake — Non-Partition

The formula only holds when \(\{B_i\}\) is a true partition: mutually exclusive and exhaustive. Using overlapping or incomplete sets produces double-counting or an undercount. Always verify \(\sum_i P(B_i) = 1\) before applying.

3
The Math — Full Derivation
▼

Formal Statement

Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)\) be a probability space. Let \(\{B_1, B_2, \ldots, B_n\}\) be a finite partition of \(\Omega\) with \(P(B_i) > 0\) for all \(i\). Then for any event \(A \in \mathcal{F}\):

$$P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i)$$

Proof

Step 1. Since \(\{B_i\}\) is a partition, the sets \(\{A \cap B_i\}\) are pairwise disjoint:

$$(A \cap B_i) \cap (A \cap B_j) = A \cap (B_i \cap B_j) = A \cap \emptyset = \emptyset \quad (i \neq j)$$

Step 2. Their union recovers \(A\):

$$\bigcup_{i=1}^n (A \cap B_i) = A \cap \bigcup_{i=1}^n B_i = A \cap \Omega = A$$

Step 3. By countable additivity (\(\sigma\)-additivity) of \(P\):

$$P(A) = P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^n (A \cap B_i)\right) = \sum_{i=1}^n P(A \cap B_i)$$

Step 4. Apply the definition of conditional probability \(P(A \cap B_i) = P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i)\):

$$P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i) \qquad \blacksquare$$

Extension: Continuous Case

For a continuous latent variable \(Z\) with density \(p(z)\):

$$p(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} p(x \mid z)\,p(z)\,dz$$

This is the marginal density — the partition becomes an uncountable continuum and the sum becomes an integral. The structure is identical.

Numerical Trace

Email spam example: \(B_1\) = Work (\(P=0.50\)), \(B_2\) = Newsletter (\(P=0.30\)), \(B_3\) = Unknown (\(P=0.20\)).

$$P(A) = \underbrace{0.05 \times 0.50}_{0.0250} + \underbrace{0.40 \times 0.30}_{0.1200} + \underbrace{0.80 \times 0.20}_{0.1600} = 0.3050$$

Partition check: \(0.50 + 0.30 + 0.20 = 1.00\). ✓

Connection to Bayes' Theorem

\(P(A)\) computed here is exactly the normalizing constant (evidence) in Bayes' rule:

$$P(B_i \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i)}{P(A)} = \frac{P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i)}{\sum_j P(A \mid B_j)\,P(B_j)}$$

Without the Law of Total Probability, Bayes' theorem cannot be normalized. Every Bayesian update implicitly computes a total-probability sum.

📐 Formula Summary

Discrete: \(P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^n P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i)\)
Continuous: \(p(x) = \int p(x \mid z)\,p(z)\,dz\)
Joint-marginal identity: \(P(A) = \sum_i P(A, B_i)\)

ML Connections — Mixture Models & Marginal Likelihood
▼

Mixture Models

A Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) defines a joint density over data \(x\) and latent component \(z \in \{1,\ldots,K\}\):

$$p(x) = \sum_{k=1}^K \underbrace{\pi_k}_{P(z=k)} \cdot \underbrace{\mathcal{N}(x;\,\mu_k,\,\Sigma_k)}_{P(x \mid z=k)}$$

This is verbatim the Law of Total Probability: the mixture weights \(\pi_k\) form the partition prior (\(\sum_k \pi_k = 1\)), and the Gaussian components are the conditional densities. The E-step of EM computes the posterior \(P(z=k \mid x)\) using Bayes' rule — which requires this marginal \(p(x)\) as the denominator.

Marginal Likelihood (Evidence)

In Bayesian model comparison, the marginal likelihood of data \(\mathcal{D}\) given model \(\mathcal{M}\) is:

$$p(\mathcal{D} \mid \mathcal{M}) = \int p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta, \mathcal{M})\,p(\theta \mid \mathcal{M})\,d\theta$$

Here the continuous partition is the parameter space. This integral is typically intractable, which motivates MCMC, variational inference, and the ELBO:

$$\ln p(\mathcal{D}) \geq \mathbb{E}_{q(\theta)}\!\left[\ln p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)\right] - D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q(\theta) \,\|\, p(\theta)\right)$$

Naive Bayes Classifier

In Naive Bayes, a document's class probability uses the Law of Total Probability to compute the prior predictive \(P(\text{doc})\). The classifier picks \(\hat{y} = \arg\max_y P(y)\prod_j P(x_j \mid y)\), where \(P(y)\) and \(P(x_j \mid y)\) are estimated from training data — a direct application of the partition-based decomposition.

💡 Unifying View

Whenever you see a sum (or integral) of the form \(\sum_z p(\cdot \mid z)\,p(z)\), you are looking at the Law of Total Probability. It shows up as: marginal likelihood, mixture model density, forward algorithm in HMMs, predictive posterior in Bayesian regression, and the normalizing constant in any Bayes update.

Key Takeaways
▼
  • Core identity: \(P(A) = \sum_i P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i)\) — a weighted average of conditional probabilities, weighted by partition priors.
  • Requires a partition: \(\{B_i\}\) must be mutually exclusive and exhaustive. Check \(\sum_i P(B_i)=1\).
  • Enables marginalization: "Summing out" a latent variable \(B\) from the joint \(P(A,B)\) to get the marginal \(P(A)\).
  • Foundation for Bayes: The denominator \(P(A)\) in Bayes' rule is computed by the Law of Total Probability.
  • ML appearances: Mixture model density, EM algorithm E-step, marginal likelihood, HMM forward pass, variational ELBO, Naive Bayes normalizer.

🔑 Decision Guide

Use the exact discrete sum when the partition is finite and small. Use Monte Carlo integration when the continuous integral is too complex to derive analytically. Use variational inference or Laplace approximation in high-dimensional probabilistic models.
