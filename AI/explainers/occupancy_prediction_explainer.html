Is this voxel occupied — and by what? A shape-agnostic alternative to 3D bounding boxes for driving perception

Understanding Occupancy Prediction
▼

What is it? Instead of asking "where are the cars, pedestrians, and cyclists, and what boxes contain them?", occupancy prediction asks a more primitive question for every small cube (voxel) of space around the vehicle: is this voxel occupied by something solid, and if so, what kind of thing? The output is a dense 3D grid — typically covering ~80m around the car, split into voxels of ~0.4-0.5m — where each cell holds an occupancy probability and (usually) a semantic class.

Analogy: A 3D bounding-box detector is like a person who has only ever been trained to recognize "car-shaped things" and "pedestrian-shaped things" — show them a fallen ladder, a low concrete barrier, or a truck with an overhanging trailer, and their neat rectangular mental model breaks. Occupancy prediction is like sculpting with clay instead: it doesn't presuppose any shape at all, it just marks "solid" or "empty" everywhere, the way LiDAR-based obstacle maps have always worked, but learned end-to-end from cameras.

💡 If you remember one thing

Occupancy prediction trades the compactness of "one box per object" for the generality of "one label per voxel." It handles arbitrary and unknown shapes, overhangs, debris, and partially-observed objects that a fixed-category bounding-box vocabulary cannot represent — at the cost of a much larger, denser output.

How It Works Internally
▼

The Pipeline: Cameras → Features → 3D → Voxels

A modern camera-based occupancy network (in the style of Tesla's Occupancy Networks, BEVFormer, TPVFormer, or OccNet) runs four stages: (1) a shared 2D CNN/ViT backbone extracts per-pixel feature maps from each of the 6-8 surround cameras; (2) a view transform lifts those 2D image features into 3D space; (3) the lifted features are aggregated into a voxel (or BEV, or tri-perspective-view) feature volume around the ego vehicle; (4) lightweight 3D convolutional or MLP heads read out per-voxel occupancy and semantic class.

Lifting 2D → 3D: Two Dominant Strategies

Depth-based lifting (Lift-Splat-Shoot style): for every image pixel, the network predicts a discrete probability distribution over depth bins along that camera ray, then "splats" the pixel's feature into 3D at each depth weighted by that probability. Query-based lifting (BEVFormer/deformable-attention style): instead, a fixed set of 3D voxel (or BEV) queries — one per grid cell — each project themselves back into every camera image and use deformable cross-attention to pull in the relevant image features. Query-based lifting avoids committing to an explicit depth estimate and lets the network attend to whichever pixels are informative.

The Occupancy and Semantic Heads

Once the 3D volume is populated, a small 3D-conv or MLP head produces, per voxel, an occupancy logit (free vs. occupied) and, for occupied voxels, a class distribution (car, pedestrian, vegetation, drivable surface, or a catch-all "general object" class for anything unrecognized). Many production systems (e.g. Tesla's stack) further add a temporal component — recurrent or attention-based fusion across several past frames — so moving objects are more completely reconstructed even when momentarily occluded, and an occupancy flow head that predicts a 3D velocity per occupied voxel for short-horizon forecasting without explicit object tracking.

🔑 Key Property

Occupancy prediction is a dense, per-voxel task — it makes no assumption about the number, shape, or category of objects in the scene. A bounding-box detector must decide "how many objects, what class, what box" all at once; an occupancy network just answers "solid or not" independently (well, jointly via shared features) for every cell, which is exactly why it generalizes to open-set obstacles.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Camera Ray → Voxel Projection

To lift a voxel center \(X_w = (x,y,z)\) in world/ego coordinates into camera \(k\)'s image plane, apply the extrinsic (world→camera) transform \(T_k = [R_k \mid t_k]\) followed by the intrinsic projection \(K_k\):

$$\begin{bmatrix} u \\ v \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \sim K_k \, (R_k X_w + t_k), \qquad z_{cam} = [R_k X_w + t_k]_z$$

Query-based lifting samples image features at \((u,v)\) via bilinear/deformable sampling only when \(z_{cam} > 0\) (the voxel projects in front of that camera); depth-based lifting instead predicts a distribution \(p(d)\) over depth bins per pixel and scatters the feature \(f_{u,v}\) into every voxel along the ray weighted by \(p(d)\).

The Occupancy Objective

Let \(o_i \in \{0,1\}\) be the ground-truth occupancy of voxel \(i\) and \(\hat p_i = \sigma(\text{logit}_i)\) the predicted probability. Because free space vastly outnumbers occupied space, training uses class-balanced / focal binary cross-entropy:

$$\mathcal{L}_{occ} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha_{o_i}\,(1-\hat p_i^{\,o_i}(1-\hat p_i)^{1-o_i})^{\gamma} \Big[ o_i \log \hat p_i + (1-o_i)\log(1-\hat p_i) \Big]$$

For occupied voxels, a standard multi-class cross-entropy is added over the semantic head's softmax output \(\hat q_i \in \mathbb{R}^{C}\) against the ground-truth class \(c_i\): \(\mathcal{L}_{sem} = -\frac1{N_{occ}}\sum_i \log \hat q_i[c_i]\). An optional flow head regresses a per-voxel velocity \(\hat v_i \in \mathbb{R}^3\) with an L1/L2 loss against LiDAR-derived scene flow.

Evaluation: Voxel IoU and mIoU

Binary occupancy quality is measured with voxel-level Intersection-over-Union between predicted occupied set \(\hat O\) and ground-truth occupied set \(O\):

$$\text{IoU} = \frac{|\hat O \cap O|}{|\hat O \cup O|}$$

Semantic quality (as used by Occ3D-nuScenes, SemanticKITTI, and Tesla-style internal benchmarks) is the mean of per-class IoU over all \(C\) semantic classes, computed only where ground truth is observed/labeled (camera-visible + LiDAR-accumulated voxels):

$$\text{mIoU} = \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C} \frac{|\hat O_c \cap O_c|}{|\hat O_c \cup O_c|}$$
⚠️ Common Pitfall

Ground-truth occupancy is itself hard to obtain — it's usually built by densifying and voxelizing accumulated multi-sweep LiDAR point clouds, which is noisy and incomplete for thin or distant structures. mIoU numbers are also sensitive to voxel resolution and the visibility mask used at evaluation time, so scores are not directly comparable across benchmarks with different voxel sizes.

Key Takeaways
▼

Occupancy vs. 3D bounding boxes:

  • Bounding boxes assume a fixed category set and a convex-cuboid shape prior — occupancy makes neither assumption
  • Occupancy naturally represents overhangs (tree canopies, low bridges, open truck trailers), irregular debris, and partially-observed objects
  • Occupancy is a strict superset in information: a bounding box can be recovered by clustering + fitting a box to occupied voxels, but not vice versa
  • The cost: a dense voxel grid is much larger to predict and store than a handful of 9-DoF boxes, and requires 3D-labeled (usually LiDAR-derived) supervision

Where it's used:

  • Tesla Occupancy Networks — camera-only, real-time voxel occupancy + flow, feeding planning directly without an intermediate object-detection bottleneck
  • Occ3D (nuScenes / Waymo variants), SemanticKITTI, OpenOccupancy — standardized voxel-grid benchmarks with per-class mIoU leaderboards
  • General robotics obstacle avoidance, where "is this space traversable" matters more than "what object is this"
💡 Mental Model

Think of 3D detection as parametric scene understanding (fit a small number of shape templates) and occupancy prediction as non-parametric scene understanding (describe the scene one voxel at a time). Many production stacks now run occupancy as the primary geometric representation and derive object tracks from it as a downstream, optional step.
