Generalization Error & Empirical Risk Minimization

Why training accuracy lies — and the mathematics of how much
Advanced Classical ML · Statistical Learning Theory Must Know
1
Understanding the Concept
▼

A machine learning model learns by seeing examples — a training set drawn from some underlying distribution \(\mathcal{D}\). But we ultimately care about how well the model works on new, unseen data from that same distribution. These two things are almost never equal, and the gap between them is what we call the generalization error.

💡 The Core Intuition

Training accuracy is what your model did on your data. Generalization is what it will do on the world. A student who memorizes past exam questions may score 100% on a practice test yet fail a new exam — this is poor generalization.

Formal Definitions

True (Population) Risk: The expected loss over the full data distribution:

$$R(h) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}[\ell(h(x), y)]$$

We never observe \(R(h)\) directly — \(\mathcal{D}\) is unknown.

Empirical Risk: The average loss on your finite training set \(S = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n\):

$$\hat{R}_S(h) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(h(x_i), y_i)$$

This IS observable. ERM picks the hypothesis that minimizes this proxy.

Generalization Error (Gap):

$$\text{gen}(h) = R(h) - \hat{R}_S(h)$$
🔑 The Key Property

ERM is sound when \(\hat{R}_S(h) \approx R(h)\), i.e., when the generalization gap is small. Statistical learning theory gives us finite-sample guarantees on how large this gap can be, depending on sample size \(n\) and the capacity of the hypothesis class \(\mathcal{H}\).

2
How ERM Works Internally
▼

ERM is deceptively simple: find the \(h \in \mathcal{H}\) that makes the fewest mistakes on your training data. Formally:

$$\hat{h}_{\text{ERM}} = \underset{h \in \mathcal{H}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\; \hat{R}_S(h) = \underset{h \in \mathcal{H}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \ell(h(x_i), y_i)$$

The Uniform Convergence Framework

The central question: when does small empirical risk guarantee small true risk? The key insight from Vapnik & Chervonenkis is that we need uniform convergence — all hypotheses in \(\mathcal{H}\) must have empirical risk close to true risk simultaneously:

$$\Pr\!\left[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\left|\hat{R}_S(h) - R(h)\right| > \epsilon\right] \leq \delta$$

If uniform convergence holds, then:

$$R(\hat{h}_{\text{ERM}}) \leq \hat{R}_S(\hat{h}_{\text{ERM}}) + \epsilon \leq R(h^*) + 2\epsilon$$

where \(h^* = \underset{h\in\mathcal{H}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\; R(h)\) is the best possible hypothesis in the class.

📐 VC Generalization Bound (Finite \(\mathcal{H}\))

For a finite hypothesis class \(|\mathcal{H}| < \infty\), with probability at least \(1-\delta\):

$$R(\hat{h}) \leq \hat{R}_S(\hat{h}) + \sqrt{\frac{\ln|\mathcal{H}| + \ln(1/\delta)}{2n}}$$

The gap shrinks as \(\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{n})\). More hypotheses = larger bound.

The Bias–Variance–Capacity Triangle

Three forces govern generalization:

  • Approximation error (bias): How well can \(\mathcal{H}\) represent the truth? Richer \(\mathcal{H}\) → lower bias.
  • Estimation error (variance): How well does ERM recover the best \(h\) in \(\mathcal{H}\)? Richer \(\mathcal{H}\) → harder estimation → higher variance.
  • Sample complexity: How many samples \(n\) does it take for estimation error to be \(\leq \epsilon\)?
⚠️ The Overfitting Failure Mode

When \(\mathcal{H}\) is too rich relative to \(n\), ERM memorises training noise. The ERM solution achieves \(\hat{R}_S = 0\) (zero training error) while \(R(\hat{h}) \gg 0\). The generalization gap explodes.

3
The Mathematics — Bounds & Guarantees
▼

Hoeffding's Inequality (Single Hypothesis)

For a fixed hypothesis \(h\) with bounded loss \(\ell \in [0,1]\), by Hoeffding's inequality:

$$\Pr\!\left[R(h) - \hat{R}_S(h) > \epsilon\right] \leq e^{-2n\epsilon^2}$$

Rearranging with \(\delta = e^{-2n\epsilon^2}\) gives \(\epsilon = \sqrt{\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{2n}}\).

Union Bound (Finite Hypothesis Class)

For all \(M = |\mathcal{H}|\) hypotheses simultaneously, apply the union bound:

$$\Pr\!\left[\exists\, h\in\mathcal{H}: R(h) - \hat{R}_S(h) > \epsilon\right] \leq M \cdot e^{-2n\epsilon^2}$$

Setting the RHS equal to \(\delta\) and solving for \(\epsilon\):

$$\epsilon = \sqrt{\frac{\ln M + \ln(1/\delta)}{2n}}$$

Sample Complexity

How many samples to guarantee \(\epsilon\)-accuracy with confidence \(1-\delta\)?

$$n \geq \frac{\ln M + \ln(1/\delta)}{2\epsilon^2}$$

VC Dimension (Infinite \(\mathcal{H}\))

For infinite hypothesis classes (e.g., linear classifiers in \(\mathbb{R}^d\)), capacity is measured by VC dimension \(\text{vc}(\mathcal{H}) = d_{\text{vc}}\). By the fundamental theorem of statistical learning theory:

$$R(\hat{h}) \leq \hat{R}_S(\hat{h}) + \mathcal{O}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{d_{\text{vc}}\ln(n/d_{\text{vc}}) + \ln(1/\delta)}{n}}\right)$$

Rademacher Complexity (Data-Dependent)

The Rademacher complexity of \(\mathcal{H}\) on a sample \(S\) of size \(n\):

$$\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_S(\mathcal{H}) = \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\!\left[\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i h(x_i)\right]$$

where \(\sigma_i \in \{-1, +1\}\) are i.i.d. Rademacher random variables. The generalization bound becomes:

$$R(\hat{h}) \leq \hat{R}_S(\hat{h}) + 2\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_S(\mathcal{H}) + \sqrt{\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{2n}}$$
📐 Numerical Example

\(|\mathcal{H}| = 2^{20} \approx 10^6\) hypotheses, \(\delta = 0.05\), \(n = 1000\). Bound: \(\sqrt{(20\ln 2 + \ln 20)/(2000)} = \sqrt{(13.86 + 3.0)/2000} \approx \sqrt{0.00843} \approx 0.092\). The true risk can be up to 9.2 percentage points above training accuracy — with 95% confidence.

⚠️ Common Misconception

These bounds are often vacuous for deep neural networks (VC dim is astronomical). Modern deep learning generalizes far better than classical bounds predict. Active research into why is ongoing (implicit regularization, PAC-Bayes, compression bounds).

Key Takeaways & Related Topics
▼
✅ What to Remember
  • ERM minimizes the proxy (empirical risk), not the true objective (true risk).
  • The generalization gap \(R(\hat{h}) - \hat{R}_S(\hat{h})\) is the price we pay.
  • Gap shrinks as \(\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{n})\) — quadrupling data halves the bound.
  • Gap grows with model capacity (\(\ln|\mathcal{H}|\) or \(d_{\text{vc}}\)).
  • Regularization = adding a capacity penalty to ERM to shrink the gap.

🔑 Interview One-liner

ERM is justified when uniform convergence holds: all models in \(\mathcal{H}\) converge, so the model with lowest training loss also has near-lowest true loss. The sample complexity for this is \(\mathcal{O}((\ln|\mathcal{H}|)/\epsilon^2)\).
