Luong Attention

Dot, General & Concat — three ways to score alignment in seq2seq NMT
Intermediate NLP Seq2Seq / NMT O(S) per decoder step
1
Understanding Luong Attention
▼

What is it? When a sequence-to-sequence model translates a sentence, the decoder needs to know which source words matter right now for the word it's about to produce. Luong attention (Luong, Pham & Manning, 2015) is a simple recipe for that: score every encoder hidden state against the current decoder state, turn the scores into weights with softmax, and blend the encoder states into one "context vector" using those weights. The paper's real contribution is offering three interchangeable scoring functions — dot, general, and concat — plus a cheaper "local" variant that only looks at a small window instead of the whole sentence.

Analogy: Imagine translating one word at a time while your eyes dart back and forth across the source sentence, briefly resting more on the word you're currently rendering. Luong attention formalizes that darting glance: "how relevant is each source word to what I'm about to say next?" — computed fresh at every single output step, then normalized into a set of glance-weights that sum to one.

💡 If you remember one thing

All three score functions answer the same question — "how similar are decoder state \(h_t\) and encoder state \(h_s\)?" — they just differ in HOW they measure similarity: raw dot product (no parameters), a learned bilinear form (one matrix), or a small neural network (matrix + vector + nonlinearity).

2
How It Works Internally
▼

The Three Score Functions

Given the decoder's current hidden state \(h_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\) and each encoder hidden state \(h_s \in \mathbb{R}^d\) (\(s=1,\dots,S\)), Luong et al. define:

$$\text{score}(h_t,h_s)=\begin{cases}h_t^\top h_s & \text{dot}\\[4pt] h_t^\top W_a\, h_s & \text{general}\\[4pt] v_a^\top \tanh\big(W_a[h_t;h_s]\big) & \text{concat}\end{cases}$$

Dot is parameter-free — it only works well when encoder and decoder hidden states already live in a comparable space (same dimensionality, similar training dynamics). General inserts a learned matrix \(W_a \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\) that can rotate and rescale the encoder space before comparison — this is what allows encoder and decoder to have different effective geometries. Concat concatenates \([h_t;h_s]\) and pushes it through a feed-forward layer with a nonlinearity before collapsing to a scalar with \(v_a\) — this is essentially Bahdanau's original additive attention, reused as one option among three.

From Scores to a Prediction

Once scores \(e_{t,s}\) exist for every source position, softmax turns them into alignment weights \(a_t(s)\) that sum to 1. These weight a sum over the encoder states to build the context vector \(c_t=\sum_s a_t(s)h_s\). Luong's key design choice is where attention is applied: the decoder RNN first produces \(h_t\) on its own (attention has no influence on that step), and only afterward is \(c_t\) combined with \(h_t\) through one more layer, \(\tilde h_t=\tanh(W_c[c_t;h_t])\), to produce the state that actually predicts the next word.

🔑 Key Property

Luong attention is a post-hoc mechanism: \(h_t = \text{RNN}(h_{t-1}, y_{t-1})\) first, then attention corrects it into \(\tilde h_t\). Bahdanau attention is pre-hoc: the context vector is computed from \(h_{t-1}\) and fed INTO the RNN as part of computing \(h_t\). Both converge on the same idea — align, then blend — but wire it into the decoder loop differently.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Softmax Alignment as a Soft Dictionary Lookup

Think of attention as a differentiable key-value lookup: encoder states \(h_s\) are simultaneously the "keys" (compared against the query \(h_t\)) and the "values" (blended into the output). The softmax guarantees \(a_t(s) \ge 0\) and \(\sum_s a_t(s)=1\), so \(c_t\) is a convex combination of encoder states — it can never leave their convex hull, which keeps the context vector numerically well-behaved regardless of sentence length.

$$a_t(s)=\frac{\exp(\text{score}(h_t,h_s))}{\sum_{s'=1}^{S}\exp(\text{score}(h_t,h_{s'}))} \qquad c_t=\sum_{s=1}^{S}a_t(s)\,h_s$$

Gradient flow: because \(c_t\) is a differentiable function of every \(h_s\) and of \(h_t\), gradients from the output loss at step \(t\) flow directly back to every encoder position in one hop — no vanishing-gradient chain through \(S\) recurrent steps like in vanilla seq2seq. This is exactly what fixes the fixed-length bottleneck of encoder-decoder RNNs without attention.

Why general and concat need more parameters: the dot score is a fixed, symmetric inner product — it cannot express "these vectors are relevant to each other but not literally similar." Adding \(W_a\) (general) or a full nonlinear layer (concat) lets the score function learn an arbitrary notion of relevance, at the cost of \(O(d^2)\) or \(O(kd)\) extra parameters that must themselves be learned from data.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

Softmax alignment weights are not a hard, discrete choice of "the one true source word" — they're a soft blend. A weight of 0.6 on noir does not mean "60% confident it's this word," it means the context vector is 60% built from that encoder state. Interpreting attention weights as calibrated importance scores is a well-known trap (Jain & Wallace, 2019).

Key Takeaways
▼
Score fnFormulaParamsNotes
Dot\(h_t^\top h_s\)0Fast; needs shared space, equal dims
General\(h_t^\top W_a h_s\)\(d^2\)Handles different encoder/decoder dims
Concat\(v_a^\top\tanh(W_a[h_t;h_s])\)\(2kd+k\)Most expressive; slowest; ≈ Bahdanau

When to reach for each: Luong et al. empirically found dot attention worked best for their global setting when encoder/decoder shared dimensionality — it's cheap and has nothing to overfit. General tends to win when the two RNNs differ in size or role. Concat rarely beat the other two in their experiments despite being the most powerful in principle, showing that extra capacity isn't free — it needs more data to train well.

💡 Where this leads

Luong's general score, \(h_t^\top W_a h_s\), is a direct ancestor of scaled dot-product attention in Transformers: \(\text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V\) replaces the single shared \(W_a\) with separate learned query/key projections \(Q=XW_Q,\,K=XW_K\) — the same bilinear-similarity idea, generalized and parallelized across every position simultaneously instead of one query at a time.

Code Trace

import numpy as np
def dot_score(h_t, h_s):
return h_t @ h_s # scalar
def general_score(h_t, h_s, Wa):
return h_t @ (Wa @ h_s) # scalar
def concat_score(h_t, h_s, Wa, v_a):
concat = np.concatenate([h_t, h_s])
return v_a @ np.tanh(Wa @ concat) # scalar
def align(h_t, encoder_states, score_fn):
e = np.array([score_fn(h_t, hs) for hs in encoder_states])
return softmax(e) # a_t(s), sums to 1
def context_vector(weights, encoder_states):
return np.sum(weights[:, None] * encoder_states, axis=0) # c_t
def attentional_hidden(c_t, h_t, Wc):
return np.tanh(Wc @ np.concatenate([c_t, h_t])) # h~_t
def predict(h_tilde, Ws):
return softmax(Ws @ h_tilde) # y_t distribution
