Understanding Relation Extraction
▼

What is it? Relation Extraction (RE) is the NLP task of deciding, for a pair of entity mentions already identified in text (e.g. by a Named Entity Recognizer), which semantic relationship — if any — holds between them. The output is a structured triple \((e_1, r, e_2)\), such as (Steve Jobs, founder_of, Apple). Stitch enough triples together and unstructured text becomes a structured knowledge graph.

Analogy: Imagine a detective skimming a case file. Whenever two names appear in the same sentence, the detective asks: "are these two connected, and how?" — colleagues, family, employer, none of the above. RE automates exactly that judgment, at the scale of millions of documents, for a fixed vocabulary of relation types.

Summary: RE = classify a candidate entity pair (plus its surrounding context) into one of a predefined set of relation labels, including a catch-all "no relation" class.

💡 If you remember one thing

RE is a classification problem over entity pairs, not a generation problem: given \((e_1, e_2, \text{context})\), predict \(r \in \mathcal{R} \cup \{\texttt{NO\_RELATION}\}\). Everything else — distant supervision, neural encoders, multi-instance learning — exists to build a better feature representation for that one classification decision.

2
How RE Works Internally
▼

The Pipeline

A typical RE system runs: (1) NER tags entity mentions and types (PER, ORG, LOC, DATE, …); (2) candidate pair enumeration pairs every two entities in the same sentence (or window), giving \(\binom{k}{2}\) candidates for \(k\) mentions; (3) pruning drops implausible pairs by type compatibility or distance; (4) context encoding turns the sentence + entity positions into a fixed vector; (5) a classifier scores every relation label, including NO_RELATION, and the arg-max (or a threshold) is emitted as the triple.

Representing the Context

Early systems used hand-built features: the shortest dependency path between \(e_1\) and \(e_2\), the words between them, POS tags, and entity type pairs (fed to an SVM or log-linear model). Neural systems replaced hand engineering with learned encoders: CNNs over the sentence (Zeng et al., 2014), Piecewise CNNs (PCNN) that split the sentence into three segments around the two entities and max-pool each separately (Zeng et al., 2015), or Transformer encoders where the sentence is augmented with entity marker tokens — [E1] Steve Jobs [/E1] co-founded [E2] Apple [/E2] — so self-attention can route information directly to the entity boundaries (Wu & He, 2019, "Matching the Blanks"-style entity markers).

Distant Supervision

Hand-labeling millions of relation examples is expensive. Distant supervision (Mintz et al., 2009) sidesteps this: align a knowledge base like Freebase/Wikidata to raw text. If \((e_1, r, e_2)\) is a KB fact, every sentence that mentions both \(e_1\) and \(e_2\) is heuristically labeled with relation \(r\) — free training data, but noisy, since co-mention does not guarantee the sentence actually expresses \(r\).

Design Decision: Why Bags of Sentences?

Because distant supervision labels are noisy at the sentence level, multi-instance learning groups all sentences mentioning the same pair into a "bag" and predicts one label per bag rather than trusting any single noisy sentence. A selective attention mechanism (Lin et al., 2016) learns to down-weight sentences that don't actually support the relation, letting reliable mentions dominate the bag's aggregated representation.

🔑 Key Property

RE sits between NER and full knowledge-graph construction: NER finds the nodes, RE finds the edges. Joint entity-and-relation models predict both simultaneously to avoid cascading NER errors into RE.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Sentence-Level Classification

Given a context encoding \(h \in \mathbb{R}^d\) for entity pair \((e_1,e_2)\), the classifier computes logits and a distribution over the relation set \(\mathcal{R}\) (size \(|\mathcal{R}|\), including NO_RELATION):

$$z = Wh + b, \qquad p(r \mid e_1, e_2, s) = \frac{\exp(z_r)}{\sum_{r' \in \mathcal{R}} \exp(z_{r'})}$$

Trained with cross-entropy against the (possibly noisy) distant-supervision label \(\hat r\): \(\mathcal{L} = -\log p(\hat r \mid e_1,e_2,s)\).

The Distant Supervision Assumption

Formally: if \((e_1, r, e_2) \in \text{KB}\), then for every sentence \(s\) mentioning both \(e_1\) and \(e_2\), assume \(s\) expresses \(r\). This is the expressed-at-least-once relaxation in practice — not every sentence must express \(r\), but at least one sentence in the bag should.

Multi-Instance Attention

For a bag \(B = \{s_1, \dots, s_n\}\) of sentences mentioning the same pair, each encoded as \(h_i\), selective attention computes a relevance score against a learned relation query vector \(r_q\), then aggregates:

$$\alpha_i = \frac{\exp(h_i^\top A\, r_q)}{\sum_{j=1}^n \exp(h_j^\top A\, r_q)}, \qquad g = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i h_i$$

The bag representation \(g\) (not any single \(h_i\)) is fed to the softmax classifier — noisy sentences automatically receive low \(\alpha_i\) and contribute little to \(g\).

⚠️ Common Pitfall

NO_RELATION dominates real corpora (most entity pairs in a sentence are unrelated), so naive training collapses to always predicting NO_RELATION. Practical systems down-sample negatives, use focal loss, or calibrate a decision threshold on \(p(\text{NO\_RELATION})\) rather than a flat arg-max.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use RE:

  • Populating or completing a knowledge graph from text (KB population)
  • Biomedical literature mining (drug–disease, gene–protein interactions)
  • Structured search / question answering over documents
  • Building a fixed, closed vocabulary of relation types you can enumerate up front

When NOT to use:

  • Relation vocabulary is open-ended / unknown in advance — use Open Information Extraction (OpenIE) instead
  • You need a one-off answer with no training data — modern LLMs with few-shot prompting often outperform trained RE classifiers out of the box
  • Entities themselves are unreliable — RE errors compound on top of NER errors, so joint entity-relation models are safer at low NER accuracy
💡 Hierarchy

NER (find entity spans) → RE (classify relations between entity pairs) → Knowledge Graph Construction (triples + entity linking + dedup). Each stage's errors propagate to the next, which is why end-to-end joint models remain an active research area.
