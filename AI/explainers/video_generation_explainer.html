Understanding Modern Video Generation
▼

The Evolution of Video Gen: Early video generation models relied on 3D U-Nets to extend 2D image diffusion models (like Stable Diffusion) into the temporal dimension. However, U-Nets struggle to scale effectively to high resolutions and long durations. The breakthrough came with Diffusion Transformers (DiT), which replace the convolutional U-Net backbone with a Transformer architecture. Models like OpenAI's Sora, Tsinghua/Zhipu's CogVideoX, and Alibaba's Wan2.1 are built entirely on this paradigm.

The Core Concept: Spacetime Patches. Just as Large Language Models (LLMs) process text as sequences of tokens, modern video generators treat videos as sequences of 3D spacetime patches. By flattening space and time into a unified token sequence, transformers can natively handle arbitrary aspect ratios, resolutions, and durations.

💡 The Unified Sequence Paradigm

By mapping visual data to tokens, video generation becomes structurally identical to language modeling. This allows video models to inherit the predictable scaling laws of transformers: more compute and parameters directly translate to better temporal consistency, physical simulation, and prompt adherence.

2
How It Works Internally
▼

The Three-Stage Pipeline

Modern video generation models utilize a highly optimized three-step pipeline to manage the massive computational requirements of processing video data:

  • 1. Spatial-Temporal Compression (3D VAE): A raw video is highly redundant. A 3D Causal Variational Autoencoder (VAE) compresses the video both spatially (e.g., $8\times8$ downsampling) and temporally (e.g., $4\times$ downsampling) into a compact latent space. The "causal" property ensures that the compression of a frame only depends on current and past frames, preventing future leakage and allowing streaming generation.
  • 2. Spacetime Patchification: The compressed 3D latent tensor is sliced into small blocks (e.g., $2\times2\times2$ cubes) and projected into a 1D sequence of vector embeddings.
  • 3. Diffusion Transformer (DiT) & Flow Matching: A transformer processes these tokens, iteratively predicting clean video tokens from noisy ones, guided by text embeddings.

Architectural Variations: Sora vs. CogVideoX vs. Wan2.1

While sharing the DiT foundation, these models differ in critical architectural decisions:

  • Sora: Pioneered the spacetime patch concept. Uses standard diffusion processes and is capable of generating up to 60 seconds of highly consistent video.
  • CogVideoX: Introduces a highly optimized 3D Causal VAE and 3D Rotary Position Embeddings (3D RoPE) to explicitly model spatial and temporal relationships. It uses a Factorized / Expert Transformer block to process text and video tokens jointly.
  • Wan2.1: Utilizes Flow Matching (Rectified Flow) instead of traditional diffusion. It features a 3D Unified Attention block and a modified architecture that allows for faster sampling with fewer steps while maintaining exceptional physical accuracy.
3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

1. Spacetime Patch Projection

Let a compressed video latent be $Z \in \mathbb{R}^{F \times H \times W \times C}$, where $F$ is frames, $H \times W$ is spatial dimensions, and $C$ is latent channels. We extract patches of size $t \times p \times p$. The total number of tokens $N$ is computed as:

$$N = \left(\frac{F}{t}\right) \times \left(\frac{H}{p}\right) \times \left(\frac{W}{p}\right)$$

Each patch is flattened and linearly projected to a dimension $D$, yielding a sequence $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$.

2. Flow Matching (Wan2.1 / Rectified Flow)

Traditional diffusion models use complex curved paths to transition from noise to data. Flow Matching simplifies this by defining a straight vector field trajectory between noise $x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and clean data $x_1$. The linear interpolation path is defined as:

$$x_t = t \cdot x_1 + (1 - t) \cdot x_0$$

The target velocity vector pointing from noise to data is simply $u_t(x_t) = x_1 - x_0$. The network $v_\theta(x_t, t)$ is trained to predict this velocity vector using the Flow Matching loss:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1), x_0, x_1} \left[ \| v_\theta(x_t, t) - (x_1 - x_0) \|^2 \right]$$

During inference, we generate a video by integrating the predicted velocity field using simple ODE solvers (e.g., Euler): $x_{t+\Delta t} = x_t + v_\theta(x_t, t)\Delta t$. This straight-line trajectory makes generation significantly faster and more stable.

3. 3D Rotary Position Embeddings (3D RoPE)

To retain spatial and temporal relations without losing structural information during flattening, CogVideoX and Wan2.1 split the query/key vectors into spatial and temporal components, applying independent rotations:

$$R^d_{\Theta, m} = \text{diag}\left(R^2_{\theta_1, m}, R^2_{\theta_2, m}, \dots, R^2_{\theta_{d/2}, m}\right)$$

where the rotation angle is determined by the token's spatial $(x, y)$ and temporal $(t)$ coordinates, ensuring the attention mechanism natively understands "where" and "when" each patch belongs.

Key Takeaways & Architectural Trade-offs
▼

Why the Industry is Moving to DiT and Flow Matching

The architectural shift represented by Sora, CogVideoX, and Wan2.1 is driven by several critical advantages over older convolutional architectures:

  • Flexible Aspect Ratios: Because spacetime patchification flattens the video into a 1D sequence, models can accept any combination of frame rate, resolution, and aspect ratio. This eliminates the need to crop videos during training.
  • Unified Multi-Modal Processing: By converting video to tokens, standard transformer blocks can easily mix and process text prompts, image inputs, and video history together in a single joint-attention space.
  • Flow Matching Efficiency: Traditional diffusion models require complex noise schedules. Flow Matching (used in Wan2.1) creates straight trajectories, allowing the ODE solver to generate high-quality videos in as few as 20–30 steps, compared to 50–100 steps for older methods.
⚠️ The Computational Challenge

Attention complexity scales quadratically with sequence length: \(\mathcal{O}(N^2)\). Since video tokens scale with both space and time, processing a 1-minute video at high resolution can result in hundreds of thousands of tokens, necessitating advanced techniques like Ring Attention and sequence-parallel training across multiple GPUs.

Code Trace: Spacetime Patchification & Flow Step
▼

This clean PyTorch-style snippet demonstrates how a 3D latent tensor is sliced into spacetime patches, flattened into tokens, and updated using a Flow Matching Euler step.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
# 1. Input: Latent Video Tensor [Batch, Frames, Channels, Height, Width]
latents = torch.randn(1, 8, 4, 16, 16) # Compressed latent representation
# 2. Spacetime Patch Projection (Patch size: t=2, p=2, p=2)
class SpacetimePatchifier(nn.Module):
def __init__(self, patch_size=(2, 2, 2), in_channels=4, embed_dim=128):
super().__init__()
self.proj = nn.Conv3d(in_channels, embed_dim, kernel_size=patch_size, stride=patch_size)
def forward(self, x):
# Permute to Conv3D format: [B, C, F, H, W]
x = x.permute(0, 2, 1, 3, 4)
tokens = self.proj(x) # [B, embed_dim, F/t, H/p, W/p]
return tokens.flatten(2).transpose(1, 2) # Flatten to [Batch, Sequence_Length, Embed_Dim]
patchifier = SpacetimePatchifier()
tokens = patchifier(latents) # Output shape: [1, 256, 128] (256 tokens)
# 3. Flow Matching Euler Step
def flow_matching_step(x_t, t, model, text_embed, dt=0.05):
# Predict velocity vector pointing from noise to data
predicted_velocity = model(x_t, t, text_embed)
# Euler Integration step: move straight along the predicted flow line
x_next = x_t + predicted_velocity * dt
return x_next
