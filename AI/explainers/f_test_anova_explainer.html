F-test & ANOVA

One-Way & Two-Way Analysis of Variance — decomposing variance to test group differences

Intermediate Probability & Statistics Hypothesis Testing 3.8 — Hypothesis Testing
1
Understanding the Concept
▶

Imagine you run an experiment with three diet plans and you want to know: are these diets actually different, or do the measured outcomes just look different by random chance? ANOVA (Analysis of Variance) answers exactly that question — for any number of groups.

Real-World Analogy

Think of ANOVA as a signal-to-noise ratio test. The signal is how far apart the group averages are from each other (between-group spread). The noise is how spread out individual measurements are within each group (within-group spread). If the signal is much bigger than the noise, something real is going on.

The F-test is the specific statistical test ANOVA uses. It computes an F-statistic — the ratio of between-group variance to within-group variance — and asks: "How unlikely is this ratio if all groups truly have the same mean?" If it's very unlikely (small p-value), we reject the null hypothesis that all group means are equal.

If You Remember One Thing

ANOVA decomposes total variance into between-group and within-group components. The F-statistic \(F = \text{MS}_\text{between} / \text{MS}_\text{within}\) measures whether group means differ more than random noise would predict. A large F → groups are genuinely different.

One-way ANOVA tests one factor (e.g., diet type). Two-way ANOVA tests two factors simultaneously (e.g., diet type AND exercise level), plus their interaction — whether the effect of one factor depends on the level of the other.

2
How ANOVA Works Internally
▶

ANOVA works by partitioning the total sum of squares (SSTotal) — a measure of how much the observations vary overall — into two non-overlapping parts:

  • SSBetween (a.k.a. SSTreatment) — variation due to differences between group means and the grand mean.
  • SSWithin (a.k.a. SSError / SSResidual) — variation within each group around its own mean (pure noise).
$$SS_\text{Total} = SS_\text{Between} + SS_\text{Within}$$

Each SS is converted to a mean square (MS) by dividing by its degrees of freedom, yielding a per-unit-variance estimate. The F-ratio then compares these two variance estimates:

$$F = \frac{MS_\text{Between}}{MS_\text{Within}} = \frac{SS_\text{Between}/(k-1)}{SS_\text{Within}/(N-k)}$$

where \(k\) is the number of groups and \(N\) is total observations. Under the null hypothesis (all group means equal), both MS estimates should be roughly equal, so \(F \approx 1\). When groups truly differ, MSBetween inflates while MSWithin stays the same, pushing \(F\) above 1.

Three Assumptions (check these first)
  • Independence: observations within and across groups are independent.
  • Normality: residuals within each group are approximately Normal (robust to mild violations for large n by CLT).
  • Homoscedasticity: all groups have equal variances (use Levene's test; if violated, use Welch's ANOVA).

3
The Mathematics of Variance Decomposition
▶

Let \(y_{ij}\) denote the \(j\)-th observation in group \(i\), for \(i=1,\ldots,k\) and \(j=1,\ldots,n_i\). Let \(\bar{y}_{i\cdot}\) be the group mean and \(\bar{y}_{\cdot\cdot}\) the grand mean, \(N = \sum_{i=1}^k n_i\).

Sums of Squares

$$SS_\text{Total} = \sum_{i=1}^k \sum_{j=1}^{n_i} (y_{ij} - \bar{y}_{\cdot\cdot})^2$$
$$SS_\text{Between} = \sum_{i=1}^k n_i (\bar{y}_{i\cdot} - \bar{y}_{\cdot\cdot})^2, \quad df = k-1$$
$$SS_\text{Within} = \sum_{i=1}^k \sum_{j=1}^{n_i} (y_{ij} - \bar{y}_{i\cdot})^2, \quad df = N-k$$

Mean Squares & F-Statistic

$$MS_\text{Between} = \frac{SS_\text{Between}}{k-1}, \qquad MS_\text{Within} = \frac{SS_\text{Within}}{N-k}$$
$$F = \frac{MS_\text{Between}}{MS_\text{Within}} \sim F(k-1,\; N-k) \text{ under } H_0$$
Why F follows the F-distribution under H₀

Under H₀ (all \(\mu_i\) equal), both \(SS_\text{Between}/\sigma^2\) and \(SS_\text{Within}/\sigma^2\) are chi-squared random variables with \(k-1\) and \(N-k\) degrees of freedom respectively, and they are independent. The ratio of two independent chi-squared variables divided by their degrees of freedom is, by definition, an \(F\)-distributed variable: \(F(k-1, N-k)\).

Expected Values (key to intuition)

Under the alternative hypothesis (groups differ by \(\tau_i = \mu_i - \mu\)):

$$\mathbb{E}[MS_\text{Between}] = \sigma^2 + \frac{\sum_{i=1}^k n_i \tau_i^2}{k-1}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[MS_\text{Within}] = \sigma^2$$

When groups have different means, \(MS_\text{Between}\) inflates above \(\sigma^2\) while \(MS_\text{Within}\) remains an unbiased estimate of \(\sigma^2\). Their ratio \(F\) therefore grows with effect size — this is the mathematical engine driving the test.

Common Misconception

A significant ANOVA F-test tells you at least one group mean differs — not which ones, not how many, and not the direction. Always follow up with post-hoc tests and report effect sizes (e.g., \(\eta^2 = SS_\text{Between}/SS_\text{Total}\)).

One-Way vs Two-Way ANOVA
▶

Two-Way ANOVA: Two Factors Simultaneously

With two factors A (levels \(a\)) and B (levels \(b\)), each with \(n\) replicates per cell, the total variance decomposes into four parts:

$$SS_\text{Total} = SS_A + SS_B + SS_{AB} + SS_\text{Within}$$

The interaction term \(SS_{AB}\) tests whether the effect of factor A depends on which level of B is present (and vice versa). Three F-tests are run simultaneously:

$$F_A = \frac{MS_A}{MS_W}, \quad F_B = \frac{MS_B}{MS_W}, \quad F_{AB} = \frac{MS_{AB}}{MS_W}$$

Interaction Effect Formulas

Cell mean formula for two-way ANOVA: \(\mu_{ij} = \mu + \alpha_i + \beta_j + (\alpha\beta)_{ij}\), where \(\alpha_i\) = effect of factor A level i, \(\beta_j\) = effect of factor B level j, and \((\alpha\beta)_{ij}\) = interaction. The interaction is non-zero when group profiles are non-parallel across factor levels.

6
Key Takeaways & When to Use
▶
  • Use One-Way ANOVA when comparing means across \(k \geq 3\) groups on a single factor (it generalises the two-sample t-test).
  • Use Two-Way ANOVA when you have two crossed factors and want to test both main effects and their interaction efficiently.
  • Check assumptions first — Shapiro-Wilk (normality), Levene's test (equal variances). For unequal variances use Welch's ANOVA; for non-normal data use Kruskal-Wallis (one-way) or Scheirer-Ray-Hare (two-way).
  • Always report effect size — \(\eta^2 = SS_\text{Between}/SS_\text{Total}\) or partial \(\eta^2\) for two-way. A tiny \(p\)-value with huge \(N\) can be practically meaningless.
  • Post-hoc tests matter — A significant F tells you "something differs" but not what. Tukey HSD is the standard default; Bonferroni is conservative but simple.
