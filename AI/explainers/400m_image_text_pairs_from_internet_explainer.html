1
Understanding WIT-400M
▼

What is it? Instead of paying humans to label a fixed set of 1,000 categories (like ImageNet), CLIP's creators scraped roughly 400 million (image, text) pairs already sitting on the public internet — a photo plus whatever caption, alt-text, or surrounding sentence described it. No annotators, no fixed label taxonomy: the "labels" are natural language written by whoever posted the image.

Analogy: Imagine trying to teach a child what things are by showing 1,000 flashcards with single-word labels ("dog", "cat", "car") versus letting them read a million picture books, each page captioned in a full sentence. The flashcards are precise but narrow. The picture books are messy and occasionally wrong, but they cover an almost unlimited vocabulary of concepts, styles, and contexts — and that breadth is exactly what lets the child (or the model) generalize to things it was never explicitly quizzed on.

💡 If you remember one thing

Web alt-text is free, weak supervision at massive scale. CLIP trades label precision for label diversity and quantity — then uses a contrastive objective, $$\mathcal{L} = \tfrac{1}{2N}\sum_i \big[\text{CE}(\text{row}_i) + \text{CE}(\text{col}_i)\big]$$ to learn a joint embedding space directly from that noisy text, enabling zero-shot transfer to tasks it never saw a single labeled example of.

How the Dataset Is Built Internally
▼

Step 1 — Cast a Wide Net

Crawl publicly available web pages and extract every (image, associated text) pair — alt attributes, captions, nearby sentences. This produces a candidate pool far larger and far noisier than any hand-curated set could ever be, because it is bounded only by "everything anyone ever put online," not by an annotation budget.

Step 2 — Query-Balanced Sampling

Raw web text is Zipfian: a tiny number of generic queries ("photo", "image") would dominate a random sample and starve rarer concepts. CLIP's authors built a vocabulary of roughly 500,000 queries (words/bigrams occurring ≥100 times in English Wikipedia, plus WordNet synsets and Wikipedia article titles), then searched for pairs whose text contained one of these queries, keeping at most 20,000 pairs per query. This caps the head of the distribution and gives long-tail concepts a proportionally larger share.

Step 3 — Filter the Noise

Not every scraped pair is usable. Alt-text is frequently generic ("IMG_4021.jpg"), unrelated SEO/spam text, wrong-language, or a watermark/stock-photo ID with no descriptive content. A filtering pipeline removes near-duplicate images, pairs with too-short or too-generic text, non-target-language text, and unsafe content — trading raw count for signal density.

Step 4 — Contrastive Pretraining, Not Classification

Rather than predicting a fixed class index, an image encoder and a text encoder are trained jointly so that the embedding of an image and the embedding of its own caption are pulled together (high cosine similarity) while all other image-text combinations in the batch are pushed apart. The text encoder becomes a general-purpose "label generator" — at inference time, any class name can be turned into a sentence and embedded, with no retraining.

🔑 Key Property

Because supervision comes from open-vocabulary natural language rather than a fixed label set, the resulting model performs zero-shot transfer: prompt it with "a photo of a {class}" for classes it has never seen labeled, and it still classifies competitively — something a softmax classifier trained on 1,000 fixed ImageNet classes structurally cannot do.

Why It Works — The Math
▼

The Symmetric Contrastive (InfoNCE) Objective

For a batch of \(N\) image-text pairs, encode images and text into L2-normalized embeddings \(I_i, T_i \in \mathbb{R}^d\). Compute the similarity matrix scaled by a learned temperature \(\tau\):

$$S_{ij} = \frac{I_i \cdot T_j}{\tau}, \qquad i,j = 1,\dots,N$$

The loss treats each row and each column as a classification problem where the correct answer is the matching diagonal index:

$$\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big[-\log\frac{e^{S_{ii}}}{\sum_j e^{S_{ij}}} \;-\; \log\frac{e^{S_{ii}}}{\sum_j e^{S_{ji}}}\Big]$$

The first term is "given this image, find its caption among \(N\) candidates" (row-wise softmax); the second is "given this caption, find its image" (column-wise softmax). Minimizing both simultaneously sharpens the diagonal — pushing matching pairs together and all \(N^2-N\) mismatched pairs apart, using every pair in the batch as a free negative example.

Zero-Shot Prediction

At inference, embed a candidate class list as sentences (e.g. "a photo of a {label}") with the same text encoder, then classify by nearest cosine similarity to the image embedding:

$$\hat{y} = \operatorname*{argmax}_{c \,\in\, \text{classes}} \; \cos\big(f_{\text{img}}(x),\, f_{\text{txt}}(\text{prompt}(c))\big)$$

Scale as a Substitute for Curation

Empirically, zero-shot accuracy improves roughly log-linearly with training-set size — each further validated by later scaling studies — following a power-law shape:

$$\text{acc}(N) \approx A - \frac{B}{N^{\,\alpha}}, \qquad \alpha \in (0,1)$$

where \(N\) is dataset size. Because \(\alpha < 1\), gains diminish but never vanish — doubling data keeps helping, just by smaller increments, which is precisely why going from millions to hundreds of millions of pairs was worth the engineering effort.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

More data is not a free lunch if the noise ratio rises with it. A noisy pair with unrelated text acts as a corrupted label for both its row and column softmax — enough of them and the effective sample-efficiency of the batch collapses. This is why filtering quality multiplies the value of scale rather than being separable from it (see Step 8 in the walkthrough below).

Key Takeaways
▼

When web-scale weak supervision wins:

  • You need open-vocabulary / zero-shot generalization, not just accuracy on a fixed label set
  • Concepts of interest are long-tailed — too numerous or too rare to afford hand-labeling
  • You can filter cheaply (dedup, language ID, length, safety) to raise signal density post-hoc
  • Compute budget can absorb a large \(N\) with an \(O(N^2)\) contrastive batch cost

When it is the wrong tool:

  • The task needs precise, safety-critical labels (e.g. medical diagnosis) — noisy alt-text is not a substitute for expert annotation
  • Your domain has almost no web presence, so scraping yields little more than the noise floor
  • Legal/licensing constraints prohibit training on scraped web content
💡 Lineage

ImageNet (1.2M, human-labeled, 1000 classes) → WIT-400M (2021, raw alt-text, query-balanced) → LAION-400M / 5B (open replications, CLIP-score filtered) → DataComp (2023, fixes the pool and turns filtering algorithm itself into the benchmark). Each generation confirms: past a threshold, curation strategy matters as much as raw pair count.
