1
Understanding Stationarity
▼

What is it? A time series is stationary if its statistical properties (mean, variance, autocorrelation) do not change over time. This means it has no trend, no seasonality, and its variability remains constant. Think of a stationary series as fluctuating around a constant mean with a constant spread, with the "memory" of past events decaying quickly.

Why does it matter? Most classical time series models (ARIMA, GARCH, etc.) assume stationarity. If your data is non-stationary, these models can produce spurious correlations, unreliable forecasts, and invalid statistical inferences (e.g., incorrect p-values for coefficients). It's like trying to fit a flat line to a constantly rising or falling curve — the assumptions are violated.

Types of Non-Stationarity:

  • Trend: The mean of the series increases or decreases over time (e.g., stock prices).
  • Seasonality: The series exhibits regular, predictable patterns at fixed intervals (e.g., daily temperature cycles, monthly sales spikes).
  • Heteroscedasticity: The variance (or spread) of the series changes over time (e.g., market volatility often increases during crises).
💡 If you remember one thing

Stationarity means the future looks statistically like the past. If a time series has a clear trend, seasonality, or changing variance, it's non-stationary. This property is crucial because it allows us to generalize patterns learned from one part of the series to another.

How to Check for Stationarity (ADF & KPSS)
▼

Visual Inspection (First Pass)

Always start by plotting your time series. Look for:

  • A clear upward or downward drift (trend).
  • Repeating patterns at regular intervals (seasonality).
  • Sections where the fluctuations are much larger or smaller than others (changing variance).

While helpful, visual inspection isn't rigorous enough. For that, we turn to statistical tests.

Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) Test

The ADF test is one of the most common unit root tests. A "unit root" implies non-stationarity. The null and alternative hypotheses are:

  • \(H_0\): The time series has a unit root (it is non-stationary).
  • \(H_1\): The time series does not have a unit root (it is stationary).

You typically compare the ADF test statistic to critical values at different significance levels (e.g., 1%, 5%, 10%). If the test statistic is more negative than the critical value, you reject \(H_0\) and conclude the series is stationary. Otherwise, you fail to reject \(H_0\), suggesting non-stationarity.

KPSS (Kwiatkowski-Phillips-Schmidt-Shin) Test

The KPSS test is complementary to the ADF test. Instead of testing for a unit root, it tests for stationarity around a deterministic trend or mean. Its hypotheses are:

  • \(H_0\): The time series is stationary (around a mean or linear trend).
  • \(H_1\): The time series is non-stationary.

For KPSS, if the test statistic is greater than the critical value, you reject \(H_0\) and conclude the series is non-stationary. This is the opposite interpretation of ADF. A common strategy is to use both tests: a series is considered stationary if ADF rejects the unit root AND KPSS fails to reject stationarity.

🔑 Key Properties

ADF tests for unit root (non-stationarity is H0). KPSS tests for stationarity (stationarity is H0). They are not redundant; a series can be non-stationary according to both, or stationary according to both, or only one. The choice of test depends on the expected type of non-stationarity (e.g., trend vs. unit root).

3
Making a Series Stationary: Differencing
▼

The Core Idea: Differencing

The most common technique to remove trends and seasonality is differencing. This involves subtracting the previous observation from the current observation. If the series has a linear trend, first-order differencing will often make it stationary. If it has a seasonal pattern, seasonal differencing can be applied.

First-Order Differencing

For a time series \(Y_t\), the first-order differenced series, denoted \(\Delta Y_t\), is given by:

$$\Delta Y_t = Y_t - Y_{t-1}$$

This effectively removes a linear trend. If the trend is quadratic, you might need second-order differencing:

$$\Delta^2 Y_t = \Delta Y_t - \Delta Y_{t-1} = (Y_t - Y_{t-1}) - (Y_{t-1} - Y_{t-2}) = Y_t - 2Y_{t-1} + Y_{t-2}$$

In practice, it's rare to go beyond second-order differencing, as it can introduce new problems and make the series harder to interpret. Each order of differencing results in one fewer data point. For example, first-order differencing on a series of length \(N\) yields a new series of length \(N-1\).

Seasonal Differencing

For a series with a seasonal period \(s\) (e.g., \(s=12\) for monthly data), seasonal differencing involves subtracting the observation from \(s\) periods ago:

$$\Delta_s Y_t = Y_t - Y_{t-s}$$

This removes the seasonal component. Sometimes, both regular and seasonal differencing are needed, applied sequentially (e.g., first regular differencing, then seasonal differencing on the result).

⚠️ Over-differencing

Applying too much differencing can lead to an "over-differenced" series. This makes the series non-invertible, increases its variance, and can introduce spurious correlations, making forecasting more difficult. Always aim for the minimum order of differencing required to achieve stationarity.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use ADF vs. KPSS:

  • ADF (Augmented Dickey-Fuller): Good for detecting unit roots (random walks), which imply non-stationarity. If the p-value is low, the series is likely stationary.
  • KPSS (Kwiatkowski-Phillips-Schmidt-Shin): Good for detecting stationarity around a mean or trend. If the p-value is low, the series is likely non-stationary (has a deterministic trend or unit root).
  • Complementary Nature: A common practice is to use both. If ADF rejects H0 (stationary) and KPSS fails to reject H0 (stationary), you have strong evidence of stationarity. If both reject H0, the series is strongly non-stationary.

Practical considerations for Differencing:

  • Order of Differencing (d): Start with \(d=1\). If the series still shows a strong trend or unit root, try \(d=2\). Rarely go beyond \(d=2\).
  • Seasonal Differencing (D): If strong seasonality is present, apply seasonal differencing (e.g., \(Y_t - Y_{t-12}\) for monthly data) before or in addition to regular differencing.
  • Over-differencing: Be cautious. An over-differenced series will have increased variance and can introduce artificial patterns (e.g., negative autocorrelation), making model fitting harder.
  • Visual Confirmation: Always visually inspect the differenced series and re-run statistical tests to confirm stationarity.
💡 Rule of Thumb

For ARIMA models, the 'I' stands for "Integrated," referring to the order of differencing needed. The goal is to transform a non-stationary series into a stationary one (ARIMA(p,d,q) where 'd' is the differencing order) so that standard AR and MA components can be effectively applied.

Code Trace (Python with statsmodels)
▼
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import adfuller, kpss
# --- 1. Generate a non-stationary series (Random Walk) ---
np.random.seed(42)
n_obs = 100
series_rw = np.cumsum(np.random.randn(n_obs) * 0.5) + 10
# --- 2. Perform ADF Test ---
print("ADF Test on Original Random Walk:")
adf_result = adfuller(series_rw)
print(f" ADF Statistic: {adf_result[0]:.2f}")
print(f" p-value: {adf_result[1]:.3f}")
print(f" Critical Values: {adf_result[4]}")
# Interpretation: p-value > 0.05, fail to reject H0 (non-stationary)
# --- 3. Perform KPSS Test ---
print("\nKPSS Test on Original Random Walk:")
kpss_result = kpss(series_rw, regression='c') # 'c' for constant mean
print(f" KPSS Statistic: {kpss_result[0]:.2f}")
print(f" p-value: {kpss_result[1]:.3f}")
print(f" Critical Values: {kpss_result[3]}")
# Interpretation: p-value < 0.05, reject H0 (non-stationary)
# --- 4. Apply First-Order Differencing ---
series_diff1 = np.diff(series_rw, n=1)
# --- 5. Perform ADF Test on Differenced Series ---
print("\nADF Test on 1st Differenced Series:")
adf_diff_result = adfuller(series_diff1)
print(f" ADF Statistic: {adf_diff_result[0]:.2f}")
print(f" p-value: {adf_diff_result[1]:.3f}")
# Interpretation: p-value < 0.05, reject H0 (stationary)
# --- 6. Perform KPSS Test on Differenced Series ---
print("\nKPSS Test on 1st Differenced Series:")
kpss_diff_result = kpss(series_diff1, regression='c')
print(f" KPSS Statistic: {kpss_diff_result[0]:.2f}")
print(f" p-value: {kpss_diff_result[1]:.3f}")
# Interpretation: p-value > 0.05, fail to reject H0 (stationary)
# Conclusion: The 1st differenced series is likely stationary.
