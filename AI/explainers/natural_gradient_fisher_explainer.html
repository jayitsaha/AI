Understanding Natural Gradient
▼

What is it? Ordinary gradient descent moves in parameter space using a ruler that treats all directions as equally important. But when a model is a probability distribution, there is a natural ruler — the Fisher information matrix \(F(\theta)\) — that measures distances by how much two distributions differ (KL divergence). Moving along the steepest descent direction in distribution space is called the natural gradient.

The Key Insight: The Euclidean gradient \(\nabla L(\theta)\) tells you the direction of steepest descent in the flat parameter space. But the same model can be described by many different parameterizations, and the Euclidean gradient changes with the parameterization. The natural gradient \(\tilde{\nabla} L = F^{-1} \nabla L\) is invariant to reparameterization — it tells you the true steepest descent direction in the space of probability distributions.

If you remember one thing

The natural gradient is \(\tilde{\nabla} L(\theta) = F(\theta)^{-1} \nabla L(\theta)\), where \(F(\theta)\) is the Fisher information matrix. It preconditions the gradient by the inverse of the local Riemannian metric, making optimization coordinate-free and invariant to how you parameterize your model.

Why it matters: In neural network training, standard SGD zig-zags in elongated loss landscapes because it ignores the geometry. Natural gradient methods (K-FAC, TRPO, NGD) exploit the Fisher structure to take more efficient curved steps — leading to faster convergence, especially in policy gradient RL and second-order optimization.

Fisher Information Matrix — The Riemannian Metric
▼

What is the Fisher Information Matrix?

For a model \(p(x|\theta)\), the Fisher information matrix is:

$$F(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p(\cdot|\theta)}\!\left[\nabla_\theta \log p(x|\theta)\; \nabla_\theta \log p(x|\theta)^\top\right]$$

It is the expected outer product of the score function \(\nabla_\theta \log p(x|\theta)\). The score is the sensitivity of the log-likelihood to parameters — high score means the parameter direction is very informative about \(x\).

Fisher = Curvature of KL Divergence

Equivalently, \(F(\theta)\) is the Hessian of the KL divergence between nearby distributions:

$$F(\theta) = \left.\frac{\partial^2}{\partial \epsilon_i\, \partial \epsilon_j} D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p(\cdot|\theta)\;\|\;p(\cdot|\theta+\epsilon)\right)\right|_{\epsilon=0}$$

This is why \(F\) is the right metric for distribution space: it measures how much the distribution changes as you move in parameter space. A large \(F_{ii}\) means the \(i\)-th parameter direction causes a large KL divergence — the parameter is highly "informative."

Why \(F^{-1}\) Corrects the Gradient

The natural gradient update solves the constrained problem: what is the steepest descent direction in parameter space subject to the constraint that the KL divergence to the new distribution is bounded?

$$\tilde{\nabla} L(\theta) = F(\theta)^{-1} \nabla L(\theta)$$

Intuitively: if moving in direction \(v\) causes a large KL shift (large \(v^\top F v\)), \(F^{-1}\) scales that direction down. Directions that barely change the distribution get amplified. This makes the step geometry-aware.

Key Property — Reparameterization Invariance

If you reparameterize \(\theta \to \phi = g(\theta)\), the Euclidean gradient changes arbitrarily. But the natural gradient update is identical — it produces the same new distribution \(p(\cdot|\theta_{\text{new}})\) regardless of parameterization. This is the defining property of information geometry.

Fisher = Expected Hessian (Gauss-Newton Connection)

For a model with negative log-likelihood loss, \(F(\theta) \approx H(\theta)\) (the Hessian of the loss), averaged over the data distribution. More precisely, for GLMs: \(F = \mathbb{E}[J^\top J]\) where \(J\) is the Jacobian of the output — this is the Gauss-Newton matrix. The natural gradient is therefore a second-order method without requiring explicit Hessian computation.

Why It Works — Derivation & Connections
▼

Derivation: Steepest Descent in a Riemannian Manifold

The natural gradient emerges from the question: what is the direction \(\delta\theta\) of steepest descent when distances are measured by the KL metric? Formally, solve:

$$\delta\theta^* = \underset{\|\delta\theta\|_F \leq \epsilon}{\operatorname{argmin}}\; L(\theta + \delta\theta)$$

where \(\|\delta\theta\|_F^2 = \delta\theta^\top F(\theta)\,\delta\theta\). Using a Lagrange multiplier (first-order Taylor expansion for small \(\epsilon\)):

$$\underset{\delta\theta}{\operatorname{min}}\;\nabla L^\top \delta\theta \quad \text{s.t.}\quad \delta\theta^\top F\,\delta\theta = \epsilon^2$$

The solution is: \(\delta\theta^* \propto -F^{-1}\nabla L\). This is the natural gradient.

Connections to Major Algorithms

Gauss-Newton / NGD: For neural networks, \(F = \mathbb{E}[J_\ell^\top J_\ell]\) (outer product of per-sample loss Jacobians). Natural gradient descent = Gauss-Newton descent on the expected negative log-likelihood.

K-FAC (Kronecker-Factored Approximate Curvature): The exact Fisher is \(O(p^2)\) in memory (intractable for large networks). K-FAC approximates \(F^{-1}\) as a Kronecker product of smaller matrices per layer: \(F^{-1} \approx A^{-1} \otimes B^{-1}\). This reduces cost to \(O(p^{1.5})\) while maintaining the natural gradient flavor.

TRPO (Trust Region Policy Optimization): In RL, the policy \(\pi_\theta(a|s)\) is a probability distribution. TRPO constrains policy updates by \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}} \| \pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{new}}}) \leq \delta\). The optimal step direction is exactly the natural gradient of the policy objective — this is why TRPO works so much better than vanilla policy gradient.

Common Pitfall

The Fisher matrix \(F(\theta)\) is computed under the model's own distribution \(p(x|\theta)\), NOT the data distribution. When people approximate \(F\) using the empirical data distribution, they get the empirical Fisher, which is a different object — it lacks the reparameterization invariance of the true Fisher and can be a poor approximation.

Amari's Information Geometry

Shun-ichi Amari formalized the Riemannian geometry of probability distributions in the 1980s–90s. The parameter space of a statistical model is a statistical manifold with Riemannian metric \(g_{ij}(\theta) = F_{ij}(\theta)\). The natural gradient is the gradient of the loss in this curved geometry — it uses the Levi-Civita connection of the Fisher metric to find the true steepest-descent direction.

Key Takeaways & Practical Connections
▼

When to use natural gradient methods:

  • Training generative models with ill-conditioned Fisher (VAEs, normalizing flows, Bayesian NNs)
  • Policy optimization in RL — TRPO and NPG are natural gradient methods
  • Second-order optimization without explicit Hessian (use K-FAC instead of L-BFGS)
  • Meta-learning, where the geometry of the task distribution matters

Hierarchy of methods:

  • SGD: \(\theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha \nabla L\) (Euclidean, ignores geometry)
  • Adam: diagonal preconditioning by gradient variance (approximates Fisher diagonal)
  • Natural GD (NGD): \(\theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha F^{-1}\nabla L\) (exact, \(O(p^2)\))
  • K-FAC: Kronecker-factored Fisher inverse (tractable for large nets)
  • TRPO/NPG: natural gradient for policy distributions (RL)
The Big Picture

Adam's per-parameter learning rate scaling is a rough approximation of the natural gradient (diagonal Fisher). The natural gradient is therefore the theoretically principled generalization of adaptive gradient methods — with the advantage of true reparameterization invariance and the Fisher's connection to KL divergence and model geometry.

Related topics: Fisher information (Part I §3.4), Gauss-Newton optimization, TRPO/PPO policy gradient, K-FAC second-order optimizer, Riemannian geometry, variational inference, information geometry (Amari).

import numpy as np
# Loss: L(theta) = (theta1-2)^2/16 + (theta2-1)^2
def loss(theta): return (theta[0]-2)**2/16 + (theta[1]-1)**2
def grad_loss(theta): return np.array([(theta[0]-2)/8, 2*(theta[1]-1)])
# Fisher matrix (analytic) for this Gaussian model
F = np.diag([1/16, 1.0]) # F = diag(1/a^2, 1/b^2)
F_inv = np.diag([16.0, 1.0])
theta = np.array([-2.0, 3.0]) # start
lr = 0.9
# ── Euclidean GD ──
for _ in range(50):
g = grad_loss(theta)
theta = theta - lr * g # zigzag!
# ── Natural GD ──
theta = np.array([-2.0, 3.0]) # reset
for _ in range(50):
g = grad_loss(theta)
nat_g = F_inv @ g # rescale by inverse metric
theta = theta - lr * nat_g # straight line!
# ── K-FAC approximation ──

from torch.optim import Optimizer
