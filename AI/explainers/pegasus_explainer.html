Pre-training a Transformer to summarize by making it fill in the sentences you removed
Advanced NLP / Seq2Seq Self-Supervised Pretraining Abstractive Summarization
1
Understanding PEGASUS
▼

What is it? PEGASUS is a Transformer encoder-decoder pretrained with a task called Gap Sentence Generation (GSG): whole sentences are removed from a document and replaced with a single mask token, and the model must generate those missing sentences, concatenated, as its output. Unlike BERT-style masked-word prediction or BART/T5's random span corruption, GSG deletes entire sentences chosen for being the most "summary-like" — so the pretraining task looks almost exactly like the downstream task of abstractive summarization.

Analogy: Imagine handing a student a news article with its three most important sentences blacked out, and asking them to write those sentences from memory using only the surrounding context. That is a summarization exercise in disguise — which is precisely why a model that gets good at this task transfers so well to real summarization, often with very few (or zero) fine-tuning examples.

💡 If you remember one thing

PEGASUS closes the pretrain/fine-tune gap by making the self-supervised objective structurally identical to summarization: pick important sentences, remove them, generate them back. The selection method matters enormously — picking sentences by importance ("Principal") beats picking them randomly or from the lead.

How Gap Sentence Generation Works Internally
▼

Two Pretraining Signals, One Dominant

PEGASUS_LARGE combines two objectives on unlabeled web/news text (the C4 and HugeNews corpora): a lightweight masked-language-modeling (MLM) loss on 15% of tokens in the un-selected sentences (à la BERT), and the primary Gap Sentence Generation loss. Ablations in the paper show GSG alone matches or beats GSG+MLM on most downstream summarization datasets — MLM's benefit shrinks as pretraining scale grows, so GSG is the component that actually explains PEGASUS's summarization skill.

The GSG Pipeline

Given a document split into sentences \(\{s_1, \dots, s_n\}\): (1) score every sentence for "importance"; (2) select the top \(m = \lceil r \cdot n \rceil\) sentences, where \(r\) is the gap sentence ratio (paper explores 15–50%, uses ~30% for the large model); (3) replace each selected sentence in the input with a single [MASK1] token; (4) concatenate the original selected sentences (in document order) to form the decoder's target sequence — this pseudo-summary is what the model learns to generate.

Design Decision: Why Score by Self-ROUGE Instead of Randomly?

The paper compares three sentence-selection strategies: Random (uniformly sample sentences), Lead (take the first \(m\) sentences — mimics news lede bias), and Principal (rank sentences by ROUGE-1 F1 similarity to the rest of the document, then keep the top \(m\), computed independently per sentence — the "Ind-Orig" variant). Principal wins decisively because high-self-ROUGE sentences behave like extractive summaries: they are the sentences whose content is most redundant with — i.e., most representative of — the rest of the document, exactly the property a good summary needs.

🔑 Key Property

GSG is self-supervised extractive-to-abstractive: the extractive step (choosing which sentences matter) requires no labels, and the generative step (writing them back from context) forces the model to practice abstractive compression — both skills a real summarizer needs.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Scoring a Sentence's Importance

For sentence \(s_i\), let \(D_{-i}\) be the rest of the document (all other sentences concatenated). The "Principal" strategy computes ROUGE-1 as a set-overlap F1 score between the sentence's unigrams and the rest-of-document's unigrams:

$$\text{score}(s_i) = \operatorname{ROUGE1_{F}}(s_i,\, D_{-i}) = \frac{2\,P_i R_i}{P_i + R_i}, \quad P_i=\frac{|\text{overlap}_i|}{|s_i|}, \quad R_i=\frac{|\text{overlap}_i|}{|D_{-i}|}$$

where \(|\text{overlap}_i|\) counts unigrams shared (with clipping) between \(s_i\) and \(D_{-i}\). Sentences are then ranked by score, and the top \(m\) are selected as gap sentences:

$$G = \operatorname{top}_m\big(\{\text{score}(s_1), \dots, \text{score}(s_n)\}\big), \qquad m = \lceil r \cdot n \rceil$$

The Training Objective

Let \(x\) be the input document with sentences in \(G\) replaced by [MASK1], and let \(y\) be the concatenation of the original gap sentences. PEGASUS is trained with standard sequence-to-sequence negative log-likelihood, teacher-forced token by token:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \log P_\theta\big(y_t \mid y_{

Because \(x\) and \(y\) are drawn from the exact structural pattern of (document, summary) pairs, minimizing \(\mathcal{L}\) directly practices the summarization skill — no separate fine-tuning objective needs to be invented; fine-tuning on real (article, summary) data becomes a small domain shift rather than a new task.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

Selecting too many gap sentences (\(r\) too high) starves the encoder of context, making the task closer to unconditional generation than summarization. Selecting too few makes GSG trivially easy and wastes pretraining compute. The paper finds the sweet spot around \(r \approx 30\%\) — enough signal, not too little context.

Key Takeaways
▼

When PEGASUS shines:

  • Low-resource / few-shot summarization — as few as 100–1000 fine-tuning examples can match models fine-tuned on the full dataset, because the pretraining task already is summarization.
  • News and article-style summarization (HugeNews-domain pretraining transfers strongly to XSum, CNN/DailyMail).
  • Any domain where extractive "important sentence" structure is a reasonable proxy for a good summary.

Limitations:

  • Self-ROUGE scoring is a crude proxy for "importance" — it can't detect abstractive relationships that don't share vocabulary.
  • Selection strategy and gap ratio are dataset-sensitive hyperparameters; the wrong choice degrades pretraining signal quality.
  • Like other large seq2seq pretrained models, still needs substantial compute for pretraining even though fine-tuning is cheap.
💡 Big Picture

Where BERT masks words to learn representations and BART/T5 corrupt spans to learn general text-to-text mapping, PEGASUS masks sentences chosen for importance to learn summarization specifically — a clean example of designing the pretraining objective to match the downstream task.

def rouge1_f1(sentence_tokens, rest_tokens):
from collections import Counter
sc, rc = Counter(sentence_tokens), Counter(rest_tokens)
overlap = sum(min(sc[t], rc[t]) for t in sc)
P = overlap / max(len(sentence_tokens), 1)
R = overlap / max(len(rest_tokens), 1)
return 0.0 if P + R == 0 else 2 * P * R / (P + R)
def select_gap_sentences(sentences, ratio=0.30, strategy="principal"):
n = len(sentences)
m = math.ceil(ratio * n)
if strategy == "lead":
return list(range(m)) # first m sentences
if strategy == "random":
return sorted(random.sample(range(n), m))
# strategy == "principal": rank by self-ROUGE-1 F1
scores = []
for i, s in enumerate(sentences):
rest = sum((sentences[j] for j in range(n) if j != i), [])
scores.append(rouge1_f1(s, rest))
ranked = sorted(range(n), key=lambda i: -scores[i])
return sorted(ranked[:m])
