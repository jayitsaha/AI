Central Limit Theorem (CLT) & Its Variants

Why sample means always converge to a Gaussian — and why Gaussians are everywhere
Foundational Lean Intermediate Probability & Statistics Limit Theorems √n Scaling
1
Understanding the CLT
▼

What is it? Take any random quantity — coin flips, heights of people, waiting times at a bus stop. Draw a sample of \(n\) observations and compute their average. The Central Limit Theorem says: no matter what shape the original distribution has, as \(n\) grows, those sample averages will be distributed like a bell curve (Gaussian). This convergence happens even if the original data is highly skewed or has a weird shape.

Real-World Analogy: Imagine you're trying to guess the average score of all students on a test. You can't survey everyone, so you pick a random group of 30. The CLT tells you that your sample average has a predictable bell-shaped uncertainty — the more students you sample, the narrower the bell. This is why opinion polls, clinical trials, and quality control in factories all work: averages of large samples are Gaussian regardless of what they're averaging.

💡 If you remember one thing

If \(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n\) are i.i.d. with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), then the standardised sample mean $$Z_n = \frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1)$$ The key numbers: the mean of \(\bar{X}_n\) is always \(\mu\), and the standard deviation shrinks as \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) — the famous \(\sqrt{n}\) scaling.

One-sentence summary: The CLT is the mathematical reason Gaussian distributions appear everywhere — sample averages converge to normal, regardless of the population's shape.

2
How the CLT Works Internally
▼

The Setup

Start with a population described by any distribution — say, Uniform(0, 1). Its true mean is \(\mu = 0.5\) and standard deviation is \(\sigma = 1/\sqrt{12} \approx 0.2887\). Now repeatedly draw samples of size \(n\) and record the sample mean \(\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\).

Three Observations That Explain Everything

  • The mean stays put: By linearity of expectation, \(\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}_n] = \mu\) always. Averaging doesn't introduce bias.
  • The spread shrinks by \(\sqrt{n}\): Since the \(X_i\) are independent, \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \sigma^2/n\), so \(\operatorname{SD}(\bar{X}_n) = \sigma/\sqrt{n}\). Double the sample size → the error bar shrinks by a factor of \(\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41\).
  • The shape becomes Gaussian: This is the deep part. The characteristic function of \(\bar{X}_n\) converges to \(e^{-t^2/2}\), the characteristic function of \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\). Shape information from the original distribution is washed out by the averaging.

Numerical Trace — Uniform(0,1), n = 30

True parameters: \(\mu = 0.5\), \(\sigma \approx 0.2887\). For \(n = 30\), the CLT predicts the sample mean distribution is approximately \(\mathcal{N}(0.5,\; (0.2887/\sqrt{30})^2) = \mathcal{N}(0.5,\; 0.0527^2)\). Simulation with 10,000 repetitions gives: mean = 0.5003, std = 0.0530. Match is essentially exact.

Design Decision: Why i.i.d.?

The classic CLT requires the \(X_i\) to be independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.). Independence ensures the variance formula \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}_n) = \sigma^2/n\) holds — with correlation, the denominator is smaller and convergence is slower. The "identically distributed" requirement can be relaxed (see Lindeberg's condition, Card 5), but a finite second moment is essential; distributions without finite variance (e.g., Cauchy) do NOT obey the CLT.

🔑 Key Property: √n Scaling

The standard error \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) is the fundamental rate: to halve your uncertainty, you must quadruple your sample size. This governs how all statistics courses, clinical trial designs, and A/B tests determine sample sizes.

⚠️ The "n ≥ 30" Rule Is a Myth

Textbooks say "CLT kicks in at \(n = 30\)." This is dangerous folklore. For a symmetric distribution (like Uniform), \(n = 5\) is often sufficient. For a heavily skewed distribution (like Exponential), you may need \(n > 100\). The required \(n\) depends on the skewness and kurtosis of the source distribution. Always check your data's shape before relying on Gaussian approximations at small \(n\).

3
The Math Behind It
▼

Formal Statement

Let \(X_1, X_2, \ldots\) be i.i.d. with \(\mathbb{E}[X_i] = \mu\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X_i) = \sigma^2 < \infty\). Define the sample mean \(\bar{X}_n = n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\). The CLT states:

$$\sqrt{n}\,\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty$$

The notation \(\xrightarrow{d}\) means convergence in distribution: the CDF of the left side converges to \(\Phi(z)\) (the standard normal CDF) at every continuity point \(z\).

Proof Sketch via Characteristic Functions

The characteristic function (CF) of a random variable \(X\) is \(\varphi_X(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{itX}]\). The key steps:

  • Let \(Y_i = (X_i - \mu)/\sigma\) (centered, scaled). Then \(\mathbb{E}[Y_i]=0\), \(\mathbb{E}[Y_i^2]=1\), and CF \(\varphi_Y(t) = 1 - t^2/2 + o(t^2)\) (Taylor expansion, using the first two moments).
  • The standardized sum is \(Z_n = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i\). By independence, its CF is \(\varphi_{Z_n}(t) = \left[\varphi_Y\!\left(\tfrac{t}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\right]^n\).
  • Substituting: \(\left[1 - \tfrac{t^2}{2n} + o\!\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\right)\right]^n \to e^{-t^2/2}\) as \(n\to\infty\) (the standard limit \((1+x/n)^n\to e^x\)).
  • Since \(e^{-t^2/2}\) is the CF of \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\), Lévy's continuity theorem gives \(Z_n \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1)\).
📐 Practical Approximation

For large \(n\), use: \(\bar{X}_n \overset{\text{approx}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mu,\; \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)\). Confidence interval for \(\mu\): $$\bar{X}_n \pm z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$ where \(z_{0.025} = 1.96\) (95% CI). If \(\sigma\) is unknown, replace it with the sample standard deviation \(s\) and use the \(t\)-distribution (Student's CLT).

⛔ Finite Variance Is Non-Negotiable

The CLT requires \(\sigma^2 < \infty\). The Cauchy distribution has undefined mean and variance — the sample mean of Cauchy random variables has the same Cauchy distribution regardless of \(n\). No convergence, no Gaussian. This is why heavy-tailed financial returns (near-Cauchy) break Gaussian risk models.

CLT Variants & Extensions
▼

Classical CLT (Lindeberg-Lévy)

The version above — i.i.d. with finite variance. This is what most people mean by "the CLT."

Lindeberg CLT (1922) — Non-Identical Distributions

Allows \(X_i\) to have different distributions \(F_i\) with means \(\mu_i\) and variances \(\sigma_i^2\), as long as no single term dominates the total variance. The Lindeberg condition says: for every \(\epsilon > 0\),

$$\frac{1}{s_n^2} \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}\!\left[(X_i - \mu_i)^2 \cdot \mathbf{1}\{|X_i - \mu_i| > \epsilon\, s_n\}\right] \to 0$$

where \(s_n^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i^2\). This is the broadest classical CLT. It captures: "the contribution of any one term to the total variance vanishes." This is why sums of many small independent sources of noise are Gaussian — no single source dominates.

Lyapunov CLT — A Simpler Sufficient Condition

A stronger but easier-to-check condition: if for some \(\delta > 0\),

$$\frac{1}{s_n^{2+\delta}} \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}\!\left[|X_i - \mu_i|^{2+\delta}\right] \to 0$$

then the CLT holds. The Lyapunov condition implies Lindeberg's. In practice, \(\delta = 1\) (requiring finite third moments) is most common. Lyapunov is easier to verify because you just bound moments rather than computing truncated expectations.

Multivariate CLT

For random vectors \(\mathbf{X}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d\) with mean \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\) and covariance \(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\):

$$\sqrt{n}\left(\bar{\mathbf{X}}_n - \boldsymbol{\mu}\right) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}_d(\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})$$

This underpins multivariate hypothesis tests (Hotelling's \(T^2\)) and the asymptotic normality of the MLE vector.

Delta Method — Functions of Sample Means

If \(\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\) and \(g\) is differentiable at \(\mu\), then:

$$\sqrt{n}\!\left(g(\bar{X}_n) - g(\mu)\right) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\; [g'(\mu)]^2 \sigma^2\right)$$

Used everywhere in statistics for transformations of estimators (e.g., asymptotic distribution of the log-odds, variance-stabilizing transforms).

Key Takeaways & Why Gaussians Are Everywhere
▼

Why Gaussians are everywhere in nature and ML: Any measurable quantity that is the sum of many small, independent contributions will be Gaussian — because the CLT guarantees it. Measurement errors (many tiny instrument imprecisions), heights (genetic + environmental factors), daily stock returns (many traders' independent decisions), gradients in neural networks (sums of per-sample gradients) — all Gaussian by CLT.

  • Error bars in experiments: The 95% CI \(\bar{X} \pm 1.96 \cdot \sigma/\sqrt{n}\) is valid because the CLT makes \(\bar{X}\) Gaussian.
  • Z-tests and t-tests: Both assume the test statistic is approximately normal — justified by CLT for \(n \geq 30\) (with caveats).
  • SGD noise in deep learning: Mini-batch gradient noise is approximately Gaussian (CLT on per-sample gradients), which explains why SGD can escape sharp minima.
  • Gaussian Processes: Any GP posterior is Gaussian — backed by CLT-style arguments in the infinite-width neural network limit.
  • Bayesian inference: The Bernstein-von Mises theorem says the posterior distribution of a parameter concentrates as a Gaussian around the MLE at rate \(\sqrt{n}\) — a CLT for posteriors.
✅ Summary

CLT: sample means → Gaussian, rate \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\). Requires: i.i.d. (or Lindeberg condition) + finite variance. Does NOT require: the data itself to be normal. Variants extend to: non-identical \(X_i\) (Lindeberg/Lyapunov), vectors (multivariate CLT), functions (delta method). Breaks down for: Cauchy and other infinite-variance distributions.
