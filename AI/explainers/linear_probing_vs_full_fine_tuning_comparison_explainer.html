Linear Probing vs. Full Fine-Tuning

Two ways to adapt a pretrained backbone — and why the choice affects out-of-distribution robustness
Foundational Transfer Learning Robustness Kumar et al. 2022
1
Understanding Linear Probing vs. Fine-Tuning
▼

What is it? You have a backbone \(\phi_\theta\) pretrained on a huge dataset (ImageNet, a web-scale corpus, etc.) and a small labeled downstream dataset. There are two classic ways to adapt it. Linear probing (LP) freezes the backbone entirely and trains only a new linear head on top of the frozen features. Full fine-tuning (FT) unfreezes everything and updates every parameter, backbone included, with the downstream loss.

Analogy: Think of the backbone as a seasoned translator who already knows dozens of languages fluently. Linear probing hires them as-is and just teaches a new assistant (the head) to read their existing notes for your specific task. Full fine-tuning instead sends the translator back to school to specifically re-learn on your small phrasebook — they might get better at your exact phrasebook, but risk forgetting some of the broader fluency that made them useful in the first place.

💡 If you remember one thing

Fine-tuning usually wins on in-distribution (ID) accuracy because it can reshape every representation for the task. But Kumar et al. (2022) showed that FT can distort the general-purpose pretrained features and lose out-of-distribution (OOD) accuracy — sometimes underperforming plain linear probing when the test distribution shifts. The fix, LP-FT, trains the head first, then fine-tunes.

2
How They Work Internally
▼

Linear Probing: Freeze, then Fit a Convex Problem

The full model is \(f(x) = w^\top \phi_\theta(x) + b\). In LP, \(\theta\) is pinned at its pretrained value \(\theta_0\) and only \((w, b)\) are learned. Because \(\phi_{\theta_0}\) is fixed, this is a convex optimization (logistic/softmax regression on fixed features) — cheap, stable, fast to converge, and only a forward pass through the backbone is needed (no backbone gradients or optimizer state to store).

Full Fine-Tuning: Unfreeze Everything

FT jointly optimizes \(\theta\), \(w\), and \(b\). Gradients now flow all the way back through every backbone layer. This is non-convex, needs far more compute and memory (activations + gradients for the whole network), and — with orders of magnitude more trainable parameters than LP relative to a small downstream dataset — is more prone to overfitting when labeled data is scarce.

Design Decision: Why Freeze At All?

Pretrained features already encode broadly useful structure. Freezing them protects that structure from being overwritten by noisy signal from a small, possibly non-representative downstream sample. The cost is that LP can't reshape features for the task at hand — it can only recombine what's already there linearly.

🔑 Key Property

LP trains \(\mathcal{O}(k \cdot C)\) parameters (feature dim \(k\) × classes \(C\)); FT trains all of \(\theta\) too — often 100-1000× more parameters. More trainable capacity means better task-specific fit, but also more room to overfit or drift away from generalizable features.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

The Two Objectives

$$\text{LP:}\quad \min_{w,b}\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \mathcal{L}\big(w^\top \phi_{\theta_0}(x_i) + b,\ y_i\big)$$
$$\text{FT:}\quad \min_{\theta,\,w,\,b}\ \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \mathcal{L}\big(w^\top \phi_{\theta}(x_i) + b,\ y_i\big)$$

LP is convex in \((w,b)\) — the global optimum is reachable and features never move. FT is non-convex and jointly optimizes a much larger space, so its trajectory depends on initialization.

The Kumar et al. (2022) Result

Their key observation: when the head \((w,b)\) starts randomly initialized (the usual FT setup), it is far from optimal at step 0. That produces a large initial error signal that backpropagates into the backbone. Early gradient steps therefore push \(\theta\) hard, in directions dictated by an untrained head — and can overwrite feature directions that were generalizable but not maximally discriminative on the small ID training sample, in favor of directions that fit ID data well (including spurious/incidental correlations) but transfer poorly under distribution shift.

$$\text{Acc}_{\text{OOD}}(\text{FT}) \ \text{can be} \ < \ \text{Acc}_{\text{OOD}}(\text{LP}) \quad \text{even though} \quad \text{Acc}_{\text{ID}}(\text{FT}) \ > \ \text{Acc}_{\text{ID}}(\text{LP})$$

LP-FT: Warm-Start the Head First

LP-FT runs two phases: (1) linear-probe to convergence, getting a near-optimal head \((\hat w, \hat b)\) that already matches the frozen features well; (2) unfreeze and fine-tune everything, but initialize the head at \((\hat w,\hat b)\) instead of randomly. Because the head no longer produces a large, badly-calibrated error signal, backbone updates in phase 2 are smaller and less distortive — preserving most of LP's OOD robustness while still climbing toward FT's ID accuracy.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

LP-FT does not reduce compute versus FT — phase 2 still backpropagates through the whole network. It's a fix for the accuracy/robustness trade-off, not a speed-up. If your deployment distribution matches training closely, plain FT (or even LP) may already be good enough and the extra phase isn't worth the engineering overhead.

Key Takeaways
▼

Prefer Linear Probing when:

  • The labeled downstream dataset is very small (overfitting risk with full FT is high)
  • You expect meaningful distribution shift between train and deployment (OOD robustness matters most)
  • Compute/memory is limited, or you want to reuse one frozen backbone for many linear heads/tasks

Prefer Full Fine-Tuning when:

  • You have ample labeled data closely matching the deployment distribution (little shift expected)
  • You have compute to spare and want to squeeze out maximum in-distribution accuracy
  • The pretrained features are only loosely related to the downstream task (need to reshape them)

Prefer LP-FT when:

  • You want FT-level ID accuracy but can't afford to sacrifice OOD robustness
  • You can afford the extra (cheap) linear-probe phase before the full fine-tune
  • This is the safest default when you're unsure how much distribution shift to expect at deployment
💡 Hierarchy

LP (cheapest, most OOD-robust, weakest ID ceiling) → LP-FT (near-FT ID accuracy, near-LP OOD robustness) → FT (highest ID ceiling, most compute, most OOD risk). LP-FT costs the same compute as FT plus a cheap warm-start — it dominates plain FT whenever distribution shift is a concern.
