Understanding TensorFlow Probability
▼

What is it? TensorFlow Probability (TFP) is a library for building probabilistic models as ordinary, differentiable TensorFlow code. Instead of hand-deriving gradients or writing custom samplers, you compose three primitives — Distribution objects, Bijector transforms, and inference engines (MCMC or variational) — and TFP handles sampling, density evaluation, and gradient flow automatically.

Analogy: Think of a Distribution as a vending machine: press "sample" and it dispenses a random value; press "log_prob" and it tells you how plausible any given value is. A Bijector is a reversible pipe you can bolt onto that machine's output — it reshapes what comes out (e.g. squashing a bell curve into a strictly-positive one) while automatically keeping the "how plausible" gauge correctly calibrated via the change-of-variables correction. MCMC and variational inference are two different strategies for the hardest question in Bayesian statistics: "given data, what does the vending machine's dial (the posterior) look like?" — one explores by taking correlated random walks, the other fits a simple parametric guess by optimization.

💡 If you remember one thing

Every TFP distribution supports two dual operations: \(x \sim \texttt{sample()}\) draws from it, and \(\texttt{log\_prob}(x) = \log p(x)\) scores any point. Bijectors let you build new distributions by warping old ones, automatically tracking $$\log p_Y(y) = \log p_X(f^{-1}(y)) - \log\left|\det J_f(f^{-1}(y))\right|$$ so densities stay mathematically correct after any invertible transform.

2
How TFP Works Internally
▼

Layer 1 — Distributions

A tfd.Distribution (e.g. tfd.Normal(loc=0., scale=1.)) is an object exposing .sample(n), .log_prob(x), .mean(), .variance(), and more. Internally, sample() draws from an underlying reparameterizable noise source (e.g. standard normal) and applies a deterministic map, which is precisely what makes gradients flow through samples — the reparameterization trick that variational inference depends on.

Layer 2 — Bijectors

A tfb.Bijector wraps a differentiable, invertible function \(f\) with three methods: forward(x), inverse(y), and forward_log_det_jacobian(x). Feeding a base distribution through a bijector via tfd.TransformedDistribution(base, bijector) yields a brand-new distribution whose log_prob is computed automatically via the change-of-variables formula — no hand derivation needed. Bijectors compose with tfb.Chain([b1, b2, b3]), and Jacobians of a chain multiply (log-Jacobians add), so arbitrarily complex invertible warps (normalizing flows) reduce to stacking simple pieces.

Layer 3 — Joint Distributions

Real models have multiple interacting random variables. tfd.JointDistributionSequential([prior, likelihood_fn, ...]) chains distributions where later ones are functions of earlier samples — e.g. a hierarchical model \(\sigma \sim \text{HalfNormal}(1)\), \(x \mid \sigma \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma)\). Sampling the joint samples every variable in causal order; log_prob sums each conditional's log density.

Layer 4 — Inference: MCMC vs Variational

Once you have a joint model plus observed data, you need the posterior \(p(\theta \mid \text{data})\), which is almost never in closed form. TFP offers two families: MCMC (tfp.mcmc) builds a Markov chain — via Metropolis-Hastings or Hamiltonian Monte Carlo — whose stationary distribution IS the posterior, so long-run samples approximate it exactly (asymptotically) but require iteration and mixing diagnostics. Variational Inference (tfp.vi) instead picks a tractable family \(q_\phi\) (e.g. a Gaussian) and optimizes \(\phi\) via gradient ascent on the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO), turning inference into optimization — fast, but only as good as the family \(q_\phi\) can approximate.

🔑 Design Decision: why bijectors instead of ad-hoc transforms?

You could always transform samples manually (e.g. y = tf.exp(x)), but then log_prob(y) would be wrong — you'd need to remember and hand-code the Jacobian correction every time. Bijectors bundle forward, inverse, and forward_log_det_jacobian into one composable, auto-differentiable unit, so TransformedDistribution gets a mathematically correct log_prob for free, and complex flows are built by simple chaining rather than re-deriving calculus each time.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Change of Variables (Bijectors)

If \(X \sim p_X\) and \(Y = f(X)\) for invertible, differentiable \(f\), the density of \(Y\) is

$$p_Y(y) = p_X\big(f^{-1}(y)\big)\,\left|\det J_{f^{-1}}(y)\right| = p_X\big(f^{-1}(y)\big)\,\left|\det J_f(f^{-1}(y))\right|^{-1}$$

For a scalar exponential bijector \(y = f(x) = e^x\), the Jacobian is \(\frac{dy}{dx} = e^x = y\), so \(\log p_Y(y) = \log p_X(\log y) - \log y\). This is exactly how tfd.LogNormal is implemented as TransformedDistribution(Normal, Exp) under the hood.

MCMC — Metropolis-Hastings Detailed Balance

Given target \(\pi(x) \propto \tilde\pi(x)\) (unnormalized posterior) and a proposal \(q(x'\mid x)\), the chain accepts a move to \(x'\) with probability \(\alpha = \min\!\left(1, \frac{\tilde\pi(x')q(x\mid x')}{\tilde\pi(x)q(x'\mid x)}\right)\). For symmetric Gaussian proposals \(q(x'\mid x)=q(x\mid x')\), this collapses to \(\alpha=\min(1, \tilde\pi(x')/\tilde\pi(x))\). One can show this transition kernel satisfies detailed balance, \(\pi(x)\,T(x'\mid x) = \pi(x')\,T(x\mid x')\), which guarantees \(\pi\) is the chain's unique stationary distribution — so long-run samples are (asymptotically, after burn-in) draws from the true posterior.

Variational Inference — the ELBO

VI turns inference into optimization by maximizing a lower bound on the (intractable) log evidence \(\log p(\text{data})\):

$$\text{ELBO}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim q_\phi}\big[\log p(x, \text{data}) - \log q_\phi(x)\big] = \log p(\text{data}) - \mathrm{KL}\big(q_\phi \,\|\, p(x\mid\text{data})\big)$$

Since KL divergence is non-negative, ELBO ≤ log-evidence always, and maximizing ELBO minimizes \(\mathrm{KL}(q_\phi \| p)\) — pulling \(q_\phi\) toward the true posterior. Gradients flow through the expectation via the reparameterization trick: writing \(x = \mu_\phi + \sigma_\phi \odot \epsilon\), \(\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\), moves the randomness outside \(\phi\), so \(\nabla_\phi \text{ELBO}\) can be estimated by Monte Carlo and backpropagated exactly as in ordinary neural network training.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

\(\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)\) is mode-seeking, not mode-covering: minimizing it penalizes \(q\) heavily for putting mass where \(p\) has none, so a unimodal \(q\) (e.g. Gaussian) fit to a multimodal posterior will typically collapse onto one mode and understate uncertainty — variance is usually too small. MCMC does not have this bias (given enough samples and good mixing) but pays for it with iteration cost and the need to diagnose convergence (trace plots, \(\hat R\), effective sample size).

Key Takeaways
▼

When to reach for MCMC:

  • You need asymptotically exact posterior samples and can afford iteration time
  • The posterior may be multimodal or heavy-tailed — MCMC can (eventually) cover all of it
  • Model is small/medium scale; use Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (gradient-informed) for continuous parameters to mix faster than random-walk Metropolis

When to reach for Variational Inference:

  • You need speed — VI is one optimization run, not thousands of sequential steps
  • Posterior is roughly unimodal / well approximated by the chosen family (mean-field Gaussian, normalizing flow, etc.)
  • You're embedding inference inside a larger trainable pipeline (e.g. a VAE) where end-to-end gradients matter more than exactness
💡 The Unifying Idea

Distributions give you sample/log_prob. Bijectors let you build new, exotic distributions from simple ones while keeping log_prob mathematically correct. Joint distributions compose distributions into full generative models. MCMC and VI are the two dual strategies — sampling vs. optimization — for turning a joint model plus data into a usable posterior.

import tensorflow as tf
import tensorflow_probability as tfp
tfd = tfp.distributions
tfb = tfp.bijectors
# 1-2: Distribution object: sample() and log_prob()
base = tfd.Normal(loc=0., scale=1.)
x = base.sample(400, seed=42) # draw samples
lp = base.log_prob(x[0]) # score a sample
# 3-4: Bijector — Exp warps Normal into LogNormal
exp_bij = tfb.Exp()
log_normal = tfd.TransformedDistribution(base, exp_bij)
y = exp_bij.forward(x) # y = exp(x)
ldj = exp_bij.forward_log_det_jacobian(x, event_ndims=0)
# 5: Chain bijectors — Shift . Scale . Tanh
chain = tfb.Chain([tfb.Shift(1.5), tfb.Scale(0.6), tfb.Tanh()])
warped = tfd.TransformedDistribution(base, chain)
# 6: Joint distribution — hierarchical model
joint = tfd.JointDistributionSequential([
tfd.HalfNormal(scale=1.0), # sigma
lambda sigma: tfd.Normal(loc=0., scale=sigma), # x | sigma
])
sigma_s, x_s = joint.sample(150)
# 7-9: MCMC — Metropolis-Hastings targeting the posterior
def target_log_prob(x):
return tfd.Mixture(
cat=tfd.Categorical(probs=[0.55, 0.45]),
components=[tfd.Normal(-2.0, 0.7), tfd.Normal(2.5, 1.1)]
).log_prob(x)
kernel = tfp.mcmc.RandomWalkMetropolis(target_log_prob)
chain_x, kr = tfp.mcmc.sample_chain(
num_results=600, current_state=0., kernel=kernel)
accept_rate = tf.reduce_mean(tf.cast(kr.is_accepted, tf.float32))
# 10-11: Variational Inference — fit surrogate q via ELBO
surrogate = tfd.Normal(loc=tf.Variable(-0.5), scale=tfp.util.TransformedVariable(
1.0, bijector=tfb.Exp()))
losses = tfp.vi.fit_surrogate_posterior(
target_log_prob, surrogate, optimizer=tf.optimizers.Adam(0.05),
num_steps=120) # minimizes -ELBO = maximizes ELBO
