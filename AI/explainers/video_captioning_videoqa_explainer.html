Understanding Video Captioning & VideoQA
▼

What is it? A video is really just a very long, very redundant sequence of images. Video captioning asks a model to watch that sequence and write a sentence describing what happens; VideoQA asks it to answer a specific question about it ("what did the person do after picking up the cup?"). Both tasks need the same core ability: understanding not just individual frames, but the order and change between them — temporal understanding.

Analogy: Imagine flipping through a photo flip-book instead of watching the real video. You can't look at every single page (too many, too redundant), so you sample a handful of representative pages. To describe the story, you don't just describe each page separately — you compare pages, notice "a cup appeared here" and "it's being raised there," and stitch that into a sentence in the right order. A video-language model does exactly this: sample frames, encode each one, let them talk to each other about ordering, then generate words that look back at the frames that matter right now.

💡 If you remember one thing

A video model never looks at the whole video at once. It (1) samples a small set of frames, (2) encodes each frame into a token, (3) lets tokens exchange temporal context, and (4) generates each output word by cross-attending to whichever frames are most relevant to that word — the attention pattern itself shifts across time as generation proceeds.

How It Works Internally
▼

Stage 1 — Frame Sampling

Raw video at 24–30 fps over even a short clip yields thousands of near-duplicate frames — encoding all of them is wasteful and mostly redundant. Models sample \(T\) frames (commonly 8–64), either uniformly (evenly spaced in time) or via keyframe/event selection (motion spikes, shot boundaries). Uniform sampling is simple and unbiased but can miss a fast, important action that occurs between two sampled frames — this is a real failure mode.

Stage 2 — Per-Frame Encoding

Each sampled frame is passed independently through an image encoder (a frozen or fine-tuned ViT/CLIP backbone) that outputs either one pooled vector per frame, or a grid of patch tokens per frame. Encoding frames independently is embarrassingly parallel and reuses powerful pretrained image representations — but on its own it is temporally blind: frame 6 has no idea what happened in frame 2.

Stage 3 — Temporal Aggregation

To fix that blindness, a lightweight temporal encoder (a small Transformer or 1D-conv stack) is applied across the sequence of \(T\) frame tokens, with a positional encoding added first so the model knows frame order. Self-attention here lets frame 6 "look back" at frame 2 and build a token that encodes not just "cup is being raised" but "cup was already picked up earlier, and is now being raised" — genuine temporal context, not a single-frame snapshot.

Stage 4 — Cross-Attention Decoder

A language decoder (GPT-style or T5-style) generates the caption or answer autoregressively. At each generation step, the decoder's query cross-attends over the \(T\) contextualized frame tokens (and, for VideoQA, the encoded question). The key idea: the attention distribution over frames is different for every generated word — the word "picks" should attend to the pick-up frames, while the word "drinks" should attend to the drinking frames, even though both words come from the same 8 frames.

🔑 Design Decision: Why Not Just Concatenate All Frames?

Feeding all patch tokens of all frames directly into the decoder's cross-attention is possible but expensive: with \(T\) frames × \(P\) patches, cross-attention cost scales with \(T\!\cdot\!P\). Pooling each frame to one (or a few) tokens first, then letting a cheap temporal encoder mix them, keeps the expensive decoder cross-attention sequence short while still giving it access to the whole clip's context.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Positional Encoding of Frame Order

Since the temporal encoder's self-attention is permutation-invariant, frame order must be injected explicitly. Each frame token \(f_i \in \mathbb{R}^d\) at position \(i\) gets a sinusoidal positional encoding added:

$$PE(i, 2k) = \sin\!\left(\frac{i}{10000^{2k/d}}\right), \qquad PE(i, 2k+1) = \cos\!\left(\frac{i}{10000^{2k/d}}\right)$$

giving \(\tilde{f}_i = f_i + PE(i)\). Different frequencies let the model recover both fine-grained ("frame 3 vs frame 4") and coarse-grained ("early vs late") ordering.

Temporal Self-Attention

The temporal encoder lets every frame token attend to every other frame token:

$$\alpha_{ij} = \operatorname{softmax}_j\!\left(\frac{\tilde{f}_i \cdot \tilde{f}_j}{\sqrt{d}}\right), \qquad c_i = \sum_{j=1}^{T} \alpha_{ij}\,\tilde{f}_j$$

where \(c_i\) is the contextualized token for frame \(i\) — a mixture of every frame weighted by relevance, so it can encode "what happened before/after this moment."

Cross-Attention Generation

At decoding step \(k\), the decoder forms a query \(q_k\) (from the question embedding and previously generated words) and cross-attends over the \(T\) contextualized frame tokens:

$$\beta_{k,i} = \operatorname{softmax}_i\!\left(\frac{q_k \cdot c_i}{\sqrt{d}}\right), \qquad z_k = \sum_{i=1}^{T} \beta_{k,i}\, c_i$$

The context vector \(z_k\) feeds into the decoder's output layer to produce word \(k\). Crucially \(\beta_{k,\cdot}\) is recomputed fresh at every step — this is exactly what lets the SAME 8 frames produce a temporally-ordered sentence: early words attend to early frames, later words attend to later frames, purely because \(q_k\) changes as generation proceeds.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

Sparse sampling trades off against compute: doubling \(T\) doubles per-frame encoding cost linearly, but self-attention inside the temporal encoder costs \(O(T^2 d)\) — quadratic in the number of frames. Naively sampling hundreds of frames to "be safe" can make the temporal encoder the bottleneck, not the video encoder.

Key Takeaways
▼

When this architecture shines:

  • Actions with clear temporal ordering ("picks up, then drinks") that single-frame models cannot express
  • Questions that require comparing moments ("what changed after X?", "how many times did Y happen?")
  • Long videos where dense frame processing would be computationally infeasible

Where it struggles:

  • Very brief actions that fall entirely between two sampled frames (missed by uniform sampling)
  • Extremely long videos, where even a coarse \(T\)-frame sample loses fine detail and the \(O(T^2)\) temporal attention becomes costly
  • Audio-dependent understanding (dialogue, sound events) unless an audio stream is fused in separately
💡 Hierarchy

Single-frame VQA (no temporal signal) ⊂ Video captioning/VideoQA with uniform sampling + temporal self-attention (this explainer) ⊂ Dense / keyframe-adaptive sampling with cross-modal (audio+text+video) fusion — each level relaxes the assumption that "a handful of evenly-spaced still frames are enough."

import torch, torch.nn.functional as F
# 1. Sample T frames uniformly from the raw video
frame_idxs = torch.linspace(0, n_video_frames - 1, steps=T).long()
frames = video[frame_idxs] # (T, H, W, 3)
# 2. Encode each frame independently (shared weights, parallel)
f = image_encoder(frames) # (T, d) e.g. pooled ViT/CLIP
# 3. Add sinusoidal positional encoding, then temporal self-attention
f_pos = f + positional_encoding(T, d) # inject frame order
c = temporal_encoder(f_pos) # (T, d), self-attends over time
# 4. Encode the question (VideoQA) or start token (captioning)
q_state = text_encoder(question_tokens) # fused into decoder state
# 5. Autoregressive decode with cross-attention over frame tokens c
words = []
for k in range(max_len):
q_k = decoder_query(words, q_state) # depends on words so far
scores = (q_k @ c.T) / (d ** 0.5) # (T,) — one score per frame
beta = F.softmax(scores, dim=-1) # attention over frames
z_k = beta @ c # weighted context vector
next_word = output_head(z_k, words)
if next_word == EOS: break
words.append(next_word)
caption = detokenize(words) # "picks cup then drinks"
