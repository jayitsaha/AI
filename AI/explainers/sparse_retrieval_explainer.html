Sparse Retrieval: BM25

A classic ranking function for information retrieval combining term frequency, inverse document frequency, and length normalization
Foundational Information Retrieval NLP O(Q*D_terms)
1
Understanding BM25
▼

What is it? BM25 (Okapi BM25) is a ranking function used by search engines to estimate the relevance of documents to a given search query. It's a "sparse" retrieval method, meaning it primarily relies on exact keyword matches and their statistics, rather than semantic similarity or dense vector embeddings. It's a direct descendant and improvement upon the classic TF-IDF (Term Frequency-Inverse Document Frequency) model, adding saturation for term frequency and sophisticated document length normalization.

Analogy: Imagine you're a librarian trying to find the best books for a customer's specific request. You wouldn't just pick books that mention the keywords a lot (Term Frequency), because a very long book might mention them frequently but only briefly. You'd also prioritize books that use rare, specific keywords from the request (Inverse Document Frequency). BM25 is like a super-smart librarian who also knows to discount long books that mention many things, and understands that after a certain point, seeing a keyword one more time doesn't make a book *that* much more relevant.

💡 If you remember one thing

BM25 scores documents based on how often query terms appear (Term Frequency, TF), how rare those terms are across the entire collection (Inverse Document Frequency, IDF), and crucially, normalizes for document length. It's a weighted sum of individual term scores, where each term's contribution is capped to prevent over-reliance on high TF and adjusted for document verbosity.

How BM25 Works Internally
▼

The Three Core Components

BM25 combines three statistical principles to calculate a relevance score for a document \(D\) given a query \(Q\):

  1. Term Frequency (TF) Saturation: A term appearing more frequently in a document generally means the document is more relevant. However, this effect isn't linear: after a certain point, additional mentions of a term provide diminishing returns. BM25's TF component incorporates a saturation function, controlled by parameter \(k_1\), which caps the impact of very high term frequencies.
  2. Inverse Document Frequency (IDF): Terms that appear in many documents across the entire corpus are less useful for distinguishing relevant from irrelevant documents. Conversely, rare terms are highly discriminative. BM25 uses an IDF component that assigns higher weights to rarer query terms, just like traditional TF-IDF.
  3. Document Length Normalization: Longer documents tend to have higher raw term frequencies for almost any term, simply because they have more words. This can unfairly boost their scores. BM25 normalizes the TF component by document length, controlled by parameter \(b\), penalizing longer documents that don't have a proportionally higher density of query terms.

The final BM25 score is the sum of these weighted components for each query term found in the document. Terms not present in a document contribute zero to its score, making it a "sparse" model.

Design Decision: Why is it an improvement over vanilla TF-IDF?

Traditional TF-IDF simply multiplies TF by IDF. This linear relationship for TF can lead to problems: a document that mentions a term 100 times might get an excessively high score, even if 100 mentions don't make it 100 times more relevant than a document with 5 mentions. Also, vanilla TF-IDF often uses simple length normalization (e.g., cosine normalization) which might not be optimal. BM25's non-linear TF saturation and adjustable length normalization (via \(k_1\) and \(b\)) are empirical improvements that often lead to better retrieval performance in practice.

🔑 Key Property

BM25 is a bag-of-words model: it treats documents and queries as unordered collections of words. Word order, proximity, or semantic relationships beyond individual term presence are not considered. This simplicity makes it computationally efficient and surprisingly effective for many retrieval tasks.

Why It Works — The Math
▼

The BM25 Ranking Formula

The BM25 score for a document \(D\) given a query \(Q\) is calculated as a sum over each query term \(t\) that is also present in the document:

$$\text{BM25}(D, Q) = \sum_{t \in Q \cap D} \text{IDF}(t) \cdot \frac{f(t, D) \cdot (k_1 + 1)}{f(t, D) + k_1 \cdot \left(1 - b + b \cdot \frac{|D|}{\text{avgdl}}\right)}$$

Let's break down each component:

1. Inverse Document Frequency (IDF)

The IDF component measures the rarity of a term \(t\) across the entire corpus. A common variant used in many implementations (e.g., Lucene) is:

$$\text{IDF}(t) = \log \left( \frac{N - n(t) + 0.5}{n(t) + 0.5} + 1 \right)$$

Where:

  • \(N\) is the total number of documents in the corpus.
  • \(n(t)\) is the number of documents containing term \(t\).
  • The \(+0.5\) terms are a smoothing factor to prevent division by zero or log of zero for very rare/common terms.
  • The \(+1\) outside the log ensures that IDF is always non-negative.

2. Term Frequency (TF) Component with Saturation and Length Normalization

This is the core of BM25's innovation. For each term \(t\) in document \(D\), its contribution is:

$$\text{TF_Component}(t, D) = \frac{f(t, D) \cdot (k_1 + 1)}{f(t, D) + k_1 \cdot \left(1 - b + b \cdot \frac{|D|}{\text{avgdl}}\right)}$$

Where:

  • \(f(t, D)\) is the term frequency of term \(t\) in document \(D\) (how many times \(t\) appears in \(D\)).
  • \(k_1\) is a positive tuning parameter (typically between 1.2 and 2.0). It controls the saturation of term frequency. A smaller \(k_1\) means TF saturates more quickly (additional term occurrences have less impact).
  • \(b\) is a tuning parameter (typically between 0.0 and 1.0). It controls the extent of document length normalization.
    • If \(b=0\), no length normalization is applied (the term \(\frac{|D|}{\text{avgdl}}\) effectively disappears).
    • If \(b=1\), full length normalization is applied (the term \(\frac{|D|}{\text{avgdl}}\) has its full impact).
  • \(|D|\) is the length of document \(D\) (number of words).
  • \(\text{avgdl}\) is the average document length in the corpus.

💡 Simplified TF Component

The term \(K_D = k_1 \cdot \left(1 - b + b \cdot \frac{|D|}{\text{avgdl}}\right)\) can be thought of as an "effective \(k_1\)" that is dynamically adjusted based on document length. Shorter documents have a smaller \(K_D\), making their TF saturate slower (giving them a boost for matching terms). Longer documents have a larger \(K_D\), making their TF saturate faster (penalizing them for verbosity).

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use BM25:

  • For traditional keyword-based search engines where exact term matching is important.
  • As a baseline for more complex retrieval models.
  • When computational efficiency is critical, as it's much faster than dense vector retrieval for large corpora.
  • In scenarios where semantic understanding is less critical than precise term presence (e.g., code search, legal document retrieval).

When NOT to use:

  • When queries and documents rely heavily on synonyms, semantic variations, or conceptual similarity that goes beyond keyword matching. (Consider dense retrieval methods like embeddings/vector search).
  • For very short, ambiguous queries where context is key.
  • If the corpus is extremely small or term frequencies are highly skewed, the IDF component might become unstable.
💡 Modern Context

Despite the rise of neural retrieval models (dense retrieval), BM25 remains highly relevant. It's often used in a hybrid approach where BM25 provides initial candidate documents (recall), which are then re-ranked by a more sophisticated (and computationally expensive) neural model (precision). This "retrieve then re-rank" paradigm leverages the strengths of both sparse and dense methods.

Code Trace (Python / NumPy)
▼
import numpy as np
class BM25:
def __init__(self, corpus, k1=1.5, b=0.75):
self.corpus = corpus
self.k1 = k1
self.b = b
self.N = len(corpus)
self.doc_len = [len(doc) for doc in corpus]
self.avgdl = sum(self.doc_len) / self.N
self.df = self._calculate_df() # document frequency for each term
self.idf = self._calculate_idf() # inverse document frequency for each term
def _calculate_df(self):
df = {}
for doc in self.corpus:
for term in set(doc):
df[term] = df.get(term, 0) + 1
return df
def _calculate_idf(self):
idf = {}
for term, freq in self.df.items():
idf[term] = np.log((self.N - freq + 0.5) / (freq + 0.5) + 1)
return idf
def _get_tf(self, term, doc):
return doc.count(term)
def score(self, query, doc_idx):
doc = self.corpus[doc_idx]
score = 0.0
doc_len = self.doc_len[doc_idx]
for term in query:
if term not in self.idf: continue # Term not in corpus
if term not in doc: continue # Term not in document
tf = self._get_tf(term, doc)
idf = self.idf[term]
# Calculate length normalization factor
len_norm_factor = 1 - self.b + self.b * (doc_len / self.avgdl)
# Calculate TF component
tf_component = (tf * (self.k1 + 1)) / (tf + self.k1 * len_norm_factor)
score += idf * tf_component
return score
