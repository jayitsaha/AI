How an agent decides what to do: fixed action vs probabilistic choice
Foundational Deep Learning Reinforcement Learning O(1) decision cost

Understanding Policies
▼

What is a Policy? In Reinforcement Learning (RL), a policy, denoted \(\pi\), is the agent's strategy: it defines how an agent behaves in an environment. Given a state \(s\), the policy dictates what action \(a\) the agent should take. It's essentially the "brain" of the agent.

Policies can be categorized into two main types based on their output:

  • Deterministic Policy: For any given state \(s\), the policy always selects the exact same action \(a\). There is no randomness involved in the action selection.
  • Stochastic Policy: For any given state \(s\), the policy outputs a probability distribution over all possible actions. The agent then samples an action from this distribution, meaning there's an element of randomness in its choice.

Analogy: Imagine you're playing chess. A deterministic policy would be a fixed rule: "If the opponent moves their queen to E4, I always move my knight to C3." There's no deviation. A stochastic policy might say: "If the opponent moves queen to E4, there's a 70% chance I move knight to C3, and a 30% chance I move pawn to D5." You roll a mental dice each time.

💡 If you remember one thing

A policy \(\pi(s)\) maps states to actions. If it maps to a single action, it's deterministic. If it maps to a probability distribution over actions, it's stochastic. The choice between them has profound implications for exploration, stability, and optimality in complex environments.

2
How Each Policy Type Works
▼

Deterministic Policy: \(\pi(s) = a\)

A deterministic policy is typically derived from a set of learned action-value functions (Q-values) or from a value function. For a given state \(s\), the agent simply chooses the action \(a\) that maximizes its expected future reward, often represented by \(Q(s,a)\).

$$\pi(s) = \arg\max_a Q(s,a)$$

This is straightforward and computationally efficient at inference time. It's commonly used in algorithms like Q-learning (after convergence) or in model-free continuous control methods (e.g., DDPG, TD3) where the policy directly outputs a continuous action value.

Stochastic Policy: \(\pi(a|s) = P(A=a|S=s)\)

A stochastic policy maintains a probability distribution over actions for each state. This distribution is often derived from action preferences (logits) or Q-values using a function like softmax (for discrete action spaces) or a Gaussian distribution (for continuous action spaces).

$$\pi(a|s) = \frac{e^{\text{preference}(s,a)}}{\sum_{a'} e^{\text{preference}(s,a')}}$$

The agent then samples an action from this distribution. This inherent randomness is crucial for exploration during training and can be beneficial in environments with partial observability or where optimal behavior requires mixed strategies.

Design Decision: Why Not Always Pick the "Best" Action?

While a deterministic policy always picks the greedily "best" action according to its current estimates, this can be problematic during learning. If the agent is always deterministic, it might get stuck in local optima, never exploring potentially better actions. Stochastic policies, by their nature, encourage exploration. Even if one action has the highest probability, others still have a non-zero chance of being chosen, allowing the agent to discover new strategies or better rewards.

🔑 Key Property

Exploration vs. Exploitation: Deterministic policies are purely exploitative once learned. Stochastic policies naturally balance exploration (trying new things) and exploitation (doing what's known to be good) by assigning probabilities, making them particularly effective during the training phase of many RL algorithms.

The Math Behind Stochastic Policies
▼

From Action-Values to Probabilities: The Softmax Function

For discrete action spaces, the most common way to convert a set of action preferences (e.g., Q-values, denoted \(Q(s,a)\)) into a probability distribution is using the softmax function. It takes a vector of real numbers and squashes them into a probability distribution, where each element is between 0 and 1 and the sum of all elements is 1.

$$\pi(a|s) = \frac{e^{Q(s,a)/\tau}}{\sum_{a' \in \mathcal{A}} e^{Q(s,a')/\tau}}$$

Here, \(\mathcal{A}\) is the set of all possible actions, and \(\tau\) (tau) is a positive parameter called temperature.

The Role of Temperature \(\tau\)

The temperature parameter \(\tau\) controls the "peakiness" or "flatness" of the probability distribution:

  • If \(\tau \to 0\) (very low temperature), the softmax function becomes more "greedy" and approaches a deterministic policy, where the action with the highest Q-value gets a probability close to 1, and all others get probabilities close to 0.
  • If \(\tau \to \infty\) (very high temperature), the softmax function approaches a uniform distribution, where all actions have roughly equal probability, regardless of their Q-values. This encourages maximum exploration.
  • For \(\tau = 1\), the probabilities are directly proportional to the exponentiated Q-values.

This temperature scaling is a powerful mechanism for controlling the exploration-exploitation trade-off within a stochastic policy. During training, \(\tau\) might be annealed (gradually reduced) over time to transition from more exploration to more exploitation.

➗ Entropy of a Policy

The "randomness" of a stochastic policy can be quantified by its entropy. For a policy \(\pi(a|s)\), the entropy is: $$H(\pi(s)) = -\sum_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \pi(a|s) \log_2 \pi(a|s)$$ A high entropy means the policy is more random (actions are more equally probable), while low entropy means it's more concentrated on a few actions. RL algorithms often try to maximize reward while also encouraging high policy entropy to promote exploration.

Key Takeaways
▼

Deterministic Policies:

  • Pros: Simple to implement, computationally efficient (no sampling needed), often perform well once the optimal policy is found. Used in off-policy algorithms like Q-learning (at deployment) and in continuous control (DDPG, TD3).
  • Cons: Prone to getting stuck in local optima during training (poor exploration), struggles in environments with partial observability or inherent stochasticity where a single "best" action isn't always optimal.

Stochastic Policies:

  • Pros: Excellent for exploration during training, can learn optimal mixed strategies in complex environments (e.g., games where opponents can exploit predictable moves), robust to observation noise. Fundamental to on-policy algorithms like Policy Gradients, A2C, and PPO.
  • Cons: Requires sampling actions (can be slower), might be harder to train (higher variance in gradients), and can be less stable in very simple, deterministic environments where a greedy choice is always best.
💡 When to choose which

For training, stochastic policies are generally preferred due to their exploration capabilities. For deployment (inference) in stable environments, a deterministic policy (derived from the learned stochastic one by taking the argmax) is often used for efficiency. However, in inherently stochastic environments, maintaining a stochastic policy even at inference can be optimal.

class StochasticPolicy(Policy):
def get_action_probabilities(self, q_values, temperature=1.0):
if temperature <= 0: # Handle edge case for deterministic-like behavior
return DeterministicPolicy(self.num_actions).get_action_probabilities(q_values)
exp_q = np.exp(np.array(q_values) / temperature)
probs = exp_q / np.sum(exp_q)
return probs
def select_action(self, q_values, temperature=1.0):
probs = self.get_action_probabilities(q_values, temperature)
return np.random.choice(self.num_actions, p=probs)
