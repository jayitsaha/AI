1
Understanding the ELBO
▼

What is it? Suppose you want to understand why a model produced some observation \(x\) — for example, a face image. The evidence is the probability your generative model assigns to that observation: \(\log p(x)\). To compute it, you would need to integrate over every possible hidden cause \(z\) that could have generated \(x\), which is usually intractable.

The Evidence Lower BOund (ELBO) is a tractable proxy. Instead of computing \(\log p(x)\) directly, you introduce a variational distribution \(q(z)\) — a simple family of distributions (e.g., Gaussians) — and show that \(\log p(x) \geq \mathrm{ELBO}(q)\). The ELBO is always computable, and maximising it pushes \(q(z)\) toward the true (intractable) posterior \(p(z|x)\).

Analogy: Think of \(\log p(x)\) as the height of a mountain peak hidden in fog. The ELBO is your GPS altitude reading standing somewhere on the slope — always below the peak, but if you climb (optimise the ELBO), you get closer. When you reach the summit, the GPS matches the true altitude exactly.

If you remember one thing

$$\log p(x) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q(z)}\!\left[\log \frac{p(x,z)}{q(z)}\right]}_{\mathrm{ELBO}(q)} + \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}(q(z)\|p(z|x))}_{\geq\,0}$$The ELBO lower-bounds \(\log p(x)\). Since \(\mathrm{KL} \geq 0\), maximising the ELBO is equivalent to minimising \(\mathrm{KL}(q\|p(z|x))\), i.e., finding the best approximation to the posterior within the variational family.

2
Deriving the ELBO — Step by Step
▼

Step 1 — Start from the definition of KL divergence

We want to approximate the true posterior \(p(z|x)\) with a simpler \(q(z)\). The KL divergence measures how bad our approximation is:

$$\mathrm{KL}(q(z)\|p(z|x)) = \mathbb{E}_{q(z)}\!\left[\log \frac{q(z)}{p(z|x)}\right] \geq 0$$

Step 2 — Substitute \(p(z|x) = p(x,z)/p(x)\)

Using Bayes' rule \(p(z|x) = p(x,z)/p(x)\):

$$\mathrm{KL}(q\|p(z|x)) = \mathbb{E}_q\!\left[\log q(z) - \log p(x,z) + \log p(x)\right]$$

Step 3 — Rearrange: solve for \(\log p(x)\)

Since \(\log p(x)\) does not depend on \(z\), it comes out of the expectation:

$$\log p(x) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q\!\left[\log p(x,z) - \log q(z)\right]}_{\mathrm{ELBO}(q)} + \mathrm{KL}(q(z)\|p(z|x))$$

Step 4 — Two equivalent forms of the ELBO

Expanding the joint \(p(x,z) = p(x|z)\,p(z)\):

$$\mathrm{ELBO}(q) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x|z)]}_{\text{reconstruction}} - \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}(q(z)\|p(z))}_{\text{complexity penalty}}$$
Key Invariant

The identity \(\log p(x) = \mathrm{ELBO} + \mathrm{KL}(q\|p(z|x))\) holds for every \(q\), not just the optimum. Since \(\mathrm{KL} \geq 0\), this simultaneously proves that (a) ELBO \(\leq \log p(x)\) always, and (b) the bound is tight iff \(q = p(z|x)\).

Common Misconception

People sometimes think "maximise reconstruction + minimise KL-to-prior separately." This is wrong. You maximise their sum (the ELBO). At the optimum, reconstruction and KL-to-prior are individually suboptimal — the ELBO trades them off automatically via the decomposition above.

Connection to VAEs
▼

A Variational Autoencoder (VAE; Kingma & Welling, 2013) is exactly a neural-network implementation of variational inference. The ELBO decomposition tells you directly what each part of the network does:

The VAE loss is \(-\mathrm{ELBO}\), minimised over encoder parameters \(\phi\) and decoder parameters \(\theta\):

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VAE}}(\theta, \phi) = -\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}[\log p_\theta(x|z)] + \mathrm{KL}(q_\phi(z|x)\|p(z))$$
  • Reconstruction term \(\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[\log p_\theta(x|z)]\): trains the decoder to reproduce \(x\) from a latent code \(z\). For Gaussian likelihoods this becomes MSE; for Bernoulli it becomes binary cross-entropy.
  • KL term \(\mathrm{KL}(q_\phi(z|x)\|p(z))\): regularises the encoder so latent codes are distributed like the prior \(p(z)=\mathcal{N}(0,I)\). For Gaussian \(q_\phi\), this has a closed form: \(\frac{1}{2}\sum_j(\sigma_j^2 + \mu_j^2 - 1 - \log \sigma_j^2)\).
Reparameterisation Trick

Sampling \(z \sim q_\phi(z|x)\) is non-differentiable. The fix: write \(z = \mu_\phi + \sigma_\phi \cdot \varepsilon\), \(\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\). Gradients flow through \(\mu_\phi\) and \(\sigma_\phi\) while \(\varepsilon\) is just noise. This makes the ELBO differentiable end-to-end.

5
Key Takeaways
▼

The three-equation cheat sheet:

$$\log p(x) = \mathrm{ELBO}(q) + \mathrm{KL}(q(z)\|p(z|x)), \quad \mathrm{KL} \geq 0$$
$$\mathrm{ELBO}(q) = \mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x|z)] - \mathrm{KL}(q(z)\|p(z))$$
$$\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{N}(\mu,\Sigma)\|\mathcal{N}(0,I)) = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma) + \|\mu\|^2 - d - \log\det\Sigma\right]$$
When to use the ELBO / VI / VAEs

Use VI when exact inference is intractable but you can compute \(\log p(x,z)\) and its gradient. VAEs extend this to high-dimensional \(x\) (images, audio) by parameterising both \(q_\phi(z|x)\) and \(p_\theta(x|z)\) as neural networks.

Related concepts: KL divergence, Jensen's inequality (ELBO derivation alternative), EM algorithm (special case of VI), importance-weighted ELBO (IWAE), normalising flows (richer variational families), diffusion models (hierarchical latent variables).

Code Trace — VAE ELBO in PyTorch
▼
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
# ELBO = E_q[log p(x|z)] - KL(q(z|x) || p(z))
# where p(z) = N(0, I), q(z|x) = N(mu, sigma^2 I)
def vae_loss(x, x_recon, mu, log_var):
# Reconstruction: -E_q[log p(x|z)]
recon = F.binary_cross_entropy(x_recon, x, reduction='sum')
# KL(q || p) closed-form for Gaussian prior
# = 0.5 * sum(sigma^2 + mu^2 - 1 - log(sigma^2))
kl = -0.5 * torch.sum(1 + log_var - mu.pow(2) - log_var.exp())
return recon + kl # = -ELBO (we minimise this)
