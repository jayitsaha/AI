Byte Pair Encoding (BPE)

Iterative subword merging for tokenization in modern NLP
Intermediate NLP Tokenization O(n * k) merges
1
Understanding BPE
▼

What is it? Byte Pair Encoding is a subword tokenization algorithm used by GPT, LLaMA, and most modern language models. Instead of splitting text into full words (which cannot handle unseen words) or individual characters (which produces very long sequences), BPE finds a middle ground by iteratively merging the most frequent pair of adjacent symbols into a new symbol. The result is a vocabulary of subword units that balances coverage and sequence length.

Analogy: Imagine you are creating shorthand for taking notes in a lecture. You notice you keep writing "th" together, so you invent a single symbol for it. Then you notice "the" appears constantly, so you merge your "th" symbol with "e". Over time, common fragments get their own shorthand symbols while rare words can still be spelled out letter-by-letter. That is exactly what BPE does to build a tokenizer vocabulary.

If you remember one thing

BPE starts with characters, counts all adjacent pairs, merges the most frequent pair into a new token, and repeats. After \(k\) merges you have a vocabulary of size \(|\text{base chars}| + k\). Common words become single tokens; rare words decompose into known subwords.

How BPE Works Internally
▼

Step 1: Pre-tokenize and Initialize

The training corpus is first split into words (by whitespace or a regex-based pre-tokenizer). Each word is decomposed into individual characters, often with a special end-of-word marker (e.g., </w>) to preserve word boundaries. The initial vocabulary is simply the set of all unique characters found in the corpus.

Step 2: Count Pair Frequencies

For every word in the corpus (weighted by how often that word appears), count the frequency of every adjacent pair of symbols. For example, in the word l o w appearing 5 times, the pair (l, o) has frequency 5 and (o, w) has frequency 5.

Step 3: Merge the Top Pair

Find the pair with the highest total frequency across the entire corpus. Replace every occurrence of that pair with a new merged symbol. Add the new symbol to the vocabulary. This single merge reduces the total number of tokens in the corpus while growing the vocabulary by one.

Step 4: Repeat

Go back to Step 2 with the updated representations. Repeat for a predetermined number of merges \(k\), or until the desired vocabulary size \(V = |\text{base}| + k\) is reached. Typical vocabulary sizes range from 30,000 to 100,000 for production models (GPT-2 uses 50,257; LLaMA uses 32,000).

Key Property

BPE is greedy and deterministic: at each step, the globally most frequent pair is merged. The merge order is recorded and defines the tokenizer. At encoding time, merges are applied in the same order, guaranteeing a unique tokenization for any input string.

Computational Complexity

Each merge iteration scans all word representations to count pairs and apply replacements. With \(n\) total tokens in the corpus and \(k\) desired merges, the naive complexity is \(O(n \cdot k)\). In practice, efficient implementations use priority queues and incremental updates to avoid full rescans, achieving near-linear time.

The Math Behind BPE
▼

Let the corpus consist of words \(w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_m\) with frequencies \(f_1, f_2, \ldots, f_m\). Each word \(w_i\) is a sequence of symbols \(s_1^{(i)}, s_2^{(i)}, \ldots, s_{n_i}^{(i)}\).

Pair Frequency

The frequency of an adjacent pair \((a, b)\) across the corpus is:

$$\text{freq}(a, b) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} f_i \cdot \left| \{ j : s_j^{(i)} = a \text{ and } s_{j+1}^{(i)} = b \} \right|$$

Merge Operation

At each iteration \(t\), select the pair with maximum frequency:

$$(a^*, b^*) = \underset{(a,b)}{\operatorname{argmax}} \; \text{freq}(a, b)$$

Then create a new symbol \(ab\) and replace all occurrences of \((a^*, b^*)\) in every word. The vocabulary grows: \(V_{t+1} = V_t \cup \{a^* b^*\}\) and the total token count decreases.

Compression Ratio

After \(k\) merges, the total number of tokens \(N_k\) in the corpus satisfies:

$$N_k = N_0 - \sum_{t=1}^{k} \text{freq}_t(a_t^*, b_t^*)$$

where \(N_0\) is the initial character-level token count and \(\text{freq}_t\) is the pair frequency at iteration \(t\). Each merge reduces the corpus length by exactly the frequency of the merged pair.

Common Misconception

BPE does not optimize a global compression objective jointly. It is a greedy algorithm: each merge is locally optimal (most frequent pair) but the sequence of merges is not guaranteed to minimize total token count for a given vocabulary budget. Despite this, it works remarkably well in practice and is the foundation of most production tokenizers.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use BPE: BPE is the default choice for training tokenizers for language models. It handles open-vocabulary problems gracefully -- any word can be represented as a sequence of subword tokens, even words never seen during training. It is used by GPT-2/3/4, RoBERTa, and many other models.

Alternatives: WordPiece (used by BERT) is similar but uses a likelihood-based scoring instead of raw frequency. Unigram (SentencePiece) starts with a large vocabulary and prunes, optimizing a probabilistic objective. BPE is simpler and empirically competitive.

Complexity Summary

Training: \(O(n \cdot k)\) where \(n\) = corpus tokens, \(k\) = number of merges. Encoding: \(O(m \cdot k)\) per word of length \(m\) using the learned merge table. Optimized implementations (e.g., tiktoken) use tries and regex for near-linear encoding.

Key insight: BPE adapts to the statistical properties of the training corpus. High-frequency words become single tokens (maximizing efficiency), while rare or novel words decompose into meaningful subword pieces (maximizing coverage). This frequency-adaptive granularity is what makes subword tokenization so effective for modern NLP.

BPE From Scratch (Python)

import re, collections
def get_vocab(corpus):
"""Split corpus into word-frequency dict with char-level tokens."""
vocab = collections.Counter()
for word in corpus.split():
# Add end-of-word marker, split into chars
vocab[' '.join(list(word)) + ' </w>'] += 1
return vocab
def get_pairs(vocab):
"""Count frequency of all adjacent symbol pairs."""
pairs = collections.Counter()
for word, freq in vocab.items():
symbols = word.split()
for i in range(len(symbols) - 1):
pairs[(symbols[i], symbols[i+1])] += freq
return pairs
def merge_vocab(pair, vocab):
"""Replace all occurrences of pair with merged symbol."""
new_vocab = {}
bigram = re.escape(' '.join(pair))
pattern = re.compile(r'(?<!\S)' + bigram + r'(?!\S)')
for word, freq in vocab.items():
new_word = pattern.sub(''.join(pair), word)
new_vocab[new_word] = freq
return new_vocab
# --- Run BPE ---
corpus = "low low low low low lower lower newest newest newest newest newest newest wider wider wider"
num_merges = 10
vocab = get_vocab(corpus)
for i in range(num_merges):
pairs = get_pairs(vocab)
if not pairs: break
best = max(pairs, key=pairs.get)
vocab = merge_vocab(best, vocab)
print(f"Merge {i+1}: {best[0]} + {best[1]} -> {''.join(best)} (freq={pairs[best]})")
