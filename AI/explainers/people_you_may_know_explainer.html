1
Understanding PYMK
▼

What is it? "People You May Know" (PYMK) or friend suggestion is a core feature of almost every social network, from Facebook to LinkedIn. Its goal is to recommend new connections to users, helping them grow their network and increasing engagement on the platform. These recommendations are typically based on shared connections, interests, demographic data, and activity patterns.

Analogy: Imagine you're at a large party. You're introduced to a new person. How do you find more people you might know? You'd likely ask, "Who do you know here?" and if they mention someone you also know, that new person becomes a strong candidate for someone "you may know" too. This simple social heuristic — mutual friends — is the bedrock of most PYMK algorithms.

💡 If you remember one thing

The most powerful and common signal for friend suggestions is shared connections (mutual friends). If two people have many friends in common, they are much more likely to know each other or want to connect.

2
How PYMK Works Internally
▼

The Core Algorithm: Graph Traversal

At its heart, PYMK is a graph problem. Users are nodes, and friendships are edges. The basic algorithm involves finding "friends of friends" (2-hop neighbors) for a target user and then scoring these potential connections.

  1. Identify Target User (U): The user for whom suggestions are being generated.
  2. Find Direct Friends (\(N(U)\)): All users directly connected to U.
  3. Find Friends of Friends (\(N(N(U))\)): All users connected to U's direct friends.
  4. Filter Candidates: Remove U itself, and any users already in \(N(U)\) (since they are already friends). The remaining users are potential suggestions.
  5. Calculate Similarity Score: For each candidate V, compute a score indicating the likelihood of U and V knowing each other (e.g., number of common friends, Jaccard Index).
  6. Rank and Filter: Sort candidates by score and present the top N suggestions.

Beyond Common Friends: Enriching Signals

While common friends are crucial, real-world PYMK systems use a rich array of additional signals to improve relevance:

  • Shared Groups/Events: Users who belong to the same groups, attended the same events, or interacted with the same content.
  • Profile Similarity: Users with similar employers, educational backgrounds, locations, or interests listed in their profiles.
  • Interaction Patterns: Users who have viewed each other's profiles, commented on mutual friends' posts, or are frequently co-tagged in photos.
  • Uploads/Contacts: Users who have uploaded their phone contacts, revealing connections not yet on the platform.
  • Embedding Similarity: Modern systems often use graph embeddings (e.g., Node2Vec, GraphSAGE) or user embeddings (from activity data) to find users that are "close" in a high-dimensional feature space.

Design Decision: Why 2-Hop Neighbors?

Direct friends (1-hop) are already connected. 2-hop neighbors represent the most immediate "next layer" of potential connections. Going to 3-hop or more quickly leads to an explosion in the number of candidates, making computation much more expensive and the relevance of suggestions generally lower (too many "strangers"). The sweet spot for balancing relevance and computational cost is usually 2-hop.

🔑 Key Property

PYMK algorithms leverage the inherent transitivity of social networks: "friends of my friends are likely to become my friends." This principle is mathematically captured by various graph similarity metrics.

3
Why It Works — The Math of Link Prediction
▼

Formalizing "Common Friends"

Let \(N(U)\) denote the set of neighbors (friends) of user \(U\). For a target user \(U\) and a candidate user \(V\), we want to quantify their "connection potential."

The simplest metric is the Common Neighbors (CN) count:

$$CN(U, V) = |N(U) \cap N(V)|$$

This counts how many mutual friends \(U\) and \(V\) share. A higher count implies a stronger potential connection. This is often sufficient for basic PYMK systems.

Normalized Similarity: Jaccard Index

The Common Neighbors count can be biased towards users with many friends. The Jaccard Index normalizes this by the total number of unique friends between \(U\) and \(V\):

$$J(U, V) = \frac{|N(U) \cap N(V)|}{|N(U) \cup N(V)|}$$

This metric ranges from 0 (no common friends) to 1 (all friends are common), providing a relative measure of overlap. It's useful for comparing potential links in dense versus sparse parts of the network.

Weighting by Rarity: Adamic-Adar Index

Sometimes, a common friend who is very well-connected (a "hub") provides less unique information than a common friend who is less connected. The Adamic-Adar Index gives more weight to common neighbors who have fewer total connections themselves:

$$AA(U, V) = \sum_{Z \in N(U) \cap N(V)} \frac{1}{\log |N(Z)|}$$

Here, \(Z\) iterates over the common neighbors of \(U\) and \(V\). If \(Z\) has few neighbors, \(\log|N(Z)|\) is small, and \(1/\log|N(Z)|\) is large, boosting the score. This captures the intuition that "rare" mutual friends are stronger signals.

⚠️ Privacy & Ethical Concerns

PYMK algorithms must be carefully designed to avoid privacy breaches (e.g., suggesting someone's therapist or ex-partner) or reinforcing echo chambers. Filtering out certain connections or adding diversity constraints is crucial. Transparency about why a suggestion is made can also build trust.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use PYMK algorithms:

  • Social Network Growth: Essential for onboarding new users and expanding existing networks.
  • Content Discovery: Implicitly, shared connections can lead to recommendations for content, groups, or events.
  • Community Building: Helping users find others with similar interests or backgrounds, fostering stronger platform communities.
  • Professional Networking: Identifying relevant professional contacts (e.g., LinkedIn).

When to be careful / potential pitfalls:

  • Cold Start Problem: New users with few or no friends cannot receive effective suggestions based on graph structure alone. Requires fallback to other signals (e.g., demographics, uploaded contacts).
  • Sparse Graphs: In networks where users have very few connections, 2-hop traversal might yield few or no candidates.
  • Privacy & Ethics: As highlighted earlier, avoiding sensitive suggestions and ensuring transparency is paramount.
  • Computational Cost: For very large networks, efficient graph algorithms (e.g., approximate nearest neighbors, graph sampling) are needed to scale beyond simple 2-hop traversals.
💡 Big Picture

PYMK systems are a prime example of link prediction in graph machine learning. They demonstrate how simple graph traversal combined with similarity metrics can yield powerful, intuitive, and highly effective recommendations that drive user engagement and network value.

import networkx as nx
from collections import defaultdict
# Example Adjacency List (representing a social graph)
graph = {
'Alice': ['Bob', 'Charlie', 'David'],
'Bob': ['Alice', 'Eve', 'Heidi'],
'Charlie': ['Alice', 'Frank', 'Grace'],
'David': ['Alice', 'Heidi', 'Ivan'],
'Eve': ['Bob', 'Ivan'],
'Frank': ['Charlie', 'Judy'],
'Grace': ['Charlie', 'Kelly'],
'Heidi': ['Bob', 'David', 'Kelly'],
'Ivan': ['David', 'Eve', 'Liam'],
'Judy': ['Frank', 'Liam'],
'Kelly': ['Grace', 'Heidi'],
'Liam': ['Ivan', 'Judy'],
}
def get_pymk_suggestions(target_user, graph, min_common_friends=1):
# 1. Find direct friends of the target user
direct_friends = set(graph.get(target_user, []))
# 2. Find friends of friends (2-hop neighbors)
fof_candidates = defaultdict(int) # Stores candidate -> common_friends_count
for friend in direct_friends:
for fof in graph.get(friend, []):
# 3. Filter out target user and already-friends
if fof != target_user and fof not in direct_friends:
# 4. Count common friends (simple score)
fof_candidates[fof] += 1
# 5. Filter by minimum common friends threshold
filtered_suggestions = {
user: count for user, count in fof_candidates.items()
if count >= min_common_friends
}
# 6. Rank suggestions by common friends count (descending)
ranked_suggestions = sorted(
filtered_suggestions.items(), key=lambda item: item[1], reverse=True
)
return ranked_suggestions
