Understanding Information Retrieval (IR)
▼

What is Information Retrieval? At its core, IR is about finding relevant documents or information from a large collection in response to a user's query. Think search engines, recommendation systems, or even finding a specific email in your inbox. The challenge is to bridge the gap between a user's expressed need (query) and the vast amount of available information.

Historically, IR relied on keywords: matching terms between query and documents. Modern IR has evolved to understand semantic meaning, using advanced machine learning models to find conceptually similar information, even if exact keywords aren't present.

💡 The Core Problem

Given a query \(q\) and a collection of documents \(D = \{d_1, d_2, \dots, d_N\}\), the goal is to return a ranked list of documents from \(D\) that are most relevant to \(q\).

Evolution of IR Models:

  • Boolean Models: Simple keyword matching with AND/OR/NOT logic. Limited expressiveness.
  • Vector Space Models (VSM): Represent documents and queries as vectors in a high-dimensional space. Similarity (e.g., cosine) measures relevance. TF-IDF is a key component.
  • Probabilistic Models: Estimate the probability that a document is relevant to a query. BM25 is a prominent example.
  • Neural/Dense Retrieval: Use deep learning to embed queries and documents into a shared vector space, capturing semantic meaning. Examples include DPR, ColBERT.
  • Re-ranking: A two-stage approach where an initial (fast) retriever finds candidates, and a more complex (slower) model re-ranks them for higher precision.
2
Classical vs. Dense Retrieval
▼

1. Classical (Lexical) Retrieval: TF-IDF & BM25

These models rely on the exact words (tokens) shared between a query and a document. They score documents based on how often query terms appear in a document (Term Frequency) and how rare those terms are across the entire collection (Inverse Document Frequency).

  • TF (Term Frequency): How many times a term \(t\) appears in a document \(d\). More occurrences usually mean more relevance. Often normalized to prevent long documents from having an unfair advantage.
  • IDF (Inverse Document Frequency): Measures how unique or informative a term is. Terms appearing in many documents (like "the", "a") have low IDF, while rare terms have high IDF. This downweights common words.
  • BM25 (Best Match 25): An advanced probabilistic model that extends TF-IDF. It incorporates document length normalization more effectively and uses parameters (\(k_1, b\)) to tune the impact of term frequency and document length. It's highly effective and still widely used as a baseline.

2. Dense Retrieval (Semantic Retrieval)

Dense retrieval models overcome the limitations of lexical matching (e.g., synonymy, polysemy) by understanding the meaning of words and phrases. They achieve this by converting queries and documents into continuous vector representations called embeddings.

  • Embeddings: High-dimensional vectors where semantically similar words, phrases, or documents are mapped close to each other in the vector space. These are typically generated by large pre-trained language models (e.g., BERT, RoBERTa).
  • Similarity Search: Once queries and documents are embedded, relevance is determined by calculating the similarity between their vectors, most commonly using cosine similarity.
  • DPR (Dense Passage Retrieval): A prominent dense retrieval model that uses two separate BERT-like encoders (one for queries, one for documents) to generate embeddings.
  • ColBERT (Contextualized Late Interaction over BERT): An improvement that generates a vector for each token in a document/query and then computes similarity by summing up maximum similarities between query token embeddings and document token embeddings. This offers more fine-grained matching than a single document vector.

3. Re-ranking

Often, dense retrieval models are computationally expensive to run over an entire corpus. Re-ranking offers a practical solution:

  1. Candidate Generation (First Stage): A fast, efficient retriever (e.g., BM25 or a simpler dense model) quickly identifies a small set of top-K candidate documents (e.g., K=100).
  2. Re-ranking (Second Stage): A more powerful, often more computationally intensive, model then re-scores and re-ranks these K candidates to produce the final, highly relevant result list. This model can use richer features or more complex neural architectures.
🔑 Key Difference

Classical methods match keywords. Dense methods match meaning. Re-ranking combines speed with precision.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

TF-IDF Score

The TF-IDF score for a term \(t\) in a document \(d\) is the product of its Term Frequency and Inverse Document Frequency. The overall score for a query \(q\) and document \(d\) is often the sum of TF-IDF scores for all query terms present in \(d\).

$$ \operatorname{TF}(t, d) = \frac{\text{count}(t, d)}{\text{length}(d)} \\ \operatorname{IDF}(t, D) = \log \left( \frac{N}{\operatorname{df}(t)} \right) \\ \operatorname{TFIDF}(q, d) = \sum_{t \in q \cap d} \operatorname{TF}(t, d) \times \operatorname{IDF}(t, D) $$

Where \(\text{count}(t, d)\) is the raw count of term \(t\) in document \(d\), \(\text{length}(d)\) is the total number of terms in \(d\), \(N\) is the total number of documents in the collection, and \(\operatorname{df}(t)\) is the number of documents containing term \(t\).

BM25 Score

BM25 is a more sophisticated variant, incorporating saturation of term frequency and document length normalization. It's often written as:

$$ \operatorname{BM25}(q, d) = \sum_{t \in q \cap d} \operatorname{IDF}(t) \times \frac{\operatorname{TF}(t, d) \times (k_1 + 1)}{\operatorname{TF}(t, d) + k_1 \times \left(1 - b + b \times \frac{\operatorname{length}(d)}{\operatorname{avgdl}}\right)} $$

Here, \(\operatorname{TF}(t, d)\) is typically the raw count. \(k_1\) (usually between 1.2 and 2.0) controls the term frequency saturation: a higher \(k_1\) means TF has a stronger impact. \(b\) (usually around 0.75) controls the document length normalization: a higher \(b\) means longer documents are penalized more heavily if they contain the term less frequently than average. \(\operatorname{avgdl}\) is the average document length in the collection.

Dense Retrieval: Cosine Similarity

In dense retrieval, queries and documents are represented as vectors (embeddings). The relevance score is typically the cosine similarity between the query vector \(\mathbf{q}\) and the document vector \(\mathbf{d}\).

$$ \operatorname{Similarity}(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{d}) = \frac{\mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{d}}{\|\mathbf{q}\| \|\mathbf{d}\|} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^k q_i d_i}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^k q_i^2} \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^k d_i^2}} $$

This measures the cosine of the angle between the two vectors. A value of 1 means they point in the exact same direction (highly similar), 0 means they are orthogonal (no similarity), and -1 means they point in opposite directions (highly dissimilar).

🧮 Note on Logarithms

The logarithm in IDF helps to dampen the effect of raw document frequency, ensuring that terms appearing in very few documents don't completely dominate the score. Different bases (e.g., natural log, base 10, base 2) are used, but the principle is the same.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use each approach:

  • TF-IDF/BM25: Excellent baselines, fast, interpretable. Good for keyword-heavy queries or when semantic nuance isn't critical. Still widely used for initial retrieval in hybrid systems.
  • Dense Retrieval (DPR, ColBERT): Best for semantic matching, handling synonyms, and queries that don't share exact keywords with relevant documents. Ideal for complex, natural language queries. Computationally more expensive for indexing and retrieval.
  • Re-ranking: A practical solution for large corpora. Use a fast retriever to narrow down candidates, then a powerful (but slower) re-ranker for precision. This balances efficiency and effectiveness.

Choosing the right model:

  • Corpus Size: For very large corpora, efficiency is key. BM25 or efficient dense retrievers (like ANCE) are preferred for initial retrieval.
  • Query Type: Keyword-based vs. natural language questions. Dense models excel at the latter.
  • Performance Requirements: Latency constraints might favor simpler models or multi-stage pipelines.
  • Data Availability: Dense models require large amounts of training data (query-document pairs) to learn effective embeddings.
💡 The Future is Hybrid

Many state-of-the-art IR systems combine lexical and dense methods (e.g., a BM25 first stage followed by a dense re-ranker, or a hybrid scoring function). This leverages the strengths of both paradigms for robust and accurate search.

import numpy as np
from collections import Counter
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
from scipy.spatial.distance import cosine
# 1. Documents and Query
documents = [
"The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog.",
"A lazy cat sleeps on the mat.",
"Brown foxes are known for their agility."
]
query = "brown fox agility"
# Preprocessing: simple tokenization and lowercase
def tokenize(text):
return text.lower().split()
tokenized_docs = [tokenize(d) for d in documents]
tokenized_query = tokenize(query)
# 2. TF-IDF Calculation
vectorizer = TfidfVectorizer()
tfidf_matrix = vectorizer.fit_transform(documents)
query_vec = vectorizer.transform([query])
tfidf_scores = 1 - cosine(query_vec.toarray(), tfidf_matrix.toarray()) # Simplified similarity
# 3. BM25 Calculation (simplified example)
k1, b = 1.5, 0.75
doc_lengths = [len(d) for d in tokenized_docs]
avg_doc_length = sum(doc_lengths) / len(doc_lengths)
bm25_scores = []
for i, doc_tokens in enumerate(tokenized_docs):
score = 0
doc_tf = Counter(doc_tokens)
for term in tokenized_query:
if term in doc_tf:
tf = doc_tf[term]
idf = vectorizer.idf_[vectorizer.vocabulary_[term]] # Re-use IDF from TfidfVectorizer
numerator = tf * (k1 + 1)
denominator = tf + k1 * (1 - b + b * (doc_lengths[i] / avg_doc_length))
score += idf * (numerator / denominator)
bm25_scores.append(score)
# 4. Dense Retrieval (Conceptual)

# dense_scores = [1 - cosine(query_embedding, d_emb) for d_emb in doc_embeddings]
print("BM25 Scores:", bm25_scores)
