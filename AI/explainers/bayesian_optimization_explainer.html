1
Understanding the Concept
▼

What is this? Imagine you need to find the best recipe for a cake, but each test batch costs you $500. You can't afford to try every combination of ingredients — you must be strategic. Bayesian Optimization is the algorithm that decides which experiment to run next, based on everything learned so far. It keeps a "belief map" about what the unknown function might look like, then intelligently picks the most promising place to probe.

The key idea: Instead of evaluating a black-box function \(f(x)\) everywhere (expensive!), maintain a probabilistic surrogate model — a Gaussian Process (GP) — that encodes both a prediction of \(f\) and our uncertainty about that prediction. An acquisition function \(\alpha(x)\) then uses this belief to balance two competing goals: exploring uncertain regions (exploration) and exploiting regions that already look promising (exploitation). We evaluate \(f\) at \(x^* = \arg\max_x \alpha(x)\), update the GP, and repeat.

💡 If You Remember One Thing

Bayesian Optimization builds a cheap surrogate model of an expensive objective, then uses an acquisition function to decide where to sample next — always choosing the point that maximises the expected utility under the surrogate's uncertainty.

Real-world analogy: You're a gold prospector. After each dig you update your mental map: "I found a little gold here, a lot there, and I haven't dug over there yet." You pick your next dig site not just where gold is most likely, but also where your map is most uncertain — because that uncertainty could hide a jackpot. That is exactly what the acquisition function formalises.

Where it's used: Hyperparameter tuning for neural networks (AutoML), drug discovery (each molecule synthesis takes days), material science, robotics policy optimisation, A/B test design. Any setting where evaluations are expensive and gradients are unavailable.

How It Works — GP Surrogate + Acquisition Loop
▼

The Bayesian Optimization Loop

Every iteration of Bayesian Optimization proceeds in four stages:

  1. Fit surrogate: Given observations \(\mathcal{D}_t = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^t\), compute the GP posterior \(p(f \mid \mathcal{D}_t)\).
  2. Maximize acquisition: Solve \(x_{t+1} = \arg\max_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \alpha(x;\mathcal{D}_t)\) cheaply (gradient-based or grid, since the surrogate is analytic).
  3. Evaluate: Query the true objective \(y_{t+1} = f(x_{t+1}) + \varepsilon\).
  4. Update: Append \((x_{t+1},y_{t+1})\) to \(\mathcal{D}_t\), go to step 1.

The Gaussian Process Surrogate

A GP is a prior over functions: any finite collection of function values is jointly Gaussian. Conditioned on observations, the posterior at a test point \(x_*\) is:

$$\mu_*(x_*) = \mathbf{k}_*^\top (K + \sigma_n^2 I)^{-1} \mathbf{y}$$
$$\sigma_*^2(x_*) = k(x_*,x_*) - \mathbf{k}_*^\top (K + \sigma_n^2 I)^{-1} \mathbf{k}_*$$

where \(\mathbf{k}_* = [k(x_*,x_1),\ldots,k(x_*,x_t)]^\top\) is the cross-covariance vector, \(K_{ij}=k(x_i,x_j)\) is the kernel matrix, and \(\sigma_n^2\) is observation noise variance. The kernel \(k(x,x')\) encodes our prior beliefs about the function's smoothness.

Key Concept: RBF Kernel

The most common kernel is the squared exponential (RBF): \(k(x,x') = \sigma_f^2 \exp\!\bigl(-\frac{\|x-x'\|^2}{2\ell^2}\bigr)\). The lengthscale \(\ell\) controls smoothness — large \(\ell\) means the function varies slowly; \(\sigma_f^2\) is the signal variance (function's amplitude). Both are fitted by maximising the marginal likelihood.

Acquisition Functions — The Four Strategies

Expected Improvement (EI) — the workhorse. Let \(y^+ = \max_i y_i\) be the best observation so far. EI computes the expected amount by which we improve over \(y^+\):

$$\text{EI}(x) = \mathbb{E}[\max(0,\, f(x)-y^+)] = (\mu_*-y^+-\xi)\Phi(Z) + \sigma_*\phi(Z)$$

where \(Z=(\mu_*-y^+-\xi)/\sigma_*\), \(\Phi\) is the standard normal CDF, \(\phi\) is the PDF, and \(\xi\geq 0\) is an exploration bonus. EI = 0 whenever \(\sigma_*=0\).

Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) — from bandit theory. Optimistically assume the function equals its upper confidence boundary:

$$\text{UCB}(x) = \mu_*(x) + \kappa\,\sigma_*(x)$$

\(\kappa>0\) trades off exploitation (\(\kappa\to0\)) vs. exploration (\(\kappa\to\infty\)). Theoretical regret bounds exist for \(\kappa=\sqrt{2\log(t^2\pi^2/3\delta)}\).

Probability of Improvement (PI) — the oldest acquisition. Maximize the probability that the next point beats \(y^+\):

$$\text{PI}(x) = P(f(x) > y^+ + \xi) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{\mu_*(x)-y^+-\xi}{\sigma_*(x)}\right)$$

PI is greedier than EI — it doesn't reward large improvements, only the probability of any improvement. This can lead to exploitation of local optima.

Thompson Sampling (TS) — draw one sample from the GP posterior and maximize it:

$$x_{t+1} = \arg\max_{x} \tilde{f}(x), \quad \tilde{f} \sim \mathcal{GP}(\mu_*, \Sigma_*)$$

TS is trivially parallelizable (draw \(q\) samples, evaluate at all \(q\) argmaxima simultaneously). Empirically competitive with EI despite its simplicity.

⚠ Common Misconception

Bayesian Optimization is not gradient descent. The acquisition maximization inner loop uses gradients (or grid search), but the outer loop never uses \(\nabla_x f(x)\) — that's the whole point. BO is designed for black-box, derivative-free settings where evaluations are the bottleneck, not computation.

GP Posterior Derivation (Gaussian Conditioning)

Assume joint Gaussianity between \(f\) at training and test points:

$$\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{y} \\ f_*\end{pmatrix} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}\\\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}K+\sigma_n^2 I & \mathbf{k}_* \\ \mathbf{k}_*^\top & k_{**}\end{pmatrix}\right)$$

Applying the Gaussian conditioning formula \(p(A\mid B)=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{A|B}, \Sigma_{A|B})\):

$$\mu_*(x_*) = \mathbf{k}_*^\top K_y^{-1}\mathbf{y}, \qquad K_y = K+\sigma_n^2 I$$
$$\sigma_*^2(x_*) = k_{**} - \mathbf{k}_*^\top K_y^{-1}\mathbf{k}_*$$

Numerical Trace (Step 1 → Step 2)

Observations: \(X = \{0.5, 2.0, 4.5\}\), \(\mathbf{y} = [-0.128, 1.221, 1.679]\), kernel RBF \(\ell=1.2\), \(\sigma_f=1.5\), \(\sigma_n=0.05\). Test point \(x_*=3.5\).

Numerical Values

k(3.5, 0.5) = 1.5² · exp(−½(3/1.2)²) = 2.25·exp(−3.125) ≈ 0.099
k(3.5, 2.0) = 2.25·exp(−½(1.5/1.2)²) = 2.25·exp(−0.781) ≈ 1.028
k(3.5, 4.5) = 2.25·exp(−½(1/1.2)²) = 2.25·exp(−0.347) ≈ 1.599
μ*(3.5) ≈ 1.681 (posterior mean)
σ*(3.5) ≈ 0.868 (posterior std)

EI at \(x_*=3.5\) with \(y^+=1.679\), \(\xi=0.1\):

$$Z = \frac{\mu_* - y^+ - \xi}{\sigma_*} = \frac{1.681 - 1.679 - 0.1}{0.868} = -0.114$$
$$\text{EI}(3.5) = (1.681-1.679-0.1)\cdot\Phi(-0.114) + 0.868\cdot\phi(-0.114) \approx 0.300$$

This is the highest EI on the grid, so \(x^* = 3.5\). Evaluating \(f(3.5) = \sin(10.5) - 0.5(0.5)^2 + 2 = 1.948\), a new best.

Marginal Likelihood for Hyperparameter Tuning

Kernel hyperparameters \(\theta = \{\ell, \sigma_f, \sigma_n\}\) are fitted by maximising the log marginal likelihood:

$$\log p(\mathbf{y}\mid X,\theta) = -\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{y}^\top K_y^{-1}\mathbf{y} - \frac{1}{2}\log|K_y| - \frac{n}{2}\log 2\pi$$

The three terms trade off data fit, model complexity (determinant acts as regulariser), and normalisation. Gradients \(\partial\log p/\partial\theta\) are analytic, enabling L-BFGS optimisation of \(\theta\).

Code: GP Posterior from Scratch (Python/NumPy)

import numpy as np; from scipy.stats import norm
from scipy.linalg import solve, cho_factor, cho_solve
def rbf_kernel(X1, X2, l=1.2, sigma_f=1.5):
# Pairwise squared distances
dists = np.sum(X1[:,None,:]**2,2) + np.sum(X2[None,:,:]**2,2) - 2*X1@X2.T
return sigma_f**2 * np.exp(-0.5 * dists / l**2)
def gp_posterior(X_obs, y_obs, X_test, sigma_n=0.05):
K = rbf_kernel(X_obs, X_obs) + sigma_n**2 * np.eye(len(X_obs))
K_s = rbf_kernel(X_obs, X_test) # (n, m)
K_ss = rbf_kernel(X_test, X_test) # (m, m)
# Use Cholesky for stability: K = L L^T
cf = cho_factor(K)
alpha = cho_solve(cf, y_obs) # K^{-1} y
mu = K_s.T @ alpha # posterior mean
V = cho_solve(cf, K_s) # K^{-1} K_s
sigma2 = np.diag(K_ss - K_s.T @ V) # posterior variance
return mu, np.sqrt(np.maximum(sigma2, 1e-9))
def expected_improvement(mu, sigma, y_best, xi=0.01):
Z = (mu - y_best - xi) / sigma
return np.maximum(0, (mu - y_best - xi)*norm.cdf(Z) + sigma*norm.pdf(Z))
# BO loop
X_obs = np.array([[0.5],[2.0],[4.5]]); y_obs = f(X_obs).ravel()
for _ in range(10):
mu, sigma = gp_posterior(X_obs, y_obs, X_cand)
ei = expected_improvement(mu, sigma, y_obs.max())
x_next = X_cand[np.argmax(ei)]
y_next = f(x_next) # expensive evaluation
X_obs = np.vstack([X_obs, x_next]); y_obs = np.append(y_obs, y_next)

EI when you want a reliable default with little tuning. UCB when you need theoretical guarantees or want explicit exploration control. Key Takeaways & Interview Prep
▼

Summary

  • Bayesian Optimization maintains a GP surrogate that provides both a mean estimate and uncertainty quantification over the objective function.
  • The acquisition function formalises the exploration-exploitation trade-off by scoring candidate points using the surrogate's posterior.
  • The inner loop (maximise acquisition) is cheap; the outer loop (evaluate \(f\)) is expensive — BO spends compute in the right place.
  • GP kernel hyperparameters are tuned by maximising the marginal likelihood (no train/val split needed).
  • BO scales to ~20 dimensions; for higher dimensions use random embeddings (REMBO), sparse GPs, or neural-network surrogates (BONAS).

Interview Questions

Q1 (Medium): What is the exploration-exploitation trade-off in BO?

A: Exploitation means evaluating where \(\mu_*(x)\) is highest (we think the optimum is there). Exploration means evaluating where \(\sigma_*(x)\) is high (we're uncertain). EI and UCB both encode this: EI via the \(\sigma_*\phi(Z)\) term, UCB via \(\kappa\sigma_*\). Setting \(\kappa=0\) or \(\xi\to\infty\) collapses to pure exploitation or exploration respectively.

Q2 (Hard): Why use EI instead of simply maximising the posterior mean?

A: Maximising \(\mu_*\) is pure exploitation — it always picks the point the GP thinks is best. But \(\mu_*\) can be very wrong in unexplored regions; the GP's uncertainty \(\sigma_*\) is not factored in. EI accounts for the probability that the true function value (drawn from the GP posterior) exceeds the current best — naturally including uncertain but potentially excellent regions.

Q3 (Hard): What is the computational bottleneck in BO and how is it addressed?

A: GP inference requires \(O(n^3)\) for the Cholesky factorisation of \(K\) (n = number of observations). As \(n\) grows (many evaluations), this becomes expensive. Solutions: (1) Sparse GPs with inducing points reduce to \(O(nm^2)\) where \(m \ll n\); (2) Deep kernel learning (DKL) — a neural net feature extractor feeds into a GP for scalability + expressiveness; (3) Bayesian Neural Networks as surrogates (DNGO, BONAS).

Q4 (Hard): How does Thompson Sampling relate to posterior sampling?

A: TS draws a function sample \(\tilde{f} \sim \mathcal{GP}(\mu_*, K_*)\). Sampling a GP function requires a Cholesky decomposition of the test covariance: \(\tilde{f} = \mu_* + L_*\mathbf{z}\) where \(L_*L_*^\top = K_*\) and \(\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\). In continuous domains, we approximate by sampling on a fine grid or using random Fourier features (Rahimi & Recht 2007) to get \(O(m)\) samples cheaply.

Common Pitfalls
  • Forgetting to normalise inputs and outputs — GP kernels are sensitive to scale; always standardise \(y\) and bound \(x\) to \([0,1]^d\).
  • Not refitting kernel hyperparameters — running BO with fixed \(\ell, \sigma_f\) from the start leads to a misspecified surrogate.
  • PI in practice — PI is greedy and exploitative; for real hyperparameter tuning EI almost always outperforms PI.
  • Assuming BO works in d > 20 — GPs suffer from the curse of dimensionality; the RBF kernel becomes uninformative at high dimension.
