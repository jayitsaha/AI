Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) & Spectrogram

Watching frequency content change over time — one windowed FFT at a time
Foundational Signal Processing Speech / Audio O(L log L) per frame
1
Understanding the STFT
▼

What is it? A plain Fourier Transform tells you which frequencies are present in a signal, but not when they occurred — it blends the whole recording into one static chord. The Short-Time Fourier Transform fixes this by chopping the signal into short overlapping frames, tapering each frame with a window, and running a separate FFT on every frame. Stack the resulting magnitude spectra side by side and you get a spectrogram: a 2-D image with time on one axis, frequency on the other, and color/brightness showing energy.

Analogy: A full-signal FFT is like taking one long-exposure photo of a firework show — every burst is smeared into a single blurry frame. The STFT is a video of the same show: press play and you watch each burst appear, peak, and fade in its own frame. You trade a little bit of blur in each still frame for the ability to see motion.

💡 If you remember one thing

The STFT is just "slide a window, multiply, FFT, repeat." Formally: $$X(m,k)=\sum_{n=0}^{L-1} x(n+mH)\,w(n)\,e^{-i2\pi kn/L}$$ where \(w(n)\) is the window (e.g. Hann), \(L\) is the window length, \(H\) is the hop size, and \(m\) indexes the frame. The spectrogram is \(|X(m,k)|^2\) (or \(|X(m,k)|\)) plotted as an image.

2
How It Works Internally
▼

Four Repeated Steps

1) Frame extraction — grab \(L\) consecutive samples starting at sample \(mH\) (frame \(m\), hop \(H\)). 2) Windowing — multiply the frame by a taper \(w(n)\), almost always a Hann window \(w(n)=0.5(1-\cos(2\pi n/(L-1)))\), which fades the edges of the frame smoothly to zero. 3) FFT — take the Discrete Fourier Transform of the windowed frame, giving a complex spectrum with magnitude (energy per frequency) and phase. 4) Stack — store the magnitude column at time index \(m\); slide forward by \(H\) samples and repeat until the window reaches the end of the signal.

Design Decision: Why Window at All?

Chopping a signal into a frame with hard edges is equivalent to multiplying by a rectangular window — and a rectangle's FFT has huge, slowly-decaying side-lobes ("spectral leakage") that smear energy across unrelated frequencies. The Hann window's smooth taper trades a slightly wider main lobe (a bit less sharp) for dramatically lower side-lobes (far less leakage) — almost always a better trade for real audio and speech.

Overlap and the Hop Size

Frames almost always overlap (hop \(H <\) window length \(L\), commonly 50–75% overlap) so that the Hann taper's fade-out at one frame's edge is compensated by the next frame's fade-in — this is what makes overlap-add reconstruction (inverse STFT) possible, and it also produces the smooth, blur-free spectrogram images you see in speech and music tools.

🔑 Key Property

Every column of a spectrogram is an independent, ordinary DFT — the "short-time" part comes entirely from windowing and hopping before the FFT, not from any new transform. The FFT itself doesn't know about time; the STFT gives it time back by chunking.

3
Why It Works — The Time-Frequency Tradeoff
▼

Two Resolutions, One Knob

Choosing the window length \(L\) sets both resolutions at once. The frequency resolution (how close two tones can be and still be told apart) is

$$\Delta f \approx \frac{f_s}{L}$$

while the time resolution (how precisely you can localize a sudden change) is roughly the window's duration

$$\Delta t \approx \frac{L}{f_s}$$

Multiply them together and \(L\) cancels: \(\Delta t \cdot \Delta f \approx 1\). This is a discrete cousin of the Gabor–Heisenberg uncertainty principle — you cannot make a window simultaneously arbitrarily short in time and arbitrarily narrow in frequency. Shrinking \(L\) sharpens "when," but blurs "what frequency." Growing \(L\) does the opposite.

Why the FFT Makes This Cheap

A direct DFT of a length-\(L\) frame costs \(O(L^2)\); the Fast Fourier Transform computes the identical result in \(O(L\log L)\) by recursively splitting even/odd indexed samples (Cooley–Tukey). Across a whole recording of \(N\) samples with hop \(H\), the total STFT cost is \(O\!\left(\frac{N}{H}L\log L\right)\) — linear in signal length, which is why spectrograms can be computed in real time.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

There is no "best" window length — only a best length for a task. Speech recognition typically uses ~20–25 ms windows (good time resolution for phonemes); pitch/tonal analysis often wants 50–100 ms+ windows (good frequency resolution for closely-spaced harmonics). Picking one blindly can hide the very feature you're trying to detect.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to reach for it:

  • Any signal whose frequency content changes over time — speech, music, vibration/fault monitoring, ECG/EEG, radar chirps
  • As the front-end feature extractor for Mel-spectrograms / MFCCs feeding ASR, TTS, and audio classification models
  • Visual inspection of transients (onsets, sweeps, clicks) that a single global FFT would hide

Choosing the window: short window (few ms) → crisp in time, blurry in frequency, good for transients; long window (tens–hundreds of ms) → crisp in frequency, blurry in time, good for stable tones/harmonics. 50–75% overlap keeps the image smooth and supports perfect reconstruction via overlap-add.

💡 Where it leads next

Because one fixed window length can't be optimal everywhere, the Constant-Q Transform and wavelets use frequency-dependent window lengths (short at high frequencies, long at low frequencies) to sidestep the STFT's fixed resolution — the natural next topic after mastering the spectrogram.

import numpy as np
def hann(L):
n = np.arange(L)
return 0.5 * (1 - np.cos(2*np.pi*n/(L-1)))
def stft(x, win_len=400, hop=160):
w = hann(win_len)
n_frames = 1 + (len(x) - win_len) // hop
out = np.zeros((n_frames, win_len//2 + 1), dtype=complex)
for m in range(n_frames):
start = m * hop
frame = x[start:start+win_len] * w # window the frame
out[m] = np.fft.rfft(frame) # FFT -> one column
return out
