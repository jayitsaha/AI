1
Understanding Online Serving
▼

What is it? Online serving is the path a single user request takes, live, to get a personalized answer — a feed, a search-result list, a recommendation — inside a strict time budget, usually 50–150ms end to end. It is the machinery that turns "millions of candidate items" into "the 10 items you actually see," on demand, at massive concurrency.

Analogy: Think of a restaurant that must serve a full custom-tailored meal in under two minutes, for thousands of tables at once, from a warehouse of a million ingredients. You can't cook from scratch (too slow) — you pre-chop what you can (caching, precomputation), narrow the menu fast with a coarse filter (retrieval), then spend your remaining time budget carefully plating the few dishes that matter most (ranking).

💡 If you remember one thing

Online serving is a funnel under a latency budget: cheap, coarse retrieval narrows millions of candidates to hundreds, then an expensive, precise ranking model scores only those survivors. Every stage trades accuracy for milliseconds, and the budget — not accuracy — is usually the binding constraint.

2
How the Serving Stack Works Internally
▼

The Six Stages

A request enters a load balancer, which routes it to a healthy replica (~2ms). That replica pulls features for the user and context — a mix of an online feature store (Redis/DynamoDB-class key-value store) and a request-scoped cache; a cache hit costs single-digit ms, a miss can cost 30–80ms because it fans out to multiple backing stores. Next, candidate retrieval uses an Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) index (HNSW/IVF-PQ) to shrink millions of items down to a few hundred candidates in ~10–15ms — trading a small amount of recall for a huge latency win over exact search. Those candidates are scored by a ranking model (a deep network, batched on GPU/CPU) — the single most expensive stage, 20–50ms, because it runs a forward pass per candidate. A lightweight business re-rank layer then applies rules — diversity, deduplication, freshness boosts, ads — for a few ms. Finally the response is serialized and returned.

Design Decision: Why Two-Stage (Retrieve then Rank)?

Running the full ranking model over every item in the catalog is the accurate approach but is computationally infeasible at low latency — O(catalog size) forward passes. Two-stage funneling makes the expensive model's cost O(few hundred) instead, by first using a cheap, approximate method (ANN over embeddings, or simple heuristics) to discard the vast majority of irrelevant items.

🔑 Key Property

Latency composes additively down the pipeline, but caching and precomputation let you move work offline — anything computed ahead of time (embeddings, feature aggregates, popular-query results) does not count against the online budget at all.

3
Why It Works — The Latency Math
▼

Sequential Composition

If a request passes through \(k\) sequential stages with latencies \(T_1,\dots,T_k\), the total latency is simply their sum:

$$T_{total} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} T_i$$

Means add cleanly. Percentiles do not. If each stage's p99 happens independently, the p99 of the sum is generally less than the sum of the p99s — but in the worst case (fully correlated tails), it can approach the sum. System designers budget for the pessimistic bound:

$$P_{99}(T_{total}) \;\le\; \sum_{i=1}^{k} P_{99}(T_i)$$

Cache-Blended Latency

For a stage with cache hit ratio \(h\), hit latency \(T_{hit}\), and miss latency \(T_{miss}\), the expected latency is a convex blend:

$$\mathbb{E}[T_{fetch}] = h \cdot T_{hit} + (1-h)\cdot T_{miss}$$

Because \(T_{miss} \gg T_{hit}\) (often 10–20×), even modest improvements in \(h\) produce large latency wins — this is why cache hit ratio is one of the most-watched metrics in serving systems.

Throughput via Little's Law

For a replica handling requests with concurrency \(C\) (in-flight requests) and mean service time \(T_s\), sustainable throughput is:

$$\lambda_{max} = \frac{C}{T_s}$$

Total system capacity is \(\min(n \cdot \lambda_{max}, \, \text{Capacity}_{bottleneck})\) — scaling replicas \(n\) helps only until a shared downstream resource (feature store, ANN index, DB) saturates.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

Teams optimize mean latency and ship a system whose p99 blows the SLA. A single slow stage (e.g. a cache miss cascading into a cold feature fetch) can dominate the tail even when it's rare, because user-perceived latency is dominated by the tail, not the average.

Key Takeaways
▼

Design principles for online serving:

  • Fund the latency budget top-down: set a p99 SLA first, then allocate ms to each stage — don't design stages in isolation and hope they fit.
  • Move as much work offline as possible: precompute embeddings, feature aggregates, and popular results so the online path only does the minimum live work.
  • Two-stage retrieve-then-rank keeps the expensive model's cost bounded regardless of catalog size.
  • Cache hit ratio is a first-class latency lever — small drops in hit ratio cause outsized p99 regressions.
  • Horizontal scaling (more replicas) only helps until a shared bottleneck (feature store, ANN index) saturates — then you must shard or cache further, not just add replicas.
💡 Budget Discipline

A useful rule of thumb: reserve roughly 20–30% of your total p99 budget as slack for network, GC pauses, and tail variance you haven't measured yet — a system tuned to exactly hit budget on paper rarely does so in production.
