Understanding Jordan Normal Form
▼

What is it? Every square matrix can be diagonalized — broken into a clean diagonal of eigenvalues — unless it has repeated eigenvalues with too few eigenvectors (a defective matrix). Jordan Normal Form (JNF) is the next best thing: the "almost-diagonal" canonical form that every matrix, diagonalizable or not, can be brought into. Instead of a pure diagonal, you get a block-diagonal matrix where each block has the eigenvalue on the diagonal and 1s just above (the superdiagonal).

Analogy: Imagine you're trying to sort a bookshelf perfectly — one book per slot (diagonalization). But some books are physically glued together in chains; you can't separate them. Jordan Form says: fine, keep the chains together, each chain becomes a Jordan block. The "1 on the superdiagonal" encodes which books are stuck to which.

If you remember one thing

Every \(n\times n\) matrix \(A\) over \(\mathbb{C}\) satisfies \(A = P J P^{-1}\) where \(J\) is block-diagonal with Jordan blocks \(J_k(\lambda) = \lambda I + N_k\) (\(N_k\) is the nilpotent shift). The blocks encode both eigenvalues and the failure of diagonalizability. \(P\)'s columns are the generalized eigenvectors forming Jordan chains.

Key vocabulary

  • Algebraic multiplicity of \(\lambda\): how many times \(\lambda\) appears as a root of \(\det(A - \lambda I)\)
  • Geometric multiplicity of \(\lambda\): \(\dim\ker(A - \lambda I)\) = number of Jordan blocks for \(\lambda\)
  • Defective matrix: geometric multiplicity < algebraic multiplicity for some eigenvalue
  • Jordan chain: sequence \(v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_k\) where \((A-\lambda I)v_j = v_{j-1}\), \((A-\lambda I)v_1 = 0\)

Jordan Blocks and Generalized Eigenvectors
▼

Anatomy of a Jordan block

A Jordan block of size \(k\) for eigenvalue \(\lambda\) is:

$$J_k(\lambda) = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & \cdots & 0 & \lambda & 1 \\ 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 & \lambda \end{bmatrix} = \lambda I_k + N_k$$

where \(N_k\) is the \(k\times k\) nilpotent shift matrix with 1s on the superdiagonal and 0s elsewhere. Note \(N_k^k = 0\) but \(N_k^{k-1} \neq 0\).

The full Jordan form

The Jordan Normal Form of an \(n\times n\) matrix is block-diagonal:

$$J = \begin{bmatrix} J_{k_1}(\lambda_1) & & \\ & J_{k_2}(\lambda_2) & \\ & & \ddots \end{bmatrix}$$

The sizes \(k_1, k_2, \ldots\) partition \(n\). The number of blocks for each \(\lambda\) equals its geometric multiplicity. The total size of all blocks for \(\lambda\) equals its algebraic multiplicity.

Generalized eigenvectors — building Jordan chains

For each Jordan block of size \(k\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda\), there is a Jordan chain of generalized eigenvectors \(v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_k\) satisfying:

$$(A - \lambda I)v_1 = 0, \quad (A-\lambda I)v_j = v_{j-1} \text{ for } j = 2,\ldots,k$$

\(v_1\) is a true eigenvector; \(v_2, \ldots, v_k\) are generalized eigenvectors. Equivalently, \(v_j \in \ker(A-\lambda I)^j \setminus \ker(A-\lambda I)^{j-1}\).

The columns of \(P = [v_1\;v_2\;\cdots\;v_k\;\cdots]\) form the similarity matrix: \(P^{-1}AP = J\).

Key distinction: diagonalizable vs defective

A matrix is diagonalizable when every Jordan block is \(1\times1\) (all 1s on superdiagonal disappear). Defective matrices have at least one block of size \(\geq 2\). The number of distinct Jordan block sizes for each eigenvalue is determined uniquely — JNF is unique up to block ordering.

3
Why It Works — The Mathematics
▼

Cayley-Hamilton and the minimal polynomial

Every matrix satisfies its own characteristic polynomial \(p(\lambda) = \det(\lambda I - A)\). The minimal polynomial \(m(\lambda)\) is the monic polynomial of least degree with \(m(A) = 0\). Its factorization reveals Jordan structure:

$$m(\lambda) = \prod_i (\lambda - \lambda_i)^{r_i}$$

where \(r_i\) is the size of the largest Jordan block for \(\lambda_i\). The characteristic polynomial uses algebraic multiplicities instead.

Invariant factor decomposition

JNF is closely related to the rational canonical form via the Smith Normal Form of \((xI - A)\) over \(\mathbb{Q}[x]\). The elementary divisors \((\lambda - \lambda_i)^{k_j}\) correspond exactly to Jordan blocks.

Key spectral mapping results

Since \(A = PJP^{-1}\), functions of \(A\) become functions of \(J\), computable block-by-block:

$$e^{tA} = P\,e^{tJ}\,P^{-1}, \qquad e^{tJ_k(\lambda)} = e^{\lambda t}\begin{bmatrix}1 & t & \frac{t^2}{2!} & \cdots & \frac{t^{k-1}}{(k-1)!} \\ 0 & 1 & t & \cdots & \frac{t^{k-2}}{(k-2)!} \\ \vdots & & \ddots & & \vdots \\ 0 & & & 1 & t \\ 0 & & & 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}$$

This is why JNF is central to solving linear ODEs: \(\dot{x} = Ax\) has solution \(x(t) = e^{tA}x_0\), and defective matrices produce polynomial-×-exponential modes like \(t e^{\lambda t}\) or \(t^2 e^{\lambda t}\).

Numerical warning

JNF is notoriously ill-conditioned. A defective matrix can be made diagonalizable by an arbitrarily small perturbation. In floating-point arithmetic, you should never compute JNF directly — instead use Schur decomposition (\(A = Q T Q^*\), \(T\) triangular) or the Singular Value Decomposition, both of which are numerically stable.

Existence proof sketch

The proof proceeds by induction on \(n\). For the inductive step, pick any eigenvalue \(\lambda\) and decompose \(\mathbb{C}^n = \ker(A-\lambda I)^n \oplus \text{(invariant complement)}\). The restriction of \(A\) to the generalized eigenspace is \(\lambda I + N\) with \(N\) nilpotent; a nilpotent matrix has JNF with all-zero diagonal and 1s on superdiagonal, which is itself. Apply Jordan decomposition theorem for nilpotent matrices (via the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a PID). This gives uniqueness of block sizes.

Key Takeaways & When to Use
▼

JNF tells you everything about a matrix's structure:

  • Number of Jordan blocks for \(\lambda\) = geometric multiplicity = number of linearly independent eigenvectors
  • Largest block size for \(\lambda\) = degree of \((\lambda - \mu)^k\) in minimal polynomial
  • Sum of block sizes for \(\lambda\) = algebraic multiplicity
  • \(A\) diagonalizable \(\Leftrightarrow\) all Jordan blocks are \(1\times1\) \(\Leftrightarrow\) minimal poly has no repeated roots

Where JNF appears in practice

  • ODEs: \(\dot{x}=Ax\) — defective matrices produce \(t e^{\lambda t}, t^2 e^{\lambda t}\) modes
  • Control theory: Jordan structure determines controllability and observability
  • Markov chains: Jordan form of transition matrix describes long-run behavior
  • Graph theory: Nilpotent adjacency matrices have pure Jordan structure revealing path structure
  • ML: Recurrent networks — defective Jacobians produce polynomial growth in gradients
Diagonalizable vs Defective: the hierarchy

Diagonal \(D\) (all distinct eigenvalues) ⊂ Diagonalizable (\(P^{-1}AP = D\)) ⊂ All matrices (\(P^{-1}AP = J\), JNF). The Jordan form is the universal canonical form for similarity. Over \(\mathbb{R}\), use real Jordan form (2×2 blocks for complex conjugate pairs); over \(\mathbb{C}\), JNF always exists.

Code Trace — Computing Jordan Form
▼
import numpy as np
from sympy import Matrix, jordan_form, symbols
# Defective 3x3: eigenvalue 5 with multiplicity 3, only 1 eigenvector
A = np.array([[5., 1., 0.],
[0., 5., 1.],
[0., 0., 5.]])
# Nilpotent part N = A - 5I
N = A - 5 * np.eye(3) # [[0,1,0],[0,0,1],[0,0,0]]
N2 = N @ N # [[0,0,1],[0,0,0],[0,0,0]]
N3 = N2 @ N # [[0,0,0],[0,0,0],[0,0,0]] — nilpotency index 3
# Jordan chains (columns of P)
v1 = np.array([1., 0., 0.]) # eigenvector: (A-5I)v1 = 0
v2 = np.array([0., 1., 0.]) # rank-2: (A-5I)v2 = v1
v3 = np.array([0., 0., 1.]) # rank-3: (A-5I)v3 = v2
P = np.column_stack([v1, v2, v3]) # similarity matrix
# Verify: P^-1 A P = J
J = np.linalg.inv(P) @ A @ P
# J ≈ [[5,1,0],[0,5,1],[0,0,5]] — the Jordan block!
# Matrix exponential via Jordan: e^(tA) = P e^(tJ) P^-1
# e^(tJ_3(5)) = e^(5t) * [[1,t,t²/2],[0,1,t],[0,0,1]]
def mat_exp_jordan_3(t, lam=5.0):
return np.exp(lam*t) * np.array([[1, t, t**2/2],
[0, 1, t ],
[0, 0, 1 ]])
# SymPy gives exact JNF (numerical is unreliable for defective)
A_sym = Matrix([[5,1,0],[0,5,1],[0,0,5]])
P_sym, J_sym = A_sym.jordan_form()
print(J_sym) # Matrix([[5, 1, 0], [0, 5, 1], [0, 0, 5]])
