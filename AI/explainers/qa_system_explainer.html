Understanding QA Systems
▼

What is it? A Question Answering (QA) system is a type of artificial intelligence that can understand a question posed in natural language and retrieve a precise answer from a given text corpus or knowledge base. Unlike traditional search engines that return a list of relevant documents, a QA system aims to provide a direct answer, often a specific span of text.

Analogy: Imagine you're in a library, but instead of finding books, you're looking for a specific sentence. A traditional search engine would hand you a stack of books that *might* contain the sentence. A QA system is like a super-librarian who not only finds the right book but also opens it to the correct page and points directly to the exact sentence that answers your question.

💡 If you remember one thing

Modern extractive QA systems typically follow a two-stage pipeline: first, a retriever quickly narrows down a large corpus to a few relevant passages; then, a more powerful reader (extractor) meticulously scans those passages to pinpoint the exact answer span. This separation of concerns balances speed and accuracy.

2
How It Works Internally
▼

The Two-Stage Pipeline

Most high-performance extractive QA systems use a two-step process to efficiently handle large document collections:

  1. Document/Passage Retrieval: Given a user's question, this component quickly sifts through a vast corpus (e.g., all of Wikipedia) to identify a small subset of documents or passages that are most likely to contain the answer. This is often done using efficient semantic search or keyword-based methods (like TF-IDF or BM25), or more recently, dense retrieval models that embed both questions and passages into a shared vector space.
  2. Answer Extraction (Reading Comprehension): The selected passages are then passed to a more sophisticated "reader" model. This model, typically a Transformer-based Large Language Model (LLM) fine-tuned for reading comprehension (e.g., BERT, RoBERTa), analyzes each passage in detail along with the question to locate and extract the precise span of text that constitutes the answer. It predicts a start and end token for the answer within the passage.

This pipeline avoids running the computationally expensive reader model over the entire corpus, which would be prohibitively slow.

Design Decision: Extractive vs. Generative QA

Extractive QA (the focus here) finds the answer verbatim within the provided text. It's robust to hallucination and ensures factual accuracy *if* the answer is present. However, it cannot synthesize answers or respond to questions requiring inference beyond direct text. Generative QA, on the other hand, uses models like GPT-3/4 to generate free-form answers based on their internal knowledge or retrieved context. While more flexible, generative models can sometimes "hallucinate" incorrect information or struggle with complex reasoning without proper grounding.

🔑 Key Property

The effectiveness of a QA system hinges on both its retriever and its reader. A poor retriever will miss relevant passages, making it impossible for even a perfect reader to find the answer. A weak reader will fail to extract the correct span even from highly relevant passages. Both components must be optimized in tandem.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Retrieval: Vector Similarity

In modern dense retrieval, both the question \(q\) and passages \(p_j\) are encoded into fixed-size dense vectors (embeddings) by neural networks. Relevance is then determined by vector similarity, often cosine similarity:

$$\text{Sim}(q, p_j) = \frac{\mathbf{v}_q \cdot \mathbf{v}_{p_j}}{\|\mathbf{v}_q\| \|\mathbf{v}_{p_j}\|}$$

A higher similarity score indicates greater semantic relevance. Passages are then ranked by this score, and a threshold is applied to select the top-K or top-N most relevant passages.

Extraction: Span Prediction Logits

For answer extraction, a reader model (e.g., BERT) takes the question and a passage as input. It then outputs two scores for every token \(t_k\) in the passage:

  1. A "start" logit \(s_k\) indicating the likelihood that \(t_k\) is the beginning of the answer.
  2. An "end" logit \(e_k\) indicating the likelihood that \(t_k\) is the end of the answer.

These logits are typically combined for all possible spans \((t_i, \dots, t_j)\) where \(i \le j\):

$$\text{Score}(i, j) = s_i + e_j$$

The model is trained to maximize the probability of the correct span. During inference, the span \((i, j)\) with the highest score (and \(i \le j\), usually with a maximum span length constraint) is chosen. A common training loss is the sum of log-likelihoods for the correct start and end positions:

$$L = -\log P(\text{start} = i^*) - \log P(\text{end} = j^*)$$

where \(P(\text{start} = i)\) is computed by applying softmax over all start logits, and similarly for the end logits. The model predicts the answer span \((i^*, j^*)\) that maximizes \(s_i + e_j\).

⚠️ Common Pitfall

A major challenge is when the answer is not present in any of the retrieved passages. Extractive QA models typically don't have a direct mechanism to say "I don't know." They will always try to find *some* span, even if it's irrelevant or nonsensical. Advanced techniques involve predicting a "no answer" special token or using a confidence threshold to filter low-scoring answers.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use QA systems:

  • When users need precise answers, not just links to documents (e.g., customer support chatbots, internal knowledge bases).
  • For tasks requiring information extraction from unstructured text (e.g., legal document analysis, scientific literature review).
  • To build sophisticated search interfaces that understand natural language queries.

When NOT to use (or use with caution):

  • When the answer is highly subjective or requires complex reasoning beyond what's explicitly stated in the text (generative QA or human intelligence may be better).
  • With very small or low-quality corpora, where the chance of finding a correct answer is low.
  • When the cost of a wrong (but confidently extracted) answer is very high — consider adding "no answer" prediction and human-in-the-loop validation.
💡 Future Directions

Hybrid QA systems are emerging, combining the strengths of extractive and generative approaches. They might use an extractive step to retrieve relevant context, then feed that context to a generative LLM to synthesize a more nuanced answer, potentially with citations back to the source text for verifiability.
