Understanding the Conjugate Gradient Method
▼

What is it? The Conjugate Gradient (CG) method solves a system of linear equations \(Ax = b\) — or equivalently, minimizes a quadratic objective \(f(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^\top Ax - b^\top x\) — where \(A\) is symmetric positive-definite. It does so in at most \(n\) steps for an \(n\)-dimensional problem, without ever storing the full matrix inverse or the Hessian.

Analogy: Imagine finding the lowest point in a valley (a bowl-shaped landscape). Steepest descent always walks straight downhill — but it keeps zig-zagging between the walls of the valley. CG instead picks each new direction to be "independent" (in a precise algebraic sense) of every direction it has already used, so it makes progress in each new dimension exactly once. In a 2D bowl, two steps suffice.

Why does this matter for ML? Solving linear systems appears everywhere: Newton's method for optimizing neural networks involves \(Ax = g\) where \(A\) is the Hessian; Gaussian Process inference needs \(K^{-1}y\); solving normal equations in ridge regression. CG is the workhorse for large-scale linear algebra when \(A\) is too large to factorize directly.

💡 If you remember one thing

CG builds a sequence of A-conjugate search directions \(\{d_0, d_1, \ldots\}\) satisfying \(d_i^\top A d_j = 0\) for \(i \neq j\). Each step optimally reduces error in one new conjugate direction — guaranteeing convergence in \(\leq n\) steps — while only requiring matrix-vector products, never the full inverse.

2
How CG Works Internally
▼

The Core Idea: A-Conjugate Directions

Two vectors \(d_i\) and \(d_j\) are A-conjugate (A-orthogonal) if \(d_i^\top A d_j = 0\). For the identity matrix \(A=I\), this reduces to ordinary orthogonality. When \(A\) captures the curvature of our objective, A-conjugacy means "making progress in \(d_i\) does not undo progress in \(d_j\)."

Steepest descent picks \(d_k = r_k\) (the residual) at every step — no memory of past directions. This causes the notorious zig-zagging: fixing one component of the error re-introduces error in directions already corrected. CG fixes this by accumulating past direction information via the scalar \(\beta_k\).

The Four-Line Algorithm

Algorithm (Hestenes-Stiefel, 1952)

Initialize: \(r_0 = b - Ax_0,\quad d_0 = r_0\)
For k = 0, 1, 2, …:
  \(\alpha_k = \dfrac{r_k^\top r_k}{d_k^\top A d_k}\)  (step length — exact line minimum)
  \(x_{k+1} = x_k + \alpha_k d_k\)
  \(r_{k+1} = r_k - \alpha_k A d_k\)
  \(\beta_k = \dfrac{r_{k+1}^\top r_{k+1}}{r_k^\top r_k}\)  (Polak-Ribière = Fletcher-Reeves here)
  \(d_{k+1} = r_{k+1} + \beta_k d_k\)  (build conjugate direction)

Why Only One Matrix-Vector Product Per Step?

Each iteration requires one product \(Ad_k\) and two dot products. Crucially, the residual update \(r_{k+1} = r_k - \alpha_k Ad_k\) reuses the same \(Ad_k\) already computed for \(\alpha_k\). For sparse or structured \(A\), this is \(\mathcal{O}(n)\) or \(\mathcal{O}(n \log n)\) — far cheaper than direct factorization at \(\mathcal{O}(n^3)\).

🔑 Key Invariant

At iteration \(k\), the CG iterate \(x_k\) is the exact minimizer of \(f(x)\) over the Krylov subspace \(\mathcal{K}_k(A,r_0) = \text{span}\{r_0, Ar_0, A^2r_0, \ldots, A^{k-1}r_0\}\). As \(k\) grows, this subspace expands until it spans all of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) — at which point \(x_k = x^*\) exactly.

⚠️ When Exact CG Fails

In finite-precision arithmetic, A-conjugacy of directions is gradually lost due to rounding errors (loss of orthogonality among residuals). In practice CG is run iteratively beyond \(n\) steps, or with preconditioning (replacing \(A\) with \(M^{-1}A\) where \(M \approx A\)) to cluster eigenvalues and accelerate convergence.

The Mathematical Foundations
▼

Equivalence: Linear System ↔ Quadratic Minimization

For SPD \(A\), solving \(Ax^* = b\) is identical to minimizing \(f(x) = \frac{1}{2}x^\top Ax - b^\top x\). The gradient \(\nabla f(x) = Ax - b = -r\) vanishes exactly at the solution.

A-Conjugate Basis Construction

The directions are built via a three-term recurrence that enforces A-orthogonality. Starting from \(d_0 = r_0\):

$$d_{k+1} = r_{k+1} + \frac{\|r_{k+1}\|^2}{\|r_k\|^2} d_k$$

This is the Fletcher-Reeves formula (equivalent to Hestenes-Stiefel here since residuals are mutually orthogonal in exact arithmetic). One can show by induction that \(d_i^\top A d_j = 0\) for all \(i \neq j\), and \(r_k \perp r_j\) for \(j < k\).

Convergence Guarantee

Let \(\kappa = \lambda_{\max}(A)/\lambda_{\min}(A)\) be the condition number. The energy-norm error satisfies:

$$\frac{\|e_k\|_A}{\|e_0\|_A} \leq 2\left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}\right)^k$$

where \(\|e\|_A = \sqrt{e^\top A e}\). For our example with \(\kappa \approx 1.94\): the CG contraction ratio per step is \(\approx 0.164\) vs steepest descent's \(\approx 0.319\). More dramatically: for \(\kappa = 100\), CG needs \(\approx 10\) iterations vs SD's \(\approx 450\) for the same precision.

Exact Convergence in n Steps (Chebyshev Polynomial Argument)

The error after \(k\) steps is \(e_k = p_k(A)e_0\) where \(p_k\) is a degree-\(k\) polynomial with \(p_k(0)=1\). CG minimizes \(\|p_k(A)e_0\|_A\) — equivalently, it finds the degree-\(k\) polynomial (with \(p_k(0)=1\)) minimizing energy error. After \(n\) steps, \(p_n\) can zero out all \(n\) eigenvalue contributions, giving \(e_n = 0\).

Preconditioned CG (PCG)

Replace the system with \(M^{-1}Ax = M^{-1}b\) where \(M\) is SPD and easy to invert. PCG iterates:

$$z_k = M^{-1}r_k, \quad \alpha_k = \frac{r_k^\top z_k}{d_k^\top A d_k}, \quad \beta_k = \frac{r_{k+1}^\top z_{k+1}}{r_k^\top z_k}$$

Good preconditioners include incomplete Cholesky, diagonal (Jacobi), and multigrid. PCG converges in \(\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\kappa(M^{-1}A)})\) iterations — the key is clustering eigenvalues of \(M^{-1}A\).

Key Takeaways & When to Use CG
▼

When to Use CG

  • \(A\) is large, sparse, or structured (only matrix-vector products needed)
  • Direct factorization (\(\mathcal{O}(n^3)\)) is too costly
  • Good preconditioner available (clustering eigenvalues)
  • Inexact Newton methods (Hessian-vector products via automatic differentiation)

When Not to Use CG

  • \(A\) is not SPD — use MINRES (symmetric indefinite) or GMRES (non-symmetric)
  • Small system (\(n \lesssim 1000\)) — direct Cholesky is simpler and more stable
  • Extremely ill-conditioned without a good preconditioner
