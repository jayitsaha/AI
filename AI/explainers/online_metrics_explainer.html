Online Metrics for RecSys

CTR, conversion rate, revenue per impression, engagement time & retention — measuring a recommender in production
Foundational RecSys Evaluation Streaming O(1) per event
1
Understanding Online Metrics
▼

What is it? Offline metrics (NDCG, recall@k, RMSE) tell you how well a model ranks a held-out dataset. Online metrics tell you what actually happens when real users see the recommendations: do they click, do they buy, do they stick around, and do they come back tomorrow? They are computed continuously from live traffic — impressions, clicks, purchases, session logs, login events — rather than from a frozen test set.

Analogy: Think of a store window display (the recommendation). Offline evaluation is a focus group rating photos of the display. Online metrics are what happens on the actual sidewalk: how many passersby glance at it (impressions), how many walk in (clicks → CTR), how many buy something (conversion rate), how much they spend (revenue per impression), how long they browse (engagement time), and whether they come back next week (retention). A display can win the focus group and still empty the store if it only attracts window-shoppers who never return.

💡 If you remember one thing

Every online metric is a ratio (or average) computed over a funnel of events: $$\text{Impressions} \to \text{Clicks} \to \text{Conversions} \to \text{Revenue}$$ CTR and CVR measure the funnel's conversion efficiency; revenue-per-impression (RPI) measures its monetary yield; engagement time and retention measure whether the experience was actually worth the user's time — the guardrails that keep CTR-chasing honest.

2
How These Metrics Work Internally
▼

The Event Funnel

Every recommendation shown to a user is logged as an impression event. A subset become click events, a subset of those become conversion events (purchase, watch, add-to-cart — whatever the business goal is), and each conversion may carry a revenue value. This funnel is the raw material for the core ratio metrics:

$$\text{CTR} = \frac{\#\text{clicks}}{\#\text{impressions}} \qquad \text{CVR} = \frac{\#\text{conversions}}{\#\text{clicks}} \qquad \text{RPI} = \frac{\text{total revenue}}{\#\text{impressions}}$$

Because RPI decomposes as \(\text{RPI} = \text{CTR} \times \text{CVR} \times \text{AOV}\) (average order value), a change in any one factor moves revenue — which is exactly why teams track all three instead of just CTR.

Engagement Time & Retention

Engagement time is the average dwell time / session duration a user spends after being served a recommendation — a signal that a click was worth the user's time, not just a reflexive tap. Retention asks a longer-horizon question: of the users active on day 0, what fraction come back on day \(D\) (commonly \(D \in \{1, 7, 30\}\))? It is computed per cohort, not per event:

$$\text{Retention}_D = \frac{\#\{\text{users active on day 0 who returned on day } D\}}{\#\{\text{users active on day 0}\}}$$

Design Decision: Why So Many Metrics?

CTR alone is gameable — clickbait thumbnails inflate clicks while tanking engagement and long-term trust. RPI alone can be gamed by over-recommending high-margin but low-relevance items. Retention is the metric hardest to fake, but it lags by days or weeks. Production systems therefore report a small dashboard of these metrics together: fast leading indicators (CTR, CVR) paired with slower guardrails (engagement time, retention) so a short-term win is not celebrated until it survives contact with real user behavior over time.

🔑 Key Property

CTR and CVR are leading indicators — available within minutes of an A/B test starting. Engagement time is a same-session guardrail. Retention is a lagging, causally strongest signal — it is the one hardest to move by manipulating the UI, because it requires the user to voluntarily come back.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

CTR / CVR as Bernoulli Rate Estimators

Each impression is (approximately) an independent Bernoulli trial: clicked or not, with true click probability \(p\). CTR is the sample mean \(\hat{p} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \mathbb{1}[\text{click}_i]\), which is unbiased with standard error \(\text{SE}(\hat p) = \sqrt{\hat p (1-\hat p)/n}\) — this is why online metrics need minimum sample sizes before you trust a delta between two variants (see A/B testing).

Revenue per Impression as a Product of Rates

$$\text{RPI} = \frac{\text{Revenue}}{\text{Impressions}} = \underbrace{\frac{\text{Clicks}}{\text{Impressions}}}_{\text{CTR}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Conversions}}{\text{Clicks}}}_{\text{CVR}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Revenue}}{\text{Conversions}}}_{\text{AOV}}$$

This decomposition is why RPI can move even when CTR is flat: AOV (basket size) or CVR (purchase intent) may have shifted. Debugging an RPI regression always means walking back through this product to find which factor broke.

Retention as Cohort Survival

Model each user's return behavior with a per-user "engagement half-life" \(h_u\): the probability they are still active \(D\) days after their first session decays roughly geometrically, \(P(\text{active at } D \mid u) \approx 0.5^{D/h_u}\). Aggregate retention is the population average of these individual survival curves — heterogeneous users (some with \(h_u\) of a few days, some of months) is exactly why real retention curves have a steep early drop followed by a long, slowly-decaying tail rather than a single exponential.

⚠️ Common Pitfall — The Novelty Effect

A new UI or ranking change often spikes CTR for the first few days simply because it's different, then decays back toward a lower steady state as the novelty wears off. Reading online metrics too early (before the novelty effect has decayed) systematically overestimates the long-run lift — always let a test run past the decay window, and watch retention, the metric novelty cannot fake for long.

Key Takeaways
▼

How to read the dashboard:

  • CTR and CVR are fast but shallow — a good proxy for immediate relevance, easy to game with clickbait
  • RPI = CTR × CVR × AOV — always decompose a revenue move into which factor actually shifted
  • Engagement time is the same-session guardrail against clickbait: rising CTR + falling engagement is a red flag
  • Retention is the slowest but most trustworthy signal — it requires a genuinely better product, not just a shinier button

Common pitfalls:

  • Novelty effect — early online-metric lifts can be an artifact of "new and different," not "better"; wait for decay
  • Simpson's paradox — an aggregate CTR lift can hide a loss in every individual segment if traffic mix shifted
  • Metric gaming — optimizing one metric (CTR) in isolation routinely regresses another (engagement, retention); always ship with guardrails
  • Short vs. long horizon — a variant that wins on Day-1 metrics can lose on Day-30 retention; the "right" horizon depends on the decision being made
💡 Hierarchy

Impressions ⊃ Clicks (CTR) ⊃ Conversions (CVR) → Revenue (RPI), cross-checked against Engagement time (session-level trust) and Retention (cohort-level trust). No single metric in this chain is sufficient on its own — the funnel metrics measure efficiency, the guardrail metrics measure whether that efficiency was earned honestly.

Code Trace

import pandas as pd, numpy as np
# events: one row per impression, joined with click/conversion/revenue logs
# columns: user_id, item_id, ts, clicked, converted, revenue, dwell_seconds
events = pd.read_parquet("impression_log.parquet")
impressions = len(events)
clicks = events["clicked"].sum()
conversions = events["converted"].sum()
revenue = events["revenue"].sum()
ctr = clicks / impressions
cvr = conversions / clicks
rpi = revenue / impressions # == ctr * cvr * aov
aov = revenue / conversions
engagement_time = events["dwell_seconds"].mean()
# Wilson / normal CI on CTR for significance testing
se_ctr = np.sqrt(ctr * (1 - ctr) / impressions)
ci95 = (ctr - 1.96 * se_ctr, ctr + 1.96 * se_ctr)
# Retention: cohort of users first seen on day 0
cohort = events.groupby("user_id")["ts"].min().dt.floor("D")
day0_users = cohort[cohort == cohort.min()].index
for D in [1, 7, 30]:
target_day = cohort.min() + pd.Timedelta(days=D)
active_on_D = events[events["ts"].dt.floor("D") == target_day]["user_id"].unique()
retention_D = len(set(day0_users) & set(active_on_D)) / len(day0_users)
print(f"Retention D{D}: {retention_D:.1%}")
