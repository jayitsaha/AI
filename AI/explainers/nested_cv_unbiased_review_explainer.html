1
The Core Idea

You want to both choose the best hyperparameters for a model and honestly estimate how well that model generalises to unseen data. The trap: if you tune HPs on the same data you use to evaluate, you leak information — the reported score is optimistically biased.

💡 Analogy

Imagine hiring a teacher to both tutor a student and then grade their exam. The grade will look great — but the student may fail a different exam. Nested CV separates the two roles: an inner "tutor" loop picks the best HP; an outer "examiner" loop grades on data the tutor never touched.

🔑 Key Property

The outer test folds are never used to make any model decision — not for HP selection, not for threshold tuning. They are pure held-out evaluation sets. This is the single invariant that makes nested CV unbiased.

⚠️ The Flat CV Bias

Standard (flat) \(K\)-fold CV: you pick the best HP by score over all folds, then report that score. Because the HP was chosen to maximise performance on those same folds, the reported metric is optimistically biased — sometimes by several percentage points. With many HPs and few samples this gap can be enormous.

2
Mechanics — Two Loops, Clear Roles

Let \(D = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n\). Nested CV with outer \(K_\text{out}\) folds and inner \(K_\text{in}\) folds:

$$\text{NCV Score} = \frac{1}{K_\text{out}} \sum_{k=1}^{K_\text{out}} \text{score}(f_k^*, D_k^{\text{test}})$$

where \(f_k^*\) is the model trained on \(D_k^{\text{train}}\) with the HP \(\lambda_k^*\) chosen by inner CV on \(D_k^{\text{train}}\) alone:

$$\lambda_k^* = \underset{\lambda \in \Lambda}{\operatorname{argmax}} \; \frac{1}{K_\text{in}} \sum_{j=1}^{K_\text{in}} \text{score}(f_{k,j}(\lambda), D_{k,j}^{\text{val}})$$

Example: \(K_\text{out}=5,\; K_\text{in}=5,\; |\Lambda|=10\) → 255 model fits vs 10 for flat CV.

3
Why Flat CV Is Over-Optimistic — Numerical Demo

Suppose you have \(n=100\) samples, 5-fold CV, and you tune over 20 HP candidates. All 20 models are equally weak (true generalisation accuracy = 70%). By pure chance, the best candidate over 5 folds will score higher than 70% — this is the selection bias.

📐 Expected Maximum of Noisy Estimates

If each of the 20 candidates has true score \(\mu = 0.70\) and CV variance \(\sigma^2\), then the expected score of the selected best candidate is:

$$\mathbb{E}[\max_{j=1}^{20} \hat{s}_j] \approx \mu + \sigma \cdot \Phi^{-1}\!\left(1 - \frac{1}{20}\right) \approx 0.70 + \sigma \cdot 1.96$$

With \(\sigma \approx 0.04\) (typical 5-fold CV on 100 samples), the selected score is ~0.778 — an upward bias of ~8 pp on data where every model truly scores 70%.

Key Takeaways
📋 When to Use Nested CV
  • Use nested CV when you need an honest estimate of generalisation AND you are also tuning HPs on the same dataset.
  • Use flat CV only for final model performance reporting when HPs were already fixed (e.g., from a separate development set).
  • Use nested CV in academic benchmarks and clinical ML — where inflated scores have real consequences.
  • Typical settings: \(K_\text{out} \in \{5,10\}\), \(K_\text{in} \in \{3,5\}\). Smaller datasets → leave-one-out for the outer loop.
⚠️ Common Mistakes
  • Fitting preprocessing (e.g., StandardScaler) on all data before CV — leaks test statistics into training.
  • Reporting the inner CV score as the generalisation estimate — that is the biased flat-CV score.
  • Using nested CV score to select the final HP — it does not tell you which single HP is best; it tells you how well the selection process generalises. For production, use all data with inner CV to pick final HP.
