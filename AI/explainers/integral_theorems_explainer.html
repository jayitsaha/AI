Green's, Stokes' & Divergence Theorems

The unifying principle: boundary integral = interior derivative integral
Intermediate Lean Advanced Vector Calculus Math Foundations Generalized Stokes
1
Understanding the Big Picture
▼

What are these theorems? Three landmark results in vector calculus — Green's theorem, Stokes' theorem, and the Divergence theorem — each relate an integral over a region (area or volume) to an integral over its boundary (curve or surface). They are not three separate facts but three incarnations of one single principle.

Analogy: Think of water flow in a network of pipes. To know how much water is building up inside a tank (divergence inside), you only need to measure the net flow across the tank's surface — you never need to look inside. To know how much a river is "swirling" inside a loop, you only need to measure the current along the loop's edge. That's Green's theorem. Stokes' theorem extends this swirl idea from flat loops to curved surfaces in 3D.

💡 If you remember one thing

All three theorems are special cases of the Generalized Stokes' Theorem: $$\int_{\partial \Omega} \omega = \int_{\Omega} d\omega$$ — the integral of a differential form \(\omega\) over the boundary equals the integral of its exterior derivative \(d\omega\) over the interior. "What comes out of the boundary = what is generated inside."

The Three Theorems — Precise Statements
▼

Green's Theorem (2D, curl ↔ circulation)

Let \(C\) be a positively-oriented, simple closed curve in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) bounding region \(D\), and let \(\mathbf{F} = (P, Q)\) be a smooth vector field. Then:

$$\oint_C P\,dx + Q\,dy = \iint_D \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right) dA$$

The left side is the circulation of \(\mathbf{F}\) around \(C\). The right side integrates the scalar curl (a.k.a. the 2D curl, or "rotation density") over the enclosed area \(D\).

Stokes' Theorem (3D, curl ↔ circulation)

Let \(S\) be an oriented smooth surface in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) bounded by curve \(\partial S\), and \(\mathbf{F}\) a smooth vector field. Then:

$$\oint_{\partial S} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot d\mathbf{S}$$

The left side is the line integral (circulation) around the boundary curve. The right side is the flux of the curl through the surface. Green's theorem is Stokes' theorem when \(S\) is a flat region in the \(xy\)-plane.

Divergence Theorem (3D, divergence ↔ flux)

Let \(V\) be a compact region in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) with outward-oriented boundary surface \(\partial V\), and \(\mathbf{F}\) a smooth vector field. Then:

$$\oiint_{\partial V} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iiint_V (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F})\, dV$$

The left side is the total outward flux through the closed surface. The right side sums the divergence (local source strength) over all volume elements.

🔑 The Unifying Pattern

Each theorem has the same skeleton: Boundary Integral = Interior of Derivative. Green/Stokes pair curl with circulation; Divergence theorem pairs divergence with outward flux. The exterior derivative \(d\) unifies curl and divergence in differential-form language.

Why They Are True — Proof Sketches
▼

Green's Theorem: Proof by Splitting

It suffices to prove two halves separately. For the \(Q\) part (the \(\partial Q/\partial x\) term), subdivide \(D\) into vertical strips. On each vertical strip of width \(dx\), the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus integrates \(\partial Q/\partial x\) in \(x\) to give the net change of \(Q\) — which equals the boundary contribution \(Q\,dy\) on that strip. Summing over all strips recovers the full surface integral and the line integral. The \(P\) part follows symmetrically.

Numerically Exact Check — Green's on a Circle

Take \(\mathbf{F} = (-y, x)\) so \(P = -y\), \(Q = x\). Region \(D\) is the unit disk.

RHS: \(\partial Q/\partial x - \partial P/\partial y = 1 - (-1) = 2\). So \(\iint_D 2\,dA = 2\pi(1)^2 = 2\pi\).

LHS: Parametrize \(C\): \(x = \cos t, y = \sin t, t \in [0,2\pi]\). Then \(P\,dx + Q\,dy = (-\sin t)(-\sin t)\,dt + (\cos t)(\cos t)\,dt = (\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t)\,dt = dt\). So \(\oint_C = \int_0^{2\pi} dt = 2\pi\). LHS = RHS = \(2\pi\). ✓

Divergence Theorem: Proof by Coordinate Slices

Write \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \partial F_x/\partial x + \partial F_y/\partial y + \partial F_z/\partial z\). It suffices to show \(\iiint_V \partial F_z/\partial z\,dV = \oiint_{\partial V} F_z \hat{k} \cdot d\mathbf{S}\) (and similarly for \(x, y\)). For the \(z\)-part: slice \(V\) into columns. By FTC in \(z\), integrating \(\partial F_z/\partial z\) from the bottom face \(z_1(x,y)\) to top face \(z_2(x,y)\) gives \(F_z(x,y,z_2) - F_z(x,y,z_1)\). On the top face \(d\mathbf{S} = +\hat{k}\,dA\); on the bottom, \(d\mathbf{S} = -\hat{k}\,dA\) (outward normals). The sum equals the flux through top and bottom. Integrating over \(x,y\) completes the proof.

Numerically Exact Check — Divergence on a Sphere

Take \(\mathbf{F} = (x, y, z)\), so \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = 3\). Region: unit ball \(V\), radius \(R=1\).

RHS: \(\iiint_V 3\,dV = 3 \cdot \frac{4}{3}\pi(1)^3 = 4\pi\).

LHS: On the unit sphere \(\mathbf{F} = (x,y,z) = \hat{n}\) (the outward unit normal). So \(\mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = 1 \cdot dS\), giving \(\oiint_{\partial V} dS = 4\pi(1)^2 = 4\pi\). LHS = RHS = \(4\pi\). ✓

⚠️ Orientation is Critical

Green's theorem requires the curve to be positively oriented (counterclockwise). Stokes' theorem requires the surface orientation and boundary orientation to be consistent (right-hand rule). The Divergence theorem requires the boundary normal to point outward. Sign errors from orientation are the most common mistake.

Generalized Stokes' Theorem & Code

The Unifying Framework: Differential Forms

In the language of differential forms on an oriented manifold \(\Omega\) with boundary \(\partial\Omega\):

$$\int_{\partial \Omega} \omega = \int_{\Omega} d\omega$$

Here \(\omega\) is a differential form and \(d\) is the exterior derivative. Each classical theorem is a special case:

  • Fundamental Theorem of Calculus: \(\omega = f\), \(\Omega = [a,b]\), gives \(f(b)-f(a) = \int_a^b f'\,dx\).
  • Green's Theorem: \(\omega = P\,dx + Q\,dy\) (1-form in \(\mathbb{R}^2\)), \(d\omega = (\partial Q/\partial x - \partial P/\partial y)\,dx\wedge dy\).
  • Stokes' Theorem: \(\omega\) = 1-form in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), \(d\omega\) = 2-form (curl flux).
  • Divergence Theorem: \(\omega\) = 2-form in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), \(d\omega\) = 3-form (divergence times volume form).

Python: Numerical Verification

import numpy as np
from scipy import integrate
# Green's Theorem: F = (-y, x) on unit disk
# LHS: line integral around unit circle
t = np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, 10000)
x, y = np.cos(t), np.sin(t)
dx, dy = np.diff(x), np.diff(y)
P, Q = -y[:-1], x[:-1] # F = (-y, x)
LHS_green = np.sum(P*dx + Q*dy) # ≈ 6.2832
# RHS: area integral of curl = 2 over unit disk
RHS_green, _ = integrate.dblquad(
lambda y,x: 2, # curl = dQ/dx - dP/dy = 1-(-1) = 2
-1, 1,
lambda x: -np.sqrt(1-x**2),
lambda x: np.sqrt(1-x**2))
# RHS_green ≈ 6.2832 (= 2π)
# Divergence Theorem: F=(x,y,z) on unit ball
# RHS: integral of div(F)=3 over unit ball = 3*(4π/3) = 4π
RHS_div, _ = integrate.tplquad(
lambda z,y,x: 3,
-1,1, lambda x:-np.sqrt(1-x**2), lambda x:np.sqrt(1-x**2),
lambda x,y:-np.sqrt(1-x**2-y**2), lambda x,y:np.sqrt(1-x**2-y**2))
# RHS_div ≈ 12.566 (= 4π). Both verify exactly.

Key Takeaways & Applications
▼

When to Use Each Theorem

  • Green's Theorem: Computing 2D areas via line integrals; evaluating circulation of a 2D flow.
  • Stokes' Theorem: Converting a hard surface integral to an easier line integral (or vice versa); proving that curl-free fields are conservative.
  • Divergence Theorem: Computing flux through closed surfaces without parametrizing them; deriving conservation laws in physics (mass, charge, momentum).

Physics Connections

  • Maxwell's equations in integral form are Stokes' and Divergence theorems applied to electric/magnetic fields.
  • Continuity equation (mass conservation) is the Divergence theorem: \(\partial \rho/\partial t + \nabla \cdot \mathbf{J} = 0\).
  • Faraday's law (\(\oint \mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = -d\Phi_B/dt\)) is Stokes' theorem for the electric field.
💡 The Deep Insight

These theorems reveal that global boundary behavior is controlled by local interior behavior. You never need to measure the entire boundary in detail — you can instead compute a simpler (divergence or curl) quantity inside. This is the foundation of conservation laws, gauge theory, and topological invariants in modern mathematics and physics.

⚠️ Common Interview Trap

Stokes' theorem requires the surface to be orientable. A Möbius strip is not orientable — Stokes' theorem does not apply. Also: the theorems assume the vector field is \(C^1\) on the closed region; singularities inside require careful handling (e.g., exclude them with a small sphere).
