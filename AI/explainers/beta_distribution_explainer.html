Understanding the Beta Distribution
▼

What is it? The Beta distribution is a probability distribution that lives entirely on the interval [0, 1]. Its purpose is to model uncertainty about a probability itself — before you observe any data, you have a belief about how likely some event is, and the Beta distribution encodes that belief.

Analogy: Imagine you're estimating the bias of a coin. You don't know if it's fair. Your belief about the true heads-probability \(\theta \in [0,1]\) is itself uncertain — it could be 0.3, or 0.5, or 0.8. The Beta distribution is a curve over [0,1] that says "here is my current credence over all possible values of \(\theta\)." A flat curve means "I have no idea." A tall, narrow curve means "I'm pretty sure \(\theta \approx 0.6\)."

Shape intuition: what do \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) control?

The distribution is parameterized by two positive shape parameters \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\). Think of them as pseudo-counts of observations: \(\alpha - 1\) prior successes and \(\beta - 1\) prior failures.

  • \(\alpha = \beta = 1\): Uniform — every probability equally likely
  • \(\alpha = \beta > 1\): Symmetric bell centered at 0.5 (more peaked as values grow)
  • \(\alpha > \beta\): Skewed toward 1 (believe high success probability)
  • \(\alpha < \beta\): Skewed toward 0 (believe low success probability)
  • \(\alpha < 1, \beta < 1\): U-shaped — mass near 0 and 1, not the middle (Jeffreys prior at \(\alpha=\beta=0.5\))
💡 If you remember one thing

The Beta distribution is the conjugate prior for the Bernoulli/Binomial likelihood. This means: if your prior belief is \(\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)\) and you observe \(k\) successes in \(n\) trials, your posterior is exactly \(\text{Beta}(\alpha + k,\; \beta + (n-k))\). No integrals needed — just add counts. Mean: \(\mu = \dfrac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\).

Conjugate Prior — How Bayesian Updating Works
▼

The Bernoulli/Binomial Likelihood

Suppose a coin lands heads with unknown probability \(\theta\). Each flip is a Bernoulli trial: \(P(X=1|\theta) = \theta\). If you observe \(k\) heads in \(n\) flips, the Binomial likelihood of the data given \(\theta\) is:

$$P(k \mid \theta, n) = \binom{n}{k} \theta^k (1-\theta)^{n-k}$$

Bayesian updating: prior × likelihood → posterior

Bayes' theorem says the posterior is proportional to prior × likelihood. If the prior is \(\text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)\), its PDF is proportional to \(\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}\). Multiplying by the likelihood:

$$p(\theta \mid k) \propto \underbrace{\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}}_{\text{prior}} \cdot \underbrace{\theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k}}_{\text{likelihood}} = \theta^{(\alpha+k)-1}(1-\theta)^{(\beta+n-k)-1}$$

This has exactly the form of a Beta PDF with updated parameters:

$$\theta \mid k \;\sim\; \text{Beta}(\alpha + k,\;\; \beta + (n-k))$$

Why "conjugate"?

A prior is conjugate to a likelihood if the posterior belongs to the same family as the prior. The Beta distribution is conjugate to the Bernoulli/Binomial family. This makes sequential updating trivially cheap: after seeing each new flip, just increment \(\alpha\) or \(\beta\) by 1. No numerical integration, no MCMC needed.

🔑 Key Property: Sequential Consistency

Bayesian updating is order-independent. Whether you process 10 flips one-by-one or all at once, the posterior is the same. Starting from \(\text{Beta}(2,3)\) and observing 7 heads, 3 tails in 10 flips → \(\text{Beta}(9, 6)\), with posterior mean \(9/15 = 0.600\). (Prior mean was \(2/5 = 0.400\); MLE was \(7/10 = 0.700\) — the posterior is a principled compromise.)

⚠️ Common Misconception

The Beta distribution is a distribution over \(\theta\) (the coin's bias), NOT a distribution over coin flip outcomes. A single flip is still Bernoulli. The Beta encodes your uncertainty about which Bernoulli is generating the data.

Why It Works — The Math
▼

PDF Definition

For \(\theta \in [0,1]\), \(\alpha > 0\), \(\beta > 0\):

$$f(\theta;\, \alpha, \beta) = \frac{\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}}{B(\alpha,\beta)}$$

where the normalizing constant is the Beta function:

$$B(\alpha,\beta) = \int_0^1 t^{\alpha-1}(1-t)^{\beta-1}\,dt = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\,\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}$$

and \(\Gamma\) is the Gamma function (\(\Gamma(n) = (n-1)!\) for positive integers). This ensures the PDF integrates to 1.

Key statistics

$$\mathbb{E}[\theta] = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}, \qquad \operatorname{Mode} = \frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha+\beta-2}\;\;(\alpha,\beta>1), \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\theta) = \frac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^2(\alpha+\beta+1)}$$

Proof that the posterior is Beta (conjugacy derivation)

Prior: \(p(\theta) \propto \theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}\). Likelihood of \(k\) successes in \(n\) trials: \(p(k|\theta) \propto \theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k}\). By Bayes:

$$p(\theta|k) \;\propto\; p(\theta)\cdot p(k|\theta) \;\propto\; \theta^{\alpha+k-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta+n-k-1}$$

This is the kernel of \(\text{Beta}(\alpha+k,\, \beta+n-k)\). Since the Beta function normalizes any such kernel uniquely, the posterior is:

$$\theta \mid k \;\sim\; \text{Beta}(\alpha + k,\;\; \beta + n - k)$$

Predictive distribution for the next flip

After observing \(k\) successes in \(n\) flips with \(\text{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)\) prior, the probability the next flip is heads is:

$$P(X_{n+1}=1 \mid k) = \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid k] = \frac{\alpha+k}{\alpha+\beta+n}$$

This is called Laplace smoothing (or add-\(\alpha\) smoothing). With \(\alpha=\beta=1\) (uniform prior): \(P = (k+1)/(n+2)\), which avoids assigning zero probability to unseen events.

∑ Numerical Example

Prior: \(\text{Beta}(2,3)\). Prior mean: \(2/5 = 0.400\). Observe 7 heads, 3 tails. Posterior: \(\text{Beta}(9,6)\). Posterior mean: \(9/15 = 0.600\). Posterior mode: \((9-1)/(9+6-2) = 8/13 \approx 0.615\). Posterior variance: \(9\cdot6/(15^2\cdot16) \approx 0.0150\).

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use the Beta distribution

  • Prior for click-through rates, conversion rates, A/B tests — anything that is a probability in [0,1]
  • Bayesian bandit algorithms (Thompson Sampling) — maintain a Beta posterior per arm, sample to balance explore/exploit
  • Calibration models — use Beta to model the distribution of predicted probabilities
  • Mixture models — Beta-Bernoulli mixture is a natural topic model (Latent Dirichlet Allocation uses Dirichlet, which generalises Beta to K categories)

Special cases to remember

  • \(\text{Beta}(1,1)\) = Uniform[0,1] — no prior knowledge
  • \(\text{Beta}(0.5, 0.5)\) = Jeffreys prior — uninformative, invariant to reparameterisation
  • \(\alpha \to \infty, \beta \to \infty\) with \(\alpha/(\alpha+\beta) \to p\) — Delta function at \(p\) (certainty)
  • \(\text{Beta}(\alpha, 1)\) = Power distribution; \(\text{Beta}(1, \beta)\) = Shifted power distribution
💡 Connections to the broader vault

Dirichlet distribution: multivariate generalisation of Beta (conjugate prior for Categorical/Multinomial). Thompson Sampling: uses Beta posteriors for multi-armed bandits. Variational Inference: Beta is a common variational family for Bernoulli latent variables. LDA: uses Dirichlet priors (generalised Beta) over topic distributions.
