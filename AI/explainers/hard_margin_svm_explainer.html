Hard-Margin SVM

Maximum-margin hyperplane, support vectors, and the primal QP
Advanced Classical ML Supervised Learning Convex Optimization
1
The 30-Second Version
▼

What is this? A Support Vector Machine (SVM) is a classifier that draws a dividing line — or, in higher dimensions, a flat boundary — between two groups of data points. Hard-margin SVM finds the best possible line: the one that sits as far as possible from both groups simultaneously. That maximum distance is called the margin.

Real-world analogy: Imagine two armies camped on opposite sides of a field. A peace treaty must draw a neutral zone. Hard-margin SVM draws that zone as wide as possible, making it hardest for either army to accidentally cross. The soldiers closest to the neutral zone — the ones who define its edge — are the "support vectors."

One-sentence summary: Among all hyperplanes that correctly separate the data, SVM picks the one where the closest point from each class is as far away as possible — maximizing the margin \(2/\|\mathbf{w}\|\).

💡 If you remember one thing

Only the support vectors — the handful of data points sitting on the ±1 gutter lines — determine the solution. Every other training point is irrelevant. Delete a non-support-vector and the model does not change at all.

How It Works — Primal QP & KKT Conditions
▼

The Primal Optimization Problem

Given labelled training data \(\{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n\) with \(y_i \in \{-1, +1\}\) and the data being linearly separable, the hard-margin SVM solves:

$$\min_{\mathbf{w},\, b} \;\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b) \geq 1 \;\; \forall i$$

The constraint \(y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b) \geq 1\) means every positive point is at least +1 and every negative point at most −1 in the signed decision value. The objective \(\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2\) is minimized because margin \(= 2/\|\mathbf{w}\|\), so minimizing \(\|\mathbf{w}\|\) maximizes the margin.

Functional vs. Geometric Margin

The functional margin of point \(i\) is \(\hat{\gamma}_i = y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b)\). Rescaling \(\mathbf{w}\) by a constant scales all functional margins equally, so we fix the scale by requiring \(\hat{\gamma}_{\min} = 1\). The geometric margin (actual distance to the hyperplane) is \(\gamma_i = \hat{\gamma}_i / \|\mathbf{w}\|\), and the total margin between the two classes is \(2/\|\mathbf{w}\|\).

KKT Conditions & the Role of Support Vectors

This is a convex quadratic program (QP). The Lagrangian is:

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, b, \boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 - \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i \bigl[y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b) - 1\bigr]$$

KKT stationarity conditions give:

$$\mathbf{w} = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i \qquad \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i y_i = 0$$

KKT complementary slackness: \(\alpha_i [y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b) - 1] = 0\) for all \(i\). This forces \(\alpha_i > 0\) only for points where the constraint is tight — i.e., exactly on the ±1 gutters. These are the support vectors. All other \(\alpha_i = 0\).

Key Concept: Sparsity

The solution \(\mathbf{w} = \sum_i \alpha_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i\) is a sparse weighted sum — only support vectors (with \(\alpha_i > 0\)) contribute. This is why SVMs are robust to irrelevant features and are memory-efficient at test time.

Dual Problem

Substituting back, we get the dual (which only involves dot products \(\mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_j\) — the key to the kernel trick):

$$\max_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j} \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j \mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_j \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \alpha_i \geq 0,\; \sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0$$

The Math — Full Derivation
▼

Step 1: Geometric margin

The signed distance from point \(\mathbf{x}\) to hyperplane \(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}+b=0\) is \((\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}+b)/\|\mathbf{w}\|\). We want the minimum signed distance across all training points to be maximized:

$$\max_{\mathbf{w},b} \frac{1}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}\min_i y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b)$$

Step 2: Fix the scale

The problem is scale-invariant: \((\mathbf{w},b)\) and \((c\mathbf{w}, cb)\) give the same hyperplane. We fix the scale by setting \(\min_i y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i+b)=1\), transforming the objective to maximizing \(1/\|\mathbf{w}\|\) — equivalently minimizing \(\|\mathbf{w}\|\), or equivalently \(\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2\) (the \(\frac{1}{2}\) simplifies the gradient):

$$\min_{\mathbf{w},b}\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i+b)\geq 1 \;\;\forall i$$

Step 3: Lagrangian & dual derivation

Introduce multipliers \(\alpha_i \geq 0\) for each constraint. The Lagrangian:

$$\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 - \sum_i \alpha_i[y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i+b)-1]$$

Setting \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{w}=0\): \(\mathbf{w} - \sum_i\alpha_i y_i\mathbf{x}_i=0 \Rightarrow \mathbf{w}=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i\mathbf{x}_i\). Setting \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial b=0\): \(\sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0\). Substituting back:

$$\mathcal{L}_D = \sum_i\alpha_i - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i\alpha_j y_i y_j \mathbf{x}_i^\top\mathbf{x}_j$$

Since the primal is convex and Slater's condition holds (data is linearly separable), strong duality holds: primal optimum = dual optimum.

Step 4: Recovering b

Once \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^*\) is found, \(\mathbf{w}^*=\sum_i\alpha_i^*y_i\mathbf{x}_i\). For any support vector \(\mathbf{x}_s\) with \(\alpha_s>0\):

$$b^* = y_s - \mathbf{w}^{*\top}\mathbf{x}_s = y_s - \sum_i\alpha_i^*y_i\mathbf{x}_i^\top\mathbf{x}_s$$

In practice average over all support vectors for numerical stability.

⚠️ Warning: Hard-margin requires linear separability

If the data is NOT linearly separable, the hard-margin primal is infeasible — no \((\mathbf{w},b)\) satisfies all constraints simultaneously. You must switch to soft-margin SVM (adding slack variables \(\xi_i\)) or use a kernel to map to a higher-dimensional space.

Margin at optimality

$$\text{Margin} = \frac{2}{\|\mathbf{w}^*\|} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\sum_{i,j}\alpha_i^*\alpha_j^*y_iy_j\mathbf{x}_i^\top\mathbf{x}_j}}$$

Numerical Trace — Worked Example
▼

Concrete example: \(\mathbf{w}=[1,1]\), \(b=-4\)

Training set (2D, linearly separable):

  • Positive class (\(y=+1\)): (3,2)*, (4,2), (2,4), (5,1), (4,3)  [* = support vector]
  • Negative class (\(y=-1\)): (2,1)*, (0,2), (1,1), (0,1), (−0.5,2.5)  [* = support vector]

Verify optimal solution

Optimal \(\mathbf{w}^*=[1,1]\), \(b^*=-4\) (hyperplane: \(x_1+x_2=4\)).

  • \(\|\mathbf{w}^*\| = \sqrt{1^2+1^2} = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.4142\)
  • Margin \(= 2/\|\mathbf{w}^*\| = 2/\sqrt{2} = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.4142\)
  • Primal objective: \(\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}^*\|^2 = \frac{1}{2}\cdot2 = 1.0\)

Check constraints

$$y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i+b) \geq 1 \;\;\forall i$$

SV⁺=(3,2): \((+1)(1\cdot3+1\cdot2-4) = 1 \geq 1\) ✓ (tight — support vector)

SV⁻=(2,1): \((-1)(1\cdot2+1\cdot1-4) = (-1)(-1) = 1 \geq 1\) ✓ (tight — support vector)

(4,2): \((+1)(4+2-4) = 2 \geq 1\) ✓ (slack)

(0,2): \((-1)(0+2-4) = 2 \geq 1\) ✓ (slack)

Dual variables

From \(\mathbf{w}=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i\mathbf{x}_i\) with only two support vectors (all others \(\alpha_i=0\)):

$$[1,1] = \alpha_1^*(+1)[3,2] + \alpha_2^*(-1)[2,1]$$

Solving: \(3\alpha_1^*-2\alpha_2^*=1\) and \(2\alpha_1^*-\alpha_2^*=1\) gives \(\alpha_1^*=\alpha_2^*=1\) — both positive, confirming both points are genuine support vectors.

Bias recovery

$$b^* = y_{SV^+} - \mathbf{w}^{*\top}\mathbf{x}_{SV^+} = 1 - (1\cdot3+1\cdot2) = 1 - 5 = -4 \checkmark$$

Check with SV⁻: \(b^* = -1 - (-1)(2+1) = -1+3 \cdot(-1)? \) ... actually \(b^* = y_{SV^-} - \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_{SV^-} = -1 - (2+1) + \)?

Correct: \(b^* = y_{SV^-} - \mathbf{w}^{*\top}\mathbf{x}_{SV^-} = (-1) - (1\cdot2+1\cdot1) = -1 - 3 = -4\) ✓ (consistent)

Math Check

The dual objective at optimum: \(W(\alpha^*)=\alpha_1^*+\alpha_2^*-\frac{1}{2}[(1)^2(3\cdot3+2\cdot2)+(1)^2(2\cdot2+1\cdot1)+2(1)(1)(+1)(-1)(3\cdot2+2\cdot1)]=2-\frac{1}{2}[13+5-2\cdot8]=2-\frac{1}{2}[2]=2-1=1.0\). Matches primal objective ✓ (strong duality).

When to use Hard-Margin SVM

  • Data is known to be perfectly linearly separable (e.g., synthetic benchmark, theoretical analysis)
  • Teaching/understanding the core principle before soft-margin extensions
  • As a subroutine in some kernel methods where separability is guaranteed in feature space

Key Takeaways
  • The margin \(2/\|\mathbf{w}\|\) is maximized by minimizing \(\|\mathbf{w}\|^2\) — a convex QP with a unique global minimum.
  • Support vectors are the only training points with \(\alpha_i > 0\); they define \(\mathbf{w}\) and \(b\) entirely.
  • Strong duality (primal=dual) holds for hard-margin SVM; the dual reveals the kernel trick.
  • Hard-margin SVM is the foundation: soft-margin adds slack variables; kernelization adds non-linear decision boundaries.
