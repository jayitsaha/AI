1
Understanding Data Collection & Annotation
▼

What is it? Before you can train any ML model, you need examples it can learn from — and for supervised learning, each example needs a correct answer attached (a "label"). Data collection is deciding where those raw examples come from; annotation is the process of attaching trustworthy labels to them. Every downstream model is a mirror of this stage — a model can never be more reliable than the data it was trained on.

Analogy: Imagine training a new employee by having them study thousands of past customer tickets with the "correct" resolution written on each one. If those resolutions come from four different senior staff who sometimes disagree, and a few tickets got mislabeled by an intern in a hurry, the trainee will absorb those inconsistencies as if they were truth. Data collection picks which tickets to study; annotation is writing (and cross-checking) the "correct" resolution on each one.

Four canonical sources feed most production pipelines: (1) system logs — implicit signals like clicks, purchases, dwell time, free but noisy and biased toward what the current system already shows users; (2) explicit user feedback — thumbs up/down, star ratings, reported issues, higher signal but sparse; (3) purchased / vendor data — data bought from data brokers or crowdsourcing platforms, fast to scale but variable quality and possible licensing/compliance risk; (4) synthetic data — generated or simulated examples (augmentation, simulators, generative models), useful for rare classes and edge cases but can miss real-world distribution quirks.

💡 If you remember one thing

Label quality is not free — it is bought with annotator agreement, redundancy, and smart sample selection. Measure agreement with Cohen's κ, resolve conflicts with majority vote (or adjudication), and spend your limited labeling budget where a model is most uncertain (active learning) instead of labeling uniformly at random.

2
How the Pipeline Works Internally
▼

Stage 1 — Ingestion

Raw records from all sources land in an unlabeled data lake. A sampling policy decides which slice actually gets sent to annotation — usually stratified by source and business priority, never a simple "label everything" because labeling is the expensive step (crowdsourced labels commonly run $0.02–$5+ per item depending on task complexity, and expert medical/legal labels can be $50+).

Stage 2 — Multi-Annotator Labeling

Each sampled item is shown to K ≥ 2 independent annotators (crowd workers, contractors, or in-house experts) who label it without seeing each other's answers — this independence is what makes agreement statistics meaningful. Every annotator also gets periodic "gold" items with known answers, used to track individual accuracy and catch annotators who are guessing or gaming the task.

Stage 3 — Agreement & Aggregation

Once labels come back, two things happen in parallel: (a) inter-annotator agreement is computed (Cohen's κ for 2 annotators, Fleiss' κ for K>2) to quantify whether the task itself is well-specified — low κ usually means the labeling instructions are ambiguous, not that annotators are careless; (b) individual votes are aggregated into one final label, typically by majority vote, with ties escalated to a senior adjudicator rather than broken arbitrarily.

Stage 4 — Quality Control & Noise Monitoring

Even after aggregation, some fraction of final labels will be wrong ("label noise"). This noise doesn't just add a constant tax to accuracy — a model trained on noisy labels effectively memorizes contradictory signal, and its achievable accuracy degrades faster than the noise rate would suggest, especially near label boundaries and for rare classes.

Stage 5 — Active Learning Loop

Instead of labeling a uniform random sample forever, a cheap proxy model is trained on what's labeled so far and used to score the remaining unlabeled pool. Items where the model is most uncertain (predicted probability closest to the decision boundary) are queued for the next labeling round — this is uncertainty sampling, the simplest and most widely deployed active learning strategy. The loop repeats: label → retrain → re-score → re-select.

🔑 Design decision: why not just label more?

Random labeling wastes budget on examples the model already classifies confidently — they teach it almost nothing new. Uncertainty sampling concentrates the (expensive, human) label budget on the examples that would most change the decision boundary, which empirically reaches a target accuracy with far fewer labels than random sampling.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Cohen's Kappa (agreement corrected for chance)

Raw percent agreement is misleading — even two random annotators will "agree" some of the time by luck. Cohen's κ corrects for that. Let \(p_o\) be observed agreement (fraction of items where both annotators picked the same label) and \(p_e\) be the agreement expected by chance given each annotator's marginal label distribution:

$$\kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e}, \qquad p_e = \sum_{c} \left(\frac{n_{c}^{(A)}}{N}\right)\left(\frac{n_{c}^{(B)}}{N}\right)$$

where \(n_c^{(A)}\) is how many times annotator A used class \(c\), and \(N\) is the total number of items. \(\kappa = 1\) means perfect agreement beyond chance; \(\kappa = 0\) means agreement is no better than chance; \(\kappa < 0\) means worse than chance (systematic disagreement).

Majority Vote Aggregation

For an item with votes \(v_1,\dots,v_K\) from \(K\) annotators, the aggregated label is:

$$\hat{y} = \underset{c}{\operatorname{argmax}} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathbb{1}[v_k = c]$$

When the argmax is not unique (a tie across ≥2 classes), the item is routed to an expert adjudicator rather than broken randomly — random tie-breaking silently injects noise exactly where the task is most ambiguous.

Effect of Label Noise on Model Accuracy

If a fraction \(\eta\) of training labels are flipped uniformly at random, the achievable test accuracy \(A(\eta)\) degrades roughly as a power-law decay from the noise-free ceiling \(A_0\):

$$A(\eta) \approx A_0 \cdot (1-\eta)^{\gamma}, \quad \gamma > 1$$

The exponent \(\gamma > 1\) captures the fact that noisy labels near the decision boundary are more damaging than a naive linear discount would predict — the model's gradient signal becomes contradictory exactly where it's already hardest to separate classes.

Uncertainty Sampling

Given a probabilistic classifier's predicted class probability \(p(\hat{y}\mid x)\) on unlabeled point \(x\), the simplest uncertainty score is margin-to-boundary:

$$U(x) = 1 - 2\left| p(\hat{y}\mid x) - 0.5 \right|$$

\(U(x)\) is maximized (\(=1\)) exactly on the decision boundary where \(p=0.5\), and shrinks to 0 for confidently-classified points. The next labeling batch is the top-\(k\) points by \(U(x)\).

⚠️ Common pitfall

Uncertainty sampling can over-select outliers and mislabeled items that look "uncertain" simply because they're noisy, not because they're informative. Production systems usually blend uncertainty with a diversity or density term (e.g. cluster-based or expected-model-change sampling) so the selected batch isn't a pile of near-duplicate edge cases.

Key Takeaways
▼

When designing a collection & annotation pipeline, decide:

  • Source mix — logs are cheap but biased toward existing behavior; feedback is high-signal but sparse; purchased data scales fast but needs quality/compliance audits; synthetic data fills rare-class gaps but can drift from reality.
  • Redundancy (K) — label each item with ≥2 annotators whenever the task is even mildly subjective; the cost of redundancy is usually far cheaper than the cost of a silently mislabeled training set.
  • Agreement threshold — treat κ < 0.4 as a signal to rewrite the labeling guidelines, not to blame the annotators.
  • Active learning — worth adopting once labeling is the bottleneck (not compute or model capacity) and you have a reasonable proxy model to score uncertainty with.
StrategyLabels to hit target accuracyBest for
Random samplingHighSimple, unbiased, no proxy model needed
Uncertainty samplingLow–MediumBottlenecked labeling budget, stable proxy model
Uncertainty + diversityLowProduction active learning, avoids outlier traps
💡 Bottom line

Data collection and annotation is a quality-under-budget optimization problem: choose diverse sources, measure agreement honestly with κ, aggregate conflicts deliberately, monitor how residual noise erodes accuracy, and spend the labeling budget where the model is most uncertain rather than uniformly.

def cohens_kappa(confusion):
N = confusion.sum()
po = np.trace(confusion) / N
row = confusion.sum(axis=1) / N # annotator A marginals
col = confusion.sum(axis=0) / N # annotator B marginals
pe = float(row @ col)
return (po - pe) / (1 - pe)
# 4) Majority vote, escalate ties to an expert adjudicator

counts = Counter(vote_list)
