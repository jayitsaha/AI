Keyword Extraction: RAKE, YAKE, KeyBERT

Uncovering the most important words and phrases in text.
NLP Text Mining Intermediate
1
Understanding Keyword Extraction
▼

What is it? Keyword extraction is a Natural Language Processing (NLP) task that automatically identifies the most important, relevant, or representative words and phrases in a document. These keywords can summarize the content, facilitate information retrieval, or categorize text.

Why is it important? In an age of information overload, quickly grasping the essence of a document, article, or conversation is crucial. Keyword extraction helps by:

  • Summarization: Providing a concise overview of content.
  • Indexing & Search: Improving search engine relevance and document organization.
  • Topic Modeling: Identifying key themes within large text corpora.
  • Recommendation Systems: Matching users with relevant content based on shared keywords.

This explainer focuses on three distinct approaches: RAKE (a statistical, graph-based method), YAKE (an unsupervised statistical method), and KeyBERT (an embedding-based method leveraging large language models).

💡 Analogy

Imagine you're speed-reading a textbook and want to quickly highlight the most critical terms. Keyword extraction is like having an AI assistant that does this for you, but with different "highlighting strategies" depending on the algorithm.

2
How Each Algorithm Works
▼

1. RAKE (Rapid Automatic Keyword Extraction)

RAKE is an unsupervised, graph-based algorithm. It identifies keywords by analyzing the frequency and co-occurrence of words within candidate phrases. It works by:

  1. Tokenization: Splits text into words and identifies stop words (common words like "the", "is", "a").
  2. Candidate Phrase Generation: Sequences of non-stop words are considered candidate keywords.
  3. Word Graph Construction: For each candidate phrase, a graph is built where words are nodes. An edge exists between words if they co-occur in the same candidate phrase.
  4. Word Scoring: Each word is scored based on its degree (number of co-occurring words) divided by its frequency (how often it appears). Words that appear frequently but co-occur with many unique words get higher scores.
  5. Phrase Scoring: The score of a candidate phrase is the sum of the scores of its constituent words.
  6. Ranking: Phrases are ranked by their scores.

2. YAKE (Yet Another Keyword Extractor)

YAKE is an unsupervised, statistical keyword extraction algorithm that relies on a set of statistical features extracted from the text itself. It aims to identify keywords that are highly relevant to the document but less common in general language. Key features include:

  • Casing: Words starting with uppercase letters often denote proper nouns or important terms.
  • Word Position: Words appearing early in a document or sentence are often more important.
  • Frequency: How often a word appears in the document.
  • Relatedness to Context: How many unique words precede or follow a given word.
  • Term Occurrence: The number of times a term appears in the document.

YAKE combines these features into a single score, where a lower score indicates higher importance.

3. KeyBERT

KeyBERT leverages transformer models (like BERT) to create context-aware embeddings. Unlike RAKE and YAKE, it understands the semantic meaning of words and phrases. Its process involves:

  1. Document Embedding: The entire document is passed through a pre-trained BERT model to get a single vector representation (embedding).
  2. Candidate Keyword Generation: Candidate phrases are extracted (e.g., using n-grams, or simple tokenization).
  3. Candidate Embedding: Each candidate keyword is also embedded using the same BERT model.
  4. Similarity Scoring: The cosine similarity between the document embedding and each candidate keyword embedding is calculated. Candidates most similar to the document are preferred.
  5. Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR): To ensure diversity among the extracted keywords, MMR is applied. It selects keywords that are both similar to the document and dissimilar to already selected keywords. This prevents redundant keywords.
🔑 Key Evolution

Keyword extraction has evolved from purely statistical (RAKE, YAKE) to semantically-aware methods (KeyBERT). Statistical methods are fast and don't need pre-trained models, but lack contextual understanding. Embedding-based methods are slower and resource-intensive but capture deeper meaning.

The Math Behind the Magic
▼

RAKE: Degree and Frequency

RAKE's core idea is that important words appear frequently within candidate phrases but also co-occur with many other unique words. For a word \(w\):

$$ \text{Frequency}(w) = F(w) $$
$$ \text{Degree}(w) = D(w) \quad (\text{sum of frequencies of words co-occurring with } w) $$

The score for an individual word is then:

$$ \text{Score}(w) = \frac{D(w)}{F(w)} $$

Candidate phrases are scored by summing the scores of their constituent words. This simple ratio helps filter out very common words that might appear frequently but don't have strong co-occurrence links, while boosting words that act as "hubs" in the text.

YAKE: Combining Statistical Features

YAKE computes a score for each term (word or phrase) based on several features. The final score \(S(t)\) for a term \(t\) is a weighted combination of these features. A simplified view considers:

$$ S(t) = \frac{\text{Term Frequency}(t) \times \text{Term Position}(t)}{\text{Context Score}(t) \times (1 + \text{Diversity Score}(t))} $$

Where:

  • \(\text{Term Frequency}(t)\): How often \(t\) appears.
  • \(\text{Term Position}(t)\): Average position of \(t\) in sentences (lower is better).
  • \(\text{Context Score}(t)\): Reflects how many unique words precede/follow \(t\).
  • \(\text{Diversity Score}(t)\): Penalizes terms that are too similar to other candidate terms.
A lower \(S(t)\) indicates a more important keyword.

KeyBERT: Semantic Similarity with MMR

KeyBERT's strength comes from contextual embeddings. If \(D\) is the document embedding and \(K_i\) is the embedding for candidate keyword \(i\), their similarity is typically cosine similarity:

$$ \text{Similarity}(D, K_i) = \frac{D \cdot K_i}{\|D\| \|K_i\|} $$

To ensure diversity, KeyBERT employs Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR). Given a set of candidate keywords \(R\) and already selected keywords \(S\), MMR selects the next keyword \(k_j \in R \setminus S\) that maximizes:

$$ \lambda \cdot \text{Similarity}(D, k_j) - (1-\lambda) \cdot \max_{k_i \in S} \text{Similarity}(k_j, k_i) $$

Here, \(\lambda\) is a parameter (usually between 0 and 1) that balances relevance to the document and diversity from selected keywords. A high \(\lambda\) prioritizes relevance; a low \(\lambda\) prioritizes diversity.

⚠️ Practical Considerations

RAKE is fast but lacks semantic understanding. YAKE is robust across languages (if tokenization is handled) but its statistical features can be less powerful than embeddings. KeyBERT is powerful but computationally expensive, requiring a large pre-trained model and more processing time.

Key Takeaways & Best Practices
▼

When to use RAKE:

  • You need a fast, simple, and unsupervised method.
  • Your text is relatively clean and stop words are well-defined for your language.
  • You prioritize multi-word phrases over single terms.
  • Computational resources are limited.

When to use YAKE:

  • You need an unsupervised method that is robust across different domains and languages (given proper tokenization).
  • You want a balance between speed and quality, without needing large pre-trained models.
  • You value statistical features like term position and context.

When to use KeyBERT:

  • You need highly contextual and semantically rich keywords.
  • Your domain requires deep understanding of nuances (e.g., legal, medical texts).
  • You have sufficient computational resources and can leverage large pre-trained models.
  • Diversity in keywords is important (via MMR).

General Advice: Often, a hybrid approach or combining results from different methods can yield the best results. Experiment with different algorithms and tune their parameters for your specific dataset and task.

💡 Evaluating Keywords

Evaluating keyword extraction is tricky. Human judgment is the gold standard, but metrics like Precision, Recall, and F1-score (against a human-annotated gold standard) are commonly used. For unsupervised methods, qualitative assessment is often key.

Code Trace (Python)
▼

Here's a simplified Python code snippet demonstrating the basic usage of RAKE, YAKE, and KeyBERT libraries.

from rake_nltk import Rake
from yake import KeywordExtractor
from keybert import KeyBERT
text = "Keyword extraction is a natural language processing task that identifies the most important words or phrases in a document. RAKE, YAKE, and KeyBERT are popular algorithms for this purpose."
# --- RAKE ---
r = Rake()
r.extract_keywords_from_text(text)
rake_keywords = r.get_ranked_phrases_with_scores()
print("RAKE Keywords:", rake_keywords[:3])
# --- YAKE ---
kw_extractor = KeywordExtractor(top=5, stopwords=None) # top=5 to get 5 keywords
yake_keywords = kw_extractor.extract_keywords(text)
print("YAKE Keywords:", yake_keywords[:3])
# --- KeyBERT ---
kw_model = KeyBERT()
keybert_keywords = kw_model.extract_keywords(text, keyphrase_ngram_range=(1, 3), stop_words='english', top_n=5, use_mmr=True, diversity=0.7)
print("KeyBERT Keywords:", keybert_keywords[:3])
