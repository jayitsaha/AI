Keyword Extraction: RAKE, YAKE, KeyBERT

Unsupervised methods for extracting salient keyphrases from text
Intermediate NLP Text Processing Unsupervised
1
Understanding Keyword Extraction
▼

What is it? Keyword extraction automatically identifies the most important words or phrases in a document without needing any labeled training data. It is the backbone of search engines, content tagging, document summarization, and topic modeling. Three dominant unsupervised approaches have emerged: RAKE (graph-based), YAKE (statistical), and KeyBERT (embedding-based).

Analogy: Imagine reading a newspaper article and highlighting the key terms with a marker. RAKE highlights phrases that appear together often and avoids common filler words. YAKE highlights words that are statistically unusual in their context. KeyBERT reads the entire article for meaning first, then picks the phrases whose meaning best represents the whole article.

If you remember one thing

RAKE uses word co-occurrence graphs and scores phrases by \(\text{Score}(w) = \text{deg}(w) / \text{freq}(w)\). YAKE combines five statistical features (casing, position, frequency, relatedness, sentence count). KeyBERT encodes both the document and candidate phrases with BERT, then ranks by cosine similarity.

2
RAKE: Rapid Automatic Keyword Extraction
▼

RAKE (Rose et al., 2010) is a domain-independent method that exploits the observation that keywords rarely contain stopwords or punctuation. The algorithm splits text at stopword and punctuation boundaries to form candidate phrases, then builds a word co-occurrence matrix from these phrases.

Algorithm Steps

1. Split at delimiters: Stopwords and punctuation act as phrase boundaries. The text "natural language processing is a subfield of artificial intelligence" yields candidates: ["natural language processing", "subfield", "artificial intelligence"].

2. Build co-occurrence graph: For each candidate phrase, every pair of component words co-occurs. In "natural language processing", the words natural, language, and processing each co-occur with the other two.

3. Compute word scores: Each word \(w\) gets a score based on its degree (number of co-occurring words) divided by its frequency:

$$\text{Score}(w) = \frac{\deg(w)}{\text{freq}(w)}$$

4. Score phrases: A phrase's score is the sum of its component word scores. Multi-word phrases accumulate higher scores, which is by design — RAKE favors longer, more specific keyphrases.

Common Pitfall

RAKE's reliance on stopword boundaries means it struggles with languages that lack clear stopword lists. It also over-scores very long phrases because scores are additive — a 5-word phrase will almost always outscore a 2-word phrase regardless of relevance.

3
YAKE and KeyBERT: Statistical vs. Semantic
▼

YAKE: Yet Another Keyword Extractor

YAKE (Campos et al., 2020) is a fully statistical, unsupervised method that assigns importance to each word based on five features computed from the text itself:

(1) Casing \(T_{\text{Case}}\): Words starting with uppercase (outside sentence starts) are more likely proper nouns or key terms. (2) Position \(T_{\text{Pos}}\): Words appearing earlier in the document tend to be more important. (3) Frequency \(T_{\text{Freq}}\): Normalized term frequency, but YAKE penalizes overly frequent words that become stopword-like. (4) Relatedness \(T_{\text{Rel}}\): Measures how many different words appear next to the candidate, penalizing words that co-occur with many distinct neighbors (they tend to be generic). (5) Sentence count \(T_{\text{Sent}}\): Words spread across many sentences are more topically central.

$$S(w) = \frac{T_{\text{Pos}} \cdot T_{\text{Rel}}}{T_{\text{Case}} + \frac{T_{\text{Freq}}}{T_{\text{Rel}}} + \frac{T_{\text{Rel}}}{T_{\text{Case}}}}$$

Lower \(S(w)\) means more important. Multi-word phrases are scored by combining individual word scores with a co-occurrence discount.

KeyBERT: Embedding-Based Extraction

KeyBERT (Grootendorst, 2020) takes a fundamentally different approach by leveraging pre-trained language models. It encodes the full document into a dense vector using a sentence-transformer (e.g., all-MiniLM-L6-v2), then encodes each candidate n-gram separately. The candidates most similar to the document embedding are the keywords:

$$\text{Score}(c) = \cos(\mathbf{e}_{\text{doc}}, \mathbf{e}_c) = \frac{\mathbf{e}_{\text{doc}} \cdot \mathbf{e}_c}{\|\mathbf{e}_{\text{doc}}\| \, \|\mathbf{e}_c\|}$$

KeyBERT optionally applies Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR) to diversify results, avoiding redundant keywords that are too similar to each other.

Key Distinction

RAKE and YAKE are surface-level methods: they operate on word frequencies, positions, and co-occurrence patterns. KeyBERT is a semantic method: it understands meaning. The phrase "ML models" and "machine learning algorithms" would be scored independently by RAKE/YAKE but recognized as semantically similar by KeyBERT.

Decision Guide

Use RAKE when you need speed and simplicity on English text. Use YAKE for multilingual scenarios or when you cannot install heavy dependencies. Use KeyBERT when extraction quality matters most and you can afford the compute cost of running a transformer model.

5
Key Takeaways
▼

RAKE is the simplest approach: split at stopwords, build a co-occurrence graph, and score words by degree/frequency. It inherently favors multi-word phrases and needs zero dependencies.

YAKE replaces the graph with five hand-crafted statistical features. It requires no stopword list and works across languages, but its composite scoring function can be unintuitive to tune.

KeyBERT represents the modern paradigm shift: instead of counting words, it compares the meaning of candidate phrases to the document via cosine similarity in embedding space. This captures synonymy and paraphrase but requires a pre-trained transformer model.

In practice, many NLP pipelines use a cascade: fast statistical extraction (RAKE/YAKE) for initial filtering, followed by semantic re-ranking (KeyBERT) for the final top-k selection.
