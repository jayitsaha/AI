Monotonic policy improvement via KL divergence constraint and conjugate gradient

1
Understanding TRPO
▼

What is it? Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO) is a reinforcement learning algorithm that guarantees monotonic improvement of the policy at each update step. Unlike vanilla policy gradient methods, which can suffer from unstable updates (taking too large a step and "crashing" the policy), TRPO carefully constrains each step in policy space using a KL divergence penalty. This ensures the new policy doesn't stray too far from the old one, leading to more stable and reliable learning.

Analogy: Imagine you're a mountain climber trying to reach the highest peak. A naive climber (vanilla policy gradient) might take huge leaps in the direction of steepest ascent, often overshooting the path, falling into a crevasse, or even going downhill. A TRPO climber, however, always takes steps that guarantee they move uphill, but only within a "trust region" — a small, safe area around their current position. They carefully scout the best path within this safe zone, even if it's not exactly the steepest, ensuring steady progress towards the summit without risky falls.

💡 If you remember one thing

TRPO ensures monotonic policy improvement by maximizing a surrogate objective function subject to a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence constraint between the new and old policies: \(\max_{\theta} \mathbb{E}_{s,a \sim \pi_{\theta_{old}}} \left[ \frac{\pi_{\theta}(a|s)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a|s)} A(s,a) \right] \quad \text{s.t.} \quad D_{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{old}} || \pi_{\theta}) \le \delta\). This prevents catastrophic policy updates.

2
How TRPO Works Internally
▼

The Monotonic Improvement Guarantee

The core insight of TRPO (Schulman et al., 2015) is a theoretical guarantee that a new policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) will always perform at least as well as the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{old}}\), provided the KL divergence between them is sufficiently small. This guarantee comes from a lower bound on the expected return of the new policy. This means TRPO avoids the "policy collapse" problem common in standard policy gradient methods, where a single bad update can drastically reduce performance.

The Surrogate Objective and KL Constraint

TRPO optimizes a surrogate objective function \(L(\theta)\) (a local approximation of the true expected return) over the new policy parameters \(\theta\). This objective is maximized within a trust region defined by a maximum allowable KL divergence \(\delta\) from the old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{old}}\). This constrained optimization problem is typically solved using a second-order approximation:

  • The surrogate objective is approximated by a first-order Taylor expansion around \(\theta_{old}\) (i.e., the policy gradient).
  • The KL divergence constraint is approximated by a second-order Taylor expansion, which involves the Fisher Information Matrix \(F\). This matrix acts as a metric on the policy parameter space, defining the "natural gradient" direction.

The Role of Conjugate Gradient

The constrained optimization problem effectively requires computing a step direction \(\Delta\theta\) by solving a system of linear equations involving the Fisher Information Matrix: \(F \Delta\theta = g\), where \(g\) is the policy gradient. Since \(F\) can be very large for deep neural networks, explicitly inverting it is computationally prohibitive. TRPO employs the conjugate gradient (CG) algorithm to efficiently approximate \(\Delta\theta\) without ever explicitly forming or inverting \(F\). Instead, CG only requires computing matrix-vector products of the form \(Fv\), which can be done efficiently using an R-operator (e.g., R-Hessian-vector products).

🔑 Key Property

TRPO's strength lies in its ability to take the largest possible step towards higher reward, while strictly controlling the policy change via the KL divergence. This is achieved by using the Fisher Information Matrix to define a "natural" gradient direction, and solving for this direction efficiently with conjugate gradient.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

The Policy Improvement Theorem

The theoretical foundation of TRPO is a lower bound on the expected return of a new policy \(\pi_{\theta}\) relative to an old policy \(\pi_{\theta_{old}}\):

$$\eta(\theta) \ge \eta(\theta_{old}) + \mathbb{E}_{s,a \sim \pi_{\theta_{old}}} \left[ \frac{\pi_{\theta}(a|s)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a|s)} A^{\pi_{\theta_{old}}}(s,a) \right] - C \cdot D_{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{old}} || \pi_{\theta})$$

where \(\eta(\theta)\) is the expected return, \(A^{\pi_{\theta_{old}}}(s,a)\) is the advantage function, and \(C\) is a constant related to the maximum possible advantage. This bound shows that if \(D_{KL}\) is small, we can guarantee improvement. TRPO aims to maximize the right-hand side, leading to the surrogate objective.

The Constrained Optimization Problem

TRPO seeks to find an update \(\Delta\theta = \theta - \theta_{old}\) by solving the following constrained optimization problem:

$$\max_{\Delta\theta} \left[ g^\top \Delta\theta \right] \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \frac{1}{2} \Delta\theta^\top F \Delta\theta \le \delta$$

Here, \(g = \nabla_{\theta} \mathbb{E}_{s,a \sim \pi_{\theta_{old}}} \left[ \frac{\pi_{\theta}(a|s)}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(a|s)} A(s,a) \right] \Big|_{\theta=\theta_{old}}\) is the policy gradient (first-order approximation of the surrogate objective), and \(F = \mathbb{E}_{s \sim \pi_{\theta_{old}}} \left[ \nabla_{\theta} D_{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{old}}(\cdot|s) || \pi_{\theta}(\cdot|s)) \Big|_{\theta=\theta_{old}} \right]\) is the Fisher Information Matrix (second-order approximation of the KL constraint). This is a quadratic program with a linear objective and quadratic constraint.

Solving with Conjugate Gradient

The solution to this problem is given by \(\Delta\theta = \sqrt{\frac{2\delta}{g^\top F^{-1} g}} F^{-1} g\), followed by a line search. The key challenge is computing \(F^{-1} g\). This is solved by finding \(v\) such that \(Fv = g\), which is a linear system. Conjugate gradient (CG) solves \(Ax=b\) iteratively by minimizing \(\frac{1}{2}x^\top Ax - b^\top x\). In TRPO, CG approximates \(F^{-1}g\) by finding \(v\) that minimizes \(\frac{1}{2}v^\top F v - g^\top v\). Importantly, CG only needs to compute \(F \cdot \text{vector}\) products, not \(F\) itself, which is done via the R-operator: \(F v \approx \frac{\nabla D_{KL}(\theta_{old} || \theta_{old} + \epsilon v) - \nabla D_{KL}(\theta_{old} || \theta_{old})}{\epsilon}\).

📐 The Natural Gradient Update

The direction \(F^{-1}g\) is known as the natural gradient. It's the steepest ascent direction in the Riemannian manifold defined by the Fisher metric, meaning it takes into account the curvature of the policy space. This allows TRPO to take larger, more effective steps than vanilla gradient ascent, which only considers Euclidean distance.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use TRPO:

  • When training policies for continuous control tasks where stability and monotonic improvement are critical.
  • For complex, high-dimensional policy networks where vanilla policy gradient methods struggle with hyperparameter sensitivity (e.g., learning rate).
  • When you need strong theoretical guarantees about policy performance.

When NOT to use:

  • For tasks with discrete action spaces, where the KL divergence formulation might be less natural or harder to implement.
  • When computational efficiency is paramount, as TRPO's second-order optimization (even with CG) is more expensive per step than first-order methods. Simpler algorithms like PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) offer a good balance of stability and speed.
  • For very simple environments where basic policy gradient methods already perform well enough.
💡 TRPO vs. PPO

TRPO's computational cost led to the development of PPO, which approximates the KL constraint with a clipped surrogate objective, making it much simpler and faster to implement while retaining many of TRPO's stability benefits. PPO is often the default choice for many modern RL applications.
