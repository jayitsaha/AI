Aligning Large Language Models with human preferences, safety, and helpfulness

1
Understanding RLHF
▼

What is it? Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) is a technique used to align large language models (LLMs) with complex human values like helpfulness, harmlessness, and honesty. After an LLM is initially trained on a massive text corpus (pre-training) and potentially fine-tuned on instruction data (SFT), RLHF further refines its behavior by incorporating direct human preferences. It uses a reward model, trained on human rankings of LLM outputs, to provide a scalar reward signal, which then guides a reinforcement learning algorithm (like PPO) to update the LLM's policy.

Analogy: Imagine teaching a child to write polite thank-you notes. Initially, they might just list gifts (pre-training). You then give them examples of good notes (SFT). With RLHF, you'd then let them write a few, and for each pair of notes, you'd say which one you prefer. A "preference judge" (reward model) learns your taste from these comparisons. Finally, the child (LLM) practices writing more notes, with the judge giving instant feedback (reward) on how well each note matches your style, allowing them to rapidly improve.

💡 If you remember one thing

RLHF is a three-step pipeline: (1) Pre-train/SFT an LLM, (2) Train a Reward Model (RM) from human preference data, and (3) Fine-tune the LLM using Reinforcement Learning (RL) with the RM as the reward function.

2
How It Works Internally: The Three Steps
▼

Step 1: Pre-training & Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT)

First, a base LLM is pre-trained on a vast amount of text data to learn language patterns, facts, and reasoning abilities. This model is then often Supervised Fine-tuned (SFT) on a dataset of high-quality human-written (or carefully filtered) instruction-response pairs. This makes the model better at following instructions and generating helpful responses, establishing a baseline "policy" for the RL stage.

Step 2: Reward Model (RM) Training

This is where human feedback comes in. For a diverse set of prompts, the SFT model (or multiple models) generates several different responses. Human annotators then rank these responses from best to worst according to a set of guidelines (e.g., helpfulness, harmlessness, coherence). This preference data (e.g., "Response A is better than Response B") is used to train a separate neural network, the Reward Model (RM). The RM takes a prompt and a response as input and outputs a single scalar score, which represents its prediction of human preference. The RM is typically a copy of the LLM, but with its final layer adapted to output a scalar.

Step 3: Reinforcement Learning Fine-tuning

Finally, the SFT model is further fine-tuned using a reinforcement learning algorithm, most commonly Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). The SFT model now acts as the "policy" (the agent), generating responses (actions) to prompts (states). The previously trained Reward Model (RM) serves as the "environment," providing a reward signal for each generated response. The PPO algorithm updates the LLM's weights to maximize the reward from the RM, while also including a KL divergence penalty to prevent the model from drifting too far from its initial SFT policy (which could lead to generating nonsensical or unhelpful text).

Design Decision: Why PPO (or similar RL algorithm)?

Directly fine-tuning an LLM on human preference data is hard because human feedback is sparse and expensive. The RM acts as a scalable proxy for human judgment. PPO is chosen for its stability and efficiency in policy gradient methods, allowing the LLM to learn from the RM's continuous reward signal without requiring extensive new human labels for every improvement. The KL penalty is crucial for preventing "reward hacking" (generating high-reward but low-quality text) and ensuring the model remains coherent and aligned with general language distributions.

🔑 Key Property

The Reward Model learns a continuous, differentiable approximation of human preferences, turning sparse human comparisons into a dense reward signal that can be used to efficiently optimize the LLM via standard reinforcement learning techniques.

3
Why It Works — The Math Behind Each Stage
▼

Reward Model Training (Pairwise Ranking Loss)

The Reward Model \(r_\phi(x, y)\) is trained to output a scalar score for a given prompt \(x\) and response \(y\). Given human preference data \((x, y_w, y_l)\), where \(y_w\) is preferred over \(y_l\), the RM is trained using a pairwise ranking loss. This loss encourages \(r_\phi(x, y_w)\) to be higher than \(r_\phi(x, y_l)\). A common choice is a logistic loss derived from the Bradley-Terry model:

$$L(\phi) = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \log\left(\sigma\left(r_\phi(x_i, y_{w,i}) - r_\phi(x_i, y_{l,i})\right)\right)$$

where \(\sigma\) is the sigmoid function. This loss maximizes the probability that the preferred response receives a higher score than the dispreferred one.

Reinforcement Learning (PPO Objective)

The LLM, now denoted as the policy \(\pi_\theta\), is fine-tuned using PPO. The objective function for PPO is designed to maximize the expected reward while ensuring the new policy \(\pi_\theta\) doesn't deviate too much from the previous policy \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) (typically the SFT model or the policy from the previous PPO iteration). A simplified PPO objective (ignoring the clipping term for brevity) includes a KL divergence penalty:

$$J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim D} \left[ r_\phi(x, y) - \beta \cdot D_{KL}(\pi_\theta(\cdot|x) \,||\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)) \right]$$

Here, \(r_\phi(x, y)\) is the reward from the RM. \(D_{KL}\) is the Kullback-Leibler divergence, which measures how much \(\pi_\theta\) has changed from a reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) (often the SFT model or the policy at the start of the PPO phase). The hyperparameter \(\beta\) controls the strength of this KL penalty. A larger \(\beta\) keeps \(\pi_\theta\) closer to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), preventing large policy changes and potential "reward hacking" or generation of incoherent text.

The policy gradient update then becomes:

$$\nabla J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim D} \left[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y|x) \left( r_\phi(x, y) - \beta \cdot \frac{\partial}{\partial \log \pi_\theta(y|x)} D_{KL}(\pi_\theta \,||\, \pi_{\text{ref}}) \right) \right]$$

This update encourages the policy to generate responses that yield high rewards from the RM, while the KL term regularizes the policy to stay within a reasonable distributional neighborhood of the reference model.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

The quality of the Reward Model is paramount. If the RM is poorly trained or biased, it will propagate these issues to the LLM during RL fine-tuning. This can lead to "reward hacking" (the LLM finding ways to get high scores from the RM without actually being helpful/harmless) or simply training the LLM to optimize for undesirable behaviors.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use RLHF:

  • When you need to align LLMs with complex, subjective human values (e.g., helpfulness, harmlessness, creativity, tone) that are hard to specify with explicit rules or static datasets.
  • To reduce undesirable behaviors like hallucination, toxicity, or refusal to answer.
  • To fine-tune models for specific domain expertise where nuances of "good" responses are best captured by human experts.
  • When you want the model to generate responses that are not just factually correct but also socially acceptable and user-friendly.

When NOT to use / Challenges:

  • Cost: Human annotation is expensive and time-consuming.
  • Scalability: Collecting enough diverse and high-quality human preference data can be a bottleneck for very large models or new domains.
  • Reward Hacking: The LLM might learn to exploit flaws in the RM to get high scores without actually improving its behavior in a human-aligned way.
  • Human Bias: The RM can inadvertently learn and amplify biases present in the human feedback data.
  • Model Collapse: Over-optimization on the RM, especially with a weak KL penalty, can lead to the policy drifting too far from the original language distribution, resulting in incoherent or repetitive outputs.
  • Explainability: The "why" behind an RM's score or a PPO update can be opaque, making debugging difficult.
💡 Big Picture

RLHF is a powerful bridge between human values and LLM behavior. It transforms fuzzy human preferences into a quantifiable reward signal, enabling iterative improvement through reinforcement learning. While effective, its success hinges on the quality and diversity of human feedback and careful management of the RL training process.
