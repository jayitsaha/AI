The 30-Second Version
▼

What is this? Bayesian inference is a principled way to update your beliefs in the light of evidence. You start with a prior — what you believe before seeing any data — then combine it with the likelihood of the data under different hypotheses to get a posterior — your updated belief. The evidence (also called the marginal likelihood) is the normalizing constant that makes the posterior a valid probability distribution, and it also serves as the key quantity for comparing competing models.

Analogy: Imagine you suspect a coin might be biased. Your prior says "I think it's probably fair." You flip it 10 times and get 5 heads. The likelihood measures how probable that outcome is for every possible bias value. The posterior is your updated belief about the coin's bias after seeing those flips — a compromise between your prior intuition and the data signal. The evidence is the overall probability of observing exactly those 10 flips, averaging across all possible biases — it lets you compare "maybe it's biased toward heads" versus "maybe it's a fair coin" to pick the better model.

💡 If you remember one thing

Bayes' theorem in one line: $$p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}) = \frac{p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)}{p(\mathcal{D})}$$ Posterior = (Likelihood × Prior) / Evidence. The evidence \(p(\mathcal{D}) = \int p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)\, d\theta\) is just a normalization constant for the single-model case, but becomes the model's "score" when comparing models via Bayes factors.

Why This Exists — Historical Context
▼

The Problem That Motivated Bayes

Classical (frequentist) statistics defines probability only through long-run frequencies — "the probability of heads is 0.5 because in infinitely many flips, half come up heads." This makes it impossible to talk about the probability of a hypothesis (which is either true or false, not the result of a repeating experiment). You can't say "there's a 70% chance this coin is biased" in the frequentist view.

The problem Rev. Thomas Bayes addressed (posthumously, 1763): given observed data, how do we rationally update the probability we assign to a hypothesis?

Key Milestones

1763 — Bayes & Price: "An Essay towards solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances" — introduced the inverse probability problem, the seed of the theorem. 1812 — Laplace: Generalized to continuous parameters and coined the term "probability of causes." 1930s–1960s: Frequentist revolution (Fisher, Neyman-Pearson) pushed Bayesian ideas to the margins. 1990s: MCMC algorithms (Metropolis-Hastings, Gibbs) made Bayesian inference computationally tractable, triggering a renaissance. 2000s–present: Variational Inference, Laplace approximation, and deep learning priors integrate Bayes into modern ML.

Core Concepts & The Math
▼

The Four Components

Let \(\theta\) be the unknown parameter (e.g., coin bias) and \(\mathcal{D}\) be the observed data (e.g., 5 heads in 10 flips). Bayes' theorem is:

$$\underbrace{p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})}_{\text{posterior}} = \frac{\overbrace{p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)}^{\text{likelihood}} \cdot \overbrace{p(\theta)}^{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{p(\mathcal{D})}_{\text{evidence}}}$$

Prior \(p(\theta)\): Your belief about \(\theta\) before seeing data. Encodes domain knowledge. A Beta(2,8) prior for a coin bias says "I believe the coin is probably biased toward tails." Informativeness matters: a flat (uniform) prior says "all values equally plausible."

Likelihood \(p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)\): The probability of observing the data, as a function of \(\theta\). For a coin: \(p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta) = \binom{n}{k}\theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k}\). Note: the likelihood is not a probability distribution over \(\theta\) — it doesn't integrate to 1 in \(\theta\).

Posterior \(p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})\): Your belief about \(\theta\) after seeing data. Always a valid probability distribution over \(\theta\). It is the product prior × likelihood, normalized.

Evidence \(p(\mathcal{D})\): The marginal likelihood — the probability of the data averaging over all parameter values:

$$p(\mathcal{D}) = \int p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)\, d\theta$$

For our Beta-Binomial example: \(p(\mathcal{D}) \approx 0.0514\). This single number is model-specific and enables model comparison via Bayes factors: \(BF_{12} = p(\mathcal{D} \mid M_1) / p(\mathcal{D} \mid M_2)\).

Conjugate Priors (Exact Posterior)

When the prior and posterior belong to the same distribution family, it is called a conjugate prior. This gives a closed-form posterior. For a Binomial likelihood:

$$\text{Prior: } \theta \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta) \quad \xrightarrow{\text{observe } k \text{ successes in } n} \quad \text{Posterior: } \theta \mid \mathcal{D} \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha + k,\, \beta + n - k)$$

The parameters \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) act like pseudo-counts: \(\alpha\) is the effective number of prior successes, \(\beta\) the prior failures. The posterior mode is \((\alpha+k-1)/(\alpha+\beta+n-2)\), and the mean is \((\alpha+k)/(\alpha+\beta+n)\).

🔑 Key Property: Posterior Mean = Weighted Mixture

The posterior mean lies between the prior mean and the MLE:
\(\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid \mathcal{D}] = \lambda \cdot \hat{\theta}_{MLE} + (1-\lambda) \cdot \mu_{\text{prior}}\)   where   \(\lambda = \frac{n}{\alpha+\beta+n}\).
As \(n \to \infty\), the data dominates and the posterior collapses onto the MLE. With small \(n\), the prior exerts strong pull — it acts as regularization.

Full Derivation — Evidence as Normalization

From the joint distribution \(p(\theta, \mathcal{D}) = p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)\), we get two conditional forms:

$$p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D}) = \frac{p(\theta, \mathcal{D})}{p(\mathcal{D})} = \frac{p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)}{\int p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta')\, p(\theta')\, d\theta'}$$

The denominator is the evidence. For a Beta(2,8) prior and Binomial(10, θ) likelihood with k=5:

$$p(\mathcal{D}) = \binom{10}{5} \frac{B(2+5,\; 8+5)}{B(2,\;8)} = 252 \cdot \frac{B(7,13)}{B(2,8)} \approx 0.05144$$

where \(B(a,b) = \Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)/\Gamma(a+b)\) is the Beta function.

⚠️ Common Misconception

The likelihood \(p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)\) is not a probability distribution over \(\theta\). It can take any positive value and does not integrate to 1 in \(\theta\). The prior is what makes it a proper probability model. Also: a flatter prior does NOT mean "no assumptions" — the improper uniform prior on \(\mathbb{R}\) can yield improper posteriors.

Bayes Factors for Model Comparison

Given two competing models \(M_1\) (Beta(2,8) prior) and \(M_2\) (uniform/Beta(1,1) prior):

$$BF_{12} = \frac{p(\mathcal{D} \mid M_1)}{p(\mathcal{D} \mid M_2)} = \frac{0.05144}{0.09091} \approx 0.566$$

A Bayes factor below 1 means the data favors \(M_2\). Here, the uniform prior fits the data better than the strong tail-bias prior — the 5 heads out of 10 flips is more consistent with a prior that didn't strongly predict tails.

From Scratch — Beta-Binomial Bayesian Update

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import beta as beta_dist, binom
from scipy.special import beta as beta_fn
def bayesian_update(alpha0, beta0, n_successes, n_trials):
"""Beta-Binomial conjugate Bayesian update."""
# Prior: Beta(alpha0, beta0)
prior_mean = alpha0 / (alpha0 + beta0)
prior_mode = (alpha0 - 1) / (alpha0 + beta0 - 2) if alpha0 > 1 else 0
# Posterior: Beta(alpha0 + k, beta0 + n - k) [conjugate update]
alpha_post = alpha0 + n_successes
beta_post = beta0 + (n_trials - n_successes)
post_mean = alpha_post / (alpha_post + beta_post)
post_mode = (alpha_post-1) / (alpha_post+beta_post-2)
# Evidence: p(D) = C(n,k) * B(a+k, b+n-k) / B(a, b)
from scipy.special import comb
evidence = (comb(n_trials, n_successes, exact=True)
* beta_fn(alpha_post, beta_post)
/ beta_fn(alpha0, beta0))
return {"alpha_post": alpha_post, "beta_post": beta_post,
"post_mean": post_mean, "post_mode": post_mode,
"evidence": evidence}
# Example: prior Beta(2,8), observe 5 heads in 10 flips
result = bayesian_update(alpha0=2, beta0=8, n_successes=5, n_trials=10)
print(f"Posterior: Beta({result['alpha_post']}, {result['beta_post']})")
print(f"Posterior mean: {result['post_mean']:.4f} (MLE = 0.5000)")
print(f"Evidence p(D): {result['evidence']:.6f}")

Production Usage — PyMC

import pymc as pm
import numpy as np
data = np.array([1,0,1,1,0,1,0,0,1,0]) # 5 heads, 5 tails
with pm.Model() as coin_model:
# Prior
theta = pm.Beta("theta", alpha=2, beta=8)
# Likelihood
obs = pm.Bernoulli("obs", p=theta, observed=data)
# Posterior via MCMC (or use pm.find_MAP() for point estimate)
trace = pm.sample(2000, tune=1000, return_inferencedata=True)
# Evidence via bridge sampling (for model comparison)
# log_ml = pm.compute_log_marginal_likelihood()
pm.plot_posterior(trace, var_names=["theta"]) # shows posterior dist
# ⚠️ Gotcha: evidence ≠ posterior — don't confuse pm.logp (joint log-prob)
# with the marginal likelihood. Use bridge_sampling for evidence.

Interview Deep-Dive & Key Takeaways
▼

Top Interview Questions

Q1 (Easy): What is the difference between the prior and posterior?
The prior \(p(\theta)\) encodes beliefs about \(\theta\) before seeing data. The posterior \(p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})\) is the updated belief after incorporating the data via Bayes' theorem. The prior is a marginal; the posterior is a conditional.

Q2 (Easy): What is the likelihood function?
\(p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta)\) viewed as a function of \(\theta\) for fixed observed data \(\mathcal{D}\). It is not a probability distribution over \(\theta\) — it doesn't integrate to 1 in \(\theta\). The MLE finds the \(\theta\) that maximizes it. MAP estimation adds the prior log-probability as regularization.

Q3 (Medium): What is the evidence (marginal likelihood) and why does it matter?
\(p(\mathcal{D}) = \int p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta) p(\theta) d\theta\). In the single-model case it's just a normalizing constant. Its power lies in model comparison: given models \(M_1, M_2\), the Bayes factor \(BF_{12} = p(\mathcal{D} \mid M_1)/p(\mathcal{D} \mid M_2)\) quantifies the evidence for \(M_1\) over \(M_2\) without requiring held-out test data. Models with more parameters are automatically penalized if the extra complexity doesn't improve predictions — this is the Bayesian Occam's razor.

Q4 (Medium): What is a conjugate prior?
A prior is conjugate to a likelihood if the posterior has the same functional form as the prior. Example: Beta prior + Binomial likelihood = Beta posterior. This enables closed-form Bayesian updates. Conjugacy exists for exponential family likelihoods (Gaussian-Gaussian, Poisson-Gamma, Multinomial-Dirichlet, etc.).

Q5 (Hard): MAP vs MLE vs Posterior Mean — when do they differ?
MAP = mode of posterior = MLE + log-prior penalty (like L2 regularization with Gaussian prior). Posterior Mean = \(\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid \mathcal{D}]\), which can differ from the mode for skewed posteriors. They coincide when: (a) the posterior is symmetric, or (b) data overwhelm the prior (\(n \to \infty\)). For asymmetric posteriors like Beta(1.5, 10), mode is 0.05 but mean is 0.130 — use the mean as the minimum MSE estimate.

Q6 (Hard): How does the evidence penalize over-complex models?
The evidence \(p(\mathcal{D} \mid M)\) averages the likelihood over all parameter values the model assigns non-zero prior to. A complex model with many free parameters spreads prior mass over a huge parameter space — most of it predicting data very different from what was actually observed. The evidence is therefore small (the model "wastes" prior probability on irrelevant regions). A simpler model concentrating prior mass near the truth scores higher evidence for that data, even if a more complex model can achieve higher maximum likelihood.

🎯 System Design Angle

In production ML: Bayesian priors appear as regularizers (L2 reg = Gaussian prior, L1 reg = Laplace prior). Evidence-based model selection avoids cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning (relevant in small-data regimes). Evidence lower bounds (ELBO) in Variational Inference approximate the intractable log-evidence — this is the objective function in VAEs and BBVI.

When to Use Bayesian Inference

  • Small data: prior regularizes and prevents overfitting
  • Sequential learning: posterior becomes the next prior as new data arrives
  • Uncertainty quantification needed: full posterior vs point estimate
  • Model comparison without held-out data
  • Expert knowledge available to specify meaningful priors

When NOT to Use

  • Very large data: MLE is asymptotically equivalent and computationally cheaper
  • Prior is completely unknown and misspecification risk is high
  • Evidence is intractable and approximation budget is limited
