Understanding Exact Inference
▼

What is it? In probabilistic graphical models (PGMs) like Bayesian Networks or Markov Random Fields, "inference" means calculating the probability of certain variables given observations (evidence). Exact inference refers to methods that compute these probabilities precisely, without approximation. This is often framed as finding \(P(Query | Evidence)\).

Why is it important? Exact inference provides ground truth probabilities, crucial for decision-making systems where accuracy is paramount (e.g., medical diagnosis, financial modeling). It also serves as a benchmark for approximate inference methods, which are used when exact methods become computationally intractable.

Analogy: Imagine a complex legal case with many interconnected witnesses, pieces of evidence, and potential outcomes. Exact inference is like a meticulous detective (or a supercomputer running simulations) who can perfectly calculate the probability of the defendant's guilt given all available information, without guessing or taking shortcuts. Two primary strategies for this are:

  • Variable Elimination (VE): Systematically working through the evidence, combining related pieces and summing out irrelevant variables one by one until only the query remains.
  • Belief Propagation (BP): Witnesses (variables) and investigators (factors) passing "messages" back and forth, iteratively updating their beliefs until a consensus is reached about the probability of each outcome.

💡 If you remember one thing

Exact inference methods (like Variable Elimination and Belief Propagation) guarantee correct posterior probabilities in PGMs. Their computational complexity is highly dependent on the graph structure, often exponential in the "tree-width" of the graph.

2
How It Works: VE & BP
▼

1. Representing the Model: Factor Graphs

Both Variable Elimination and Belief Propagation are best understood using factor graphs. A factor graph is a bipartite graph with two types of nodes:

  • Variable nodes: Represent random variables (e.g., Cloudy, Sprinkler).
  • Factor nodes: Represent functions (factors) that relate a subset of variables. In Bayesian Networks, CPTs (Conditional Probability Tables) are converted into factors.
Edges only exist between variable nodes and factor nodes they participate in. This explicit representation of factors simplifies the inference algorithms.

2. Variable Elimination (VE)

VE is an algorithm for computing marginal probabilities or conditional probabilities \(P(Q | E)\) by systematically eliminating (summing out) non-query, non-evidence variables. It involves two basic operations:

  1. Factor Multiplication (Product): If two factors share variables, multiply their entries for all joint assignments of those variables, creating a new, larger factor. For example, \(\phi_1(A,B) \cdot \phi_2(B,C) = \phi_{new}(A,B,C)\).
  2. Variable Summation (Sum-Out): To eliminate a variable \(X\) from a factor \(\phi(X,Y,Z)\), sum over all possible values of \(X\), creating a new factor that no longer depends on \(X\). For example, \(\sum_X \phi(X,Y,Z) = \phi_{new}(Y,Z)\).

The order of elimination is crucial: a bad order can lead to very large intermediate factors, making the computation intractable. The goal is to choose an order that minimizes the size of these intermediate factors (related to the graph's tree-width).

3. Belief Propagation (BP) / Sum-Product Algorithm

Belief Propagation (specifically the Sum-Product algorithm) computes all marginals \(P(X_i)\) for all variables \(X_i\) in a factor graph. It works by passing "messages" between adjacent variable and factor nodes:

  1. Initialization: All messages are initialized (e.g., to 1).
  2. Message Passing: Messages are iteratively passed between nodes. A node can send a message to a neighbor only after it has received messages from all its other neighbors. This process continues until messages converge (or for a fixed number of iterations in loopy BP).
  3. Belief Calculation: Once messages have converged, the belief (marginal probability) for each variable node is computed as the product of all messages it received from its neighboring factor nodes.

For factor graphs that are trees (or polytrees, which can be converted to trees), BP is guaranteed to converge and yield exact marginals in a finite number of steps. For general graphs with cycles (loopy graphs), Loopy Belief Propagation is an approximate method that may or may not converge, but often works well in practice.

🔑 Key Difference

Variable Elimination is query-specific (computes one marginal or conditional) and sensitive to elimination order. Belief Propagation (on trees) computes all marginals simultaneously and is exact, but becomes approximate (Loopy BP) on general graphs with cycles.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Joint Distribution and Marginalization

The core task of inference is to compute marginal probabilities from a joint distribution. For a Bayesian Network with variables \(\mathbf{X} = \{X_1, \ldots, X_n\}\), the joint probability is given by the product of CPTs:

$$P(\mathbf{X}) = \prod_{i=1}^n P(X_i | \text{Pa}(X_i))$$

To find the marginal probability of a variable \(X_k\), we sum the joint distribution over all other variables:

$$P(X_k) = \sum_{X_1, \ldots, X_{k-1}, X_{k+1}, \ldots, X_n} P(\mathbf{X})$$

Performing this sum naively is intractable for many variables. Variable Elimination rearranges the summation and product operations to make it efficient.

Variable Elimination Derivation

Consider a simple chain \(A \to B \to C \to D\). To find \(P(D)\), the naive sum is:

$$P(D) = \sum_A \sum_B \sum_C P(A)P(B|A)P(C|B)P(D|C)$$

By pushing summations inside, we can eliminate variables sequentially:

$$P(D) = \sum_C P(D|C) \sum_B P(C|B) \sum_A P(A)P(B|A)$$

This reveals the core operations of VE. We define new intermediate factors:

  • \(\tau_1(B) = \sum_A P(A)P(B|A)\) (Eliminate A)
  • \(\tau_2(C) = \sum_B P(C|B)\tau_1(B)\) (Eliminate B)
  • \(\tau_3(D) = \sum_C P(D|C)\tau_2(C)\) (Eliminate C)

The final factor \(\tau_3(D)\) is proportional to \(P(D)\). Normalizing it yields the true marginal. Each \(\tau\) is a new factor formed by multiplying existing factors and summing out a variable. The factor graph representation makes these operations explicit.

Belief Propagation (Sum-Product) Messages

The messages passed in Belief Propagation are essentially partial marginals. Let \(m_{X \to f}(X)\) be the message from variable node \(X\) to factor node \(f\), and \(m_{f \to X}(X)\) be the message from factor node \(f\) to variable node \(X\).

  • Variable-to-Factor Message: A variable node \(X\) sends a message to a factor node \(f\) by multiplying all messages it received from its other neighboring factor nodes: $$m_{X \to f}(X) = \prod_{f' \in \text{Ne}(X) \setminus \{f\}} m_{f' \to X}(X)$$
  • Factor-to-Variable Message: A factor node \(f\) sends a message to a variable node \(X\) by multiplying its own function with all messages it received from its other neighboring variable nodes, and then summing out all variables in its scope except \(X\): $$m_{f \to X}(X) = \sum_{\mathbf{X}_f \setminus \{X\}} f(\mathbf{X}_f) \prod_{X' \in \text{Ne}(f) \setminus \{X\}} m_{X' \to f}(X')$$ where \(\mathbf{X}_f\) are the variables in the scope of factor \(f\).

After all messages have been passed (which takes two passes in a tree: collect from leaves to root, then distribute from root to leaves), the unnormalized belief \(Bel(X)\) for any variable \(X\) is the product of all messages it received from its neighboring factors:

$$Bel(X) = \prod_{f \in \text{Ne}(X)} m_{f \to X}(X)$$

Normalizing \(Bel(X)\) gives \(P(X)\). This system is mathematically equivalent to Variable Elimination, but structured for distributed computation.

➗ Connection to Marginalization

Both VE and BP are instances of the sum-product algorithm. VE computes a single marginal by explicit factor manipulation, while BP computes all marginals by propagating partial sums/products as messages. They are fundamentally performing the same marginalization operations, just organized differently.

Key Takeaways

    
▼

  

  

    
When to use Exact Inference:

    
    
      
  • When the graph structure is simple (e.g., a tree, polytree, or low tree-width).
    • 
      
  • When high precision is required and computational resources allow.
    • 
      
  • For smaller models or as a ground truth for debugging/evaluating approximate methods.
    • 
    

    
Variable Elimination:

    
    
      
  • Pros: Conceptually simple, always exact, flexible for different queries.
    • 
      
  • Cons: Computational complexity is exponential in the "tree-width" (size of largest intermediate factor). Elimination order heavily impacts performance. Computes one query at a time.
    • 
    

    
Belief Propagation (Sum-Product on Trees):

    
    
      
  • Pros: Computes all marginals simultaneously, naturally parallelizable, intuitive message-passing framework, exact on trees/polytrees.
    • 
      
  • Cons: On general (loopy) graphs, it's approximate (Loopy BP), convergence not guaranteed, and can oscillate or give poor results.
    • 
    

    
Limitations of Exact Inference:

    
    
      
  • Intractability: For dense or high tree-width graphs (e.g., many variables with many parents), exact inference quickly becomes computationally infeasible (NP-hard in general).
    • 
      
  • Scalability: Does not scale well to very large networks or continuous variables without discretization.
    • 
    

    

      
💡 Practical Advice

      
In real-world applications with large, complex PGMs (e.g., computer vision, natural language processing), exact inference is rarely feasible. Instead, various approximate inference methods (e.g., Markov Chain Monte Carlo, variational inference) are employed, sacrificing some accuracy for computational tractability.

def variable_elimination(factors, query_vars, evidence={}):

      
    # Apply evidence: set variables to fixed values & remove them

      
    for var, val in evidence.items():

      
        factors = [f.restrict(var, val) for f in factors] # Helper: filter factor entries

      

      
    # Determine elimination order (heuristic: min-degree, min-fill, etc.)

      
    all_vars = list(set.union(*[set(f.variables) for f in factors]))

      
    elim_vars = [v for v in all_vars if v not in query_vars and v not in evidence]

      
    elim_order = get_elimination_order(elim_vars, factors) # Crucial heuristic

      

      
    for var_to_elim in elim_order:

      
        # Collect all factors involving var_to_elim

      
        factors_with_var = [f for f in factors if var_to_elim in f.variables]

      
        remaining_factors = [f for f in factors if var_to_elim not in f.variables]

      

      
        # Multiply them all together

      
        product_factor = factors_with_var[0]

      
        for i in range(1, len(factors_with_var)):

      
            product_factor = product_factor.multiply(factors_with_var[i])

      

      
        # Sum out the variable

      
        new_factor = product_factor.sum_out(var_to_elim)

      
        factors = remaining_factors + [new_factor]

      

      
    # Final product of remaining factors

      
    final_factor = factors[0]

      
    for i in range(1, len(factors)):

      
        final_factor = final_factor.multiply(factors[i])

      

      
    # Normalize to get probabilities

      
    total_sum = sum(final_factor.data.values())

      
    normalized_data = {k: v / total_sum for k, v in final_factor.data.items()}

      
    return Factor(final_factor.variables, normalized_data)
