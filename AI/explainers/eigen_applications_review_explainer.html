The Eigen-Concept in Three Domains
▼

What is an eigenvalue / eigenvector? For a square matrix \(A\), a non-zero vector \(\mathbf{v}\) is an eigenvector if multiplying by \(A\) only scales it — never rotates it:

$$A\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}$$

The scalar \(\lambda\) is the associated eigenvalue. This single equation unlocks three completely different application domains that appear throughout ML.

Three Applications, One Algebraic Idea

This review page connects the abstract eigen-decomposition to three concrete, distinct uses that recur constantly in ML pipelines:

  • PCA: The eigenvectors of the data covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) are the principal components — the directions of maximum variance. The eigenvalue \(\lambda_k\) equals the variance captured along PC\(k\).
  • Graph Laplacian: The eigenvectors of the Laplacian \(L = D - A\) encode the connectivity structure of a graph. The second-smallest eigenvalue (Fiedler value) and its vector reveal the optimal 2-way spectral cut.
  • Markov chains: The left eigenvector of the transition matrix \(P\) for eigenvalue 1 is the stationary distribution \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\) — the long-run frequency each state is visited.
💡 Unifying Insight

In each case, the eigenstructure of a carefully constructed matrix exposes the latent geometry of a problem: the geometry of variance (PCA), the geometry of graph connectivity (Laplacian), and the geometry of long-run dynamics (Markov). The math is identical; only the matrix changes.

2
PCA — Covariance Eigenvectors as Principal Axes
▼

Given \(n\) mean-centered data points \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\), PCA seeks the directions of maximum variance. The covariance matrix is:

$$\Sigma = \frac{1}{n-1} X^\top X \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$$

Because \(\Sigma\) is symmetric positive-semi-definite, it has a complete eigen-decomposition with real, non-negative eigenvalues:

$$\Sigma \mathbf{u}_k = \lambda_k \mathbf{u}_k, \quad \lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_d \geq 0$$

The \(k\)-th eigenvector \(\mathbf{u}_k\) is the \(k\)-th principal component. Projecting the data onto \(\mathbf{u}_1\) gives the 1-D representation retaining the most variance, equal to \(\lambda_1\).

Numerical Example (2-D data)

With 40 correlated points at 30° angle, covariance \(\Sigma \approx \begin{bmatrix}0.678 & 0.377 \\ 0.377 & 0.295\end{bmatrix}\). Eigenvalues: \(\lambda_1 \approx 0.909\), \(\lambda_2 \approx 0.064\). PC1 \(\approx [0.853,\ 0.523]^\top\) — closely aligned with the data's elongation axis. Variance explained: 93.5% by PC1 alone.

🔑 Key Property

PCA is equivalent to finding the best rank-\(k\) approximation of \(X\) in the Frobenius norm (Eckart–Young theorem). Equivalently, it is the SVD of \(X\): the right singular vectors of \(X\) are the eigenvectors of \(\Sigma\).

⚠️ Common Pitfall

Always mean-center (and usually scale) before PCA. Without centering, the first PC tends to point toward the data centroid, not the direction of maximum spread. Unit-variance scaling is important when features have very different physical units.

Graph Laplacian — Fiedler Vector & Spectral Cuts
▼

For an undirected graph \(G = (V, E)\) with adjacency matrix \(A\) and degree matrix \(D = \operatorname{diag}(d_1, \ldots, d_n)\), the unnormalized graph Laplacian is:

$$L = D - A$$

Key algebraic facts: (1) \(L\) is symmetric PSD; (2) \(\mathbf{1}\) (all-ones) is always an eigenvector with eigenvalue 0; (3) the multiplicity of eigenvalue 0 equals the number of connected components.

Fiedler Value and Spectral Clustering

The second-smallest eigenvalue \(\lambda_2 > 0\) (for a connected graph) is the Fiedler value or algebraic connectivity. Its eigenvector \(\mathbf{f}\) (the Fiedler vector) reveals the graph's "softest" cut:

$$\lambda_2 = \min_{\mathbf{x} \perp \mathbf{1}} \frac{\mathbf{x}^\top L \mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}^\top \mathbf{x}} = \min_{\mathbf{x} \perp \mathbf{1}} \frac{\sum_{(i,j)\in E}(x_i - x_j)^2}{\sum_i x_i^2}$$

The Rayleigh quotient form shows that \(\lambda_2\) measures how hard it is to separate the graph. Spectral clustering assigns node \(i\) to cluster A if \(f_i > 0\) and cluster B if \(f_i < 0\).

Numerical Example (6-node, 2-cluster graph)

Nodes 0–2 form one triangle, nodes 3–5 another triangle, connected by a single bridge edge (2,3). Laplacian eigenvalues: \([0,\ 0.438,\ 3,\ 3,\ 3,\ 4.562]\). Fiedler vector: \([+0.46,\ +0.46,\ +0.26,\ -0.26,\ -0.46,\ -0.46]\) — positive entries for the left cluster, negative for the right. The sign exactly identifies the spectral cut across the bridge.

💡 Why It Works

The quadratic form \(\mathbf{x}^\top L \mathbf{x} = \sum_{(i,j)\in E}(x_i-x_j)^2\) measures the total "disagreement" across edges. Minimizing this (subject to \(\mathbf{x}\perp\mathbf{1}\)) finds the smoothest non-trivial labeling — which concentrates like-signed values on densely connected subgraphs.

Markov Chains — Stationary Distribution as Dominant Eigenvector
▼

A (finite, discrete-time) Markov chain on \(n\) states is defined by a row-stochastic transition matrix \(P \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\), where \(P_{ij}\) is the probability of moving from state \(i\) to state \(j\) in one step. The state distribution at time \(t\) evolves as:

$$\boldsymbol{\pi}^{(t+1)} = P^\top \boldsymbol{\pi}^{(t)}$$

A stationary distribution satisfies \(\boldsymbol{\pi}^* = P^\top \boldsymbol{\pi}^*\) — it is a fixed point, or equivalently, a left eigenvector of \(P\) for eigenvalue 1:

$$P^\top \boldsymbol{\pi}^* = 1 \cdot \boldsymbol{\pi}^*, \quad \sum_i \pi^*_i = 1, \quad \pi^*_i \geq 0$$

For irreducible aperiodic chains, the Perron–Frobenius theorem guarantees this \(\boldsymbol{\pi}^*\) is unique and that every initial distribution converges to it.

Numerical Example (3-state weather chain)

States: Sunny (0), Cloudy (1), Rainy (2). Transition matrix \(P\) has rows \([0.7,\ 0.2,\ 0.1]\), \([0.3,\ 0.4,\ 0.3]\), \([0.2,\ 0.3,\ 0.5]\). Solving \(P^\top\boldsymbol{\pi} = \boldsymbol{\pi}\) gives: \(\boldsymbol{\pi}^* \approx [0.457,\ 0.283,\ 0.261]\). Starting from Sunny-only \([1,0,0]\), the chain converges to \(\boldsymbol{\pi}^*\) in ~7 steps (as shown in the stepper).

🔑 Eigenvalue Connection

The spectral gap \(1 - |\lambda_2|\) (gap between the largest and second-largest eigenvalue moduli of \(P\)) governs the mixing time — how quickly the chain reaches stationarity. Larger gap = faster convergence. This is why well-designed MCMC samplers aim for a large spectral gap.

⚠️ Common Confusion

\(P\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}\) (right eigenvector for eigenvalue 1) is trivial — it just says rows sum to 1. The stationary distribution is the LEFT eigenvector \(\boldsymbol{\pi}^\top P = \boldsymbol{\pi}^\top\), i.e., \(P^\top\boldsymbol{\pi} = \boldsymbol{\pi}\). These are different objects.

Key Takeaways
▼

These three applications illustrate the breadth of the eigen-idea in ML:

  • PCA uses \(\Sigma = U\Lambda U^\top\) to rotate data so that axes align with maximum variance directions — reducing dimensionality while retaining the most information.
  • Spectral clustering uses \(L = D - A\) and its Fiedler vector to embed graph nodes on a line, then thresholds to find balanced cuts — bypassing the NP-hard exact cut problem.
  • Markov stationarity uses the dominant left eigenvector of \(P\) to find the long-run equilibrium — the foundation of PageRank, MCMC analysis, and reinforcement learning baselines.
