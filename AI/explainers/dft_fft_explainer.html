The 30-Second Version
▼

What is this? Every signal — a sound wave, a stock price, a heartbeat — is a mixture of hidden patterns that repeat at different speeds (frequencies). The Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) is a mathematical recipe that takes a list of measurements and separates them into those frequency ingredients, like a prism splitting white light into a rainbow.

Real-World Analogy

Imagine pressing several piano keys simultaneously. The resulting sound is a jumble. The DFT is like a music-theory app that listens and tells you exactly which notes (frequencies) are playing and how loud each one is — even though your ear hears them blended.

The problem: The naive DFT requires \(O(n^2)\) operations — for \(n = 10^6\) samples that's a trillion multiplications. The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) exploits symmetry in the DFT matrix to cut this to \(O(n \log n)\) — roughly 20 million operations instead. This difference made real-time digital signal processing and modern telecommunications physically possible.

If You Remember One Thing

The DFT is matrix multiplication by a special matrix filled with complex roots of unity. The FFT computes the same result by recursively splitting the DFT matrix — halving the problem size at each level — achieving \(O(n \log n)\) instead of \(O(n^2)\).

One-sentence summary: The FFT makes it 50,000× faster to compute frequency content of signals by exploiting the divide-and-conquer structure hidden inside the DFT matrix.

How It Works Internally
▼

The DFT as Matrix Multiplication

Given a length-\(n\) signal \(\mathbf{x} = [x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_{n-1}]^\top\), the DFT produces \(\mathbf{X} = [X_0, X_1, \ldots, X_{n-1}]^\top\) where:

$$X_k = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} x_j \, \omega_n^{kj}, \quad \omega_n = e^{-2\pi i/n}$$

This is exactly the matrix-vector product \(\mathbf{X} = F_n \mathbf{x}\) where \(F_n\) is the DFT matrix — an \(n \times n\) complex matrix whose \((k,j)\) entry is \(\omega_n^{kj}\), the \(j\)-th power of the \(k\)-th root of unity. For \(n=8\), the twiddle factors are the 8th roots of unity spaced evenly around the complex unit circle.

The FFT: Cooley-Tukey Radix-2 Divide & Conquer

When \(n = 2^m\), the DFT can be split. Write \(x_j\) as even-indexed and odd-indexed sub-signals:

$$X_k = \underbrace{\sum_{j=0}^{n/2-1} x_{2j}\,\omega_n^{2kj}}_{= \text{DFT}_{n/2}(\text{evens}) \text{ at }k} + \omega_n^k \underbrace{\sum_{j=0}^{n/2-1} x_{2j+1}\,\omega_n^{2kj}}_{= \text{DFT}_{n/2}(\text{odds}) \text{ at }k}$$

Since \(\omega_n^{2} = \omega_{n/2}\), each half is itself a DFT of size \(n/2\). This butterfly recurrence halves the problem at every level, yielding \(\log_2 n\) levels with \(n/2\) butterflies each — total \(\frac{n}{2}\log_2 n\) complex multiplications.

Butterfly Operation

Each butterfly takes two complex values \((a, b)\) and a twiddle factor \(W = \omega_n^k\):

\(\text{top} = a + W \cdot b, \quad \text{bottom} = a - W \cdot b\)

This is a rank-2 operation — two additions and one complex multiplication. In-place butterflies avoid extra memory allocation.

Bit-Reversal Permutation

Before the butterfly passes, inputs must be reordered by bit-reversal: the index \(j\) (in binary) is reversed bit-for-bit to get its destination. For \(n=8\) (3-bit indices): index 1 = 001₂ maps to position 100₂ = 4, index 3 = 011₂ maps to position 110₂ = 6, etc. This "shuffles" the input so that each butterfly stage operates on contiguous memory — enabling in-place computation.

Common Misconception

The FFT does not approximate the DFT — it computes the exact same values, just with fewer arithmetic operations by exploiting the symmetry \(\omega_n^{k+n/2} = -\omega_n^k\).

The Math — DFT Matrix & FFT Derivation
▼

The DFT Matrix \(F_n\)

Let \(\omega_n = e^{-2\pi i/n}\). The DFT matrix is:

$$F_n = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & \cdots & 1 \\ 1 & \omega_n & \omega_n^2 & \cdots & \omega_n^{n-1} \\ 1 & \omega_n^2 & \omega_n^4 & \cdots & \omega_n^{2(n-1)} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 1 & \omega_n^{n-1} & \omega_n^{2(n-1)} & \cdots & \omega_n^{(n-1)^2} \end{bmatrix}$$

Key properties: (1) \(F_n\) is symmetric (\(F_n = F_n^\top\)); (2) \(F_n^{-1} = \frac{1}{n}\overline{F_n}\) — the inverse DFT is the conjugate divided by \(n\); (3) \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}F_n\) is unitary, meaning it preserves inner products (Parseval's theorem).

Cooley-Tukey Derivation (n = 2m)

Split the DFT sum by even/odd indices. Define \(E_k = \sum_{j=0}^{n/2-1} x_{2j}\,\omega_{n/2}^{jk}\) (DFT of evens) and \(O_k = \sum_{j=0}^{n/2-1} x_{2j+1}\,\omega_{n/2}^{jk}\) (DFT of odds). Then for \(k = 0, \ldots, n/2-1\):

$$X_k = E_k + \omega_n^k \cdot O_k \qquad X_{k+n/2} = E_k - \omega_n^k \cdot O_k$$

Both \(X_k\) and \(X_{k+n/2}\) reuse the same \(E_k\) and \(O_k\). The \(\omega_n^k\) term is the twiddle factor. This is applied recursively until the base case \(n=1\), where \(X_0 = x_0\).

Recurrence & Solution

Let \(T(n)\) = number of complex multiplications. The recurrence is:

$$T(n) = 2T\!\left(\frac{n}{2}\right) + \frac{n}{2}, \quad T(1) = 0$$

By the Master Theorem (case 2: \(a=2, b=2, f(n)=n/2=\Theta(n^{\log_2 2})=\Theta(n)\)):

$$T(n) = \frac{n}{2}\log_2 n = O(n \log n)$$

Parseval's Theorem (Energy Preservation)

$$\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} |x_j|^2 = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} |X_k|^2$$

This states that total energy is preserved between time domain and frequency domain — a consequence of \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}F_n\) being unitary.

Numerical Trace for n = 8, x = [1,2,3,4,4,3,2,1]

After bit-reversal permutation (indices 0,4,2,6,1,5,3,7), the reordered input is [1, 4, 3, 2, 2, 3, 4, 1]. Three butterfly stages follow:

Stage 1 — size-2 butterflies, twiddle = 1

Pairs (0,1),(2,3),(4,5),(6,7): [1,4]→[5,−3] · [3,2]→[5,1] · [2,3]→[5,−1] · [4,1]→[5,3]

Result after Stage 1: [5, −3, 5, 1, 5, −1, 5, 3]

Stage 2 — size-4 butterflies, twiddles W₄⁰=1, W₄¹=−i

Group [0..3]: (5,5)×1→(10,0); (−3,1)×(−i)→(−3−i, −3+i). Group [4..7]: (5,5)→(10,0); (−1,3)×(−i)→(−1−3i,−1+3i)

Result: [10, −3−i, 0, −3+i, 10, −1−3i, 0, −1+3i]

Stage 3 — size-8 butterflies, twiddles W₈⁰..W₈³

W₈⁰=1, W₈¹≈0.707−0.707i, W₈²=−i, W₈³≈−0.707−0.707i

Final: X=[20, −5.828−2.414i, 0, −0.172−0.414i, 0, −0.172+0.414i, 0, −5.828+2.414i]

The DC component X[0]=20 = sum of all inputs. X[1] and X[7] are complex conjugates (the signal is real). X[2],X[4],X[6]=0 indicates no energy at those frequencies — which makes sense for this symmetric, slowly-varying signal.

From-Scratch FFT (NumPy)

import numpy as np def fft_cooley_tukey(x): """Radix-2 Cooley-Tukey FFT. n must be a power of 2.""" n = len(x) if n == 1: # Base case return x # Split into even and odd indices evens = fft_cooley_tukey(x[::2]) # x[0],x[2],x[4],... odds = fft_cooley_tukey(x[1::2]) # x[1],x[3],x[5],... # Twiddle factors W_n^k = exp(-2πi*k/n) k = np.arange(n // 2) W = np.exp(-2j * np.pi * k / n) # shape (n/2,) # Butterfly combine top = evens + W * odds # X[k] k=0..n/2-1 bot = evens - W * odds # X[k+n/2] k=0..n/2-1 return np.concatenate([top, bot]) # Verify x = np.array([1.,2.,3.,4.,4.,3.,2.,1.]) X = fft_cooley_tukey(x) print(np.allclose(X, np.fft.fft(x))) # True # Inverse DFT: conjugate, FFT, conjugate, divide by n def ifft(X): n = len(X) return np.conj(fft_cooley_tukey(np.conj(X))) / n

Production Usage — scipy.fft

from scipy.fft import fft, ifft, fftfreq, rfft import numpy as np x = np.array([1., 2., 3., 4., 4., 3., 2., 1.]) X = fft(x) # Full complex DFT x_back = ifft(X).real # Reconstruct (should equal x) # For REAL signals: rfft is 2× faster (only computes n/2+1 unique bins) Xr = rfft(x) # shape (5,) instead of (8,) freqs = fftfreq(len(x), d=1/8) # d = 1/sample_rate # Convolution via FFT (O(n log n) instead of O(n²)) from scipy.signal import fftconvolve conv = fftconvolve(x, x[::-1]) # cross-correlation
Common Gotchas
  • Zero-padding: Pad to next power-of-2 for speed: n_fft = 2**int(np.ceil(np.log2(len(x))))
  • rfft vs fft: Real signals have conjugate-symmetric DFTs — use rfft to avoid computing redundant half.
  • Frequency bin interpretation: Bin \(k\) corresponds to frequency \(k \cdot f_s / n\) Hz where \(f_s\) is the sample rate.
  • Spectral leakage: Apply a window (Hann, Hamming) before FFT when the signal is not periodic in the window.

When to Use FFT

  • Audio/speech processing: Spectrograms, pitch detection, compression (MP3 uses MDCT, a variant)
  • Image processing: Frequency-domain filtering, JPEG compression uses DCT (FFT variant)
  • Fast polynomial multiplication: Convolve two degree-n polynomials in O(n log n)
  • Solving PDEs: Spectral methods use FFT for spatial derivatives
  • Telecommunications: OFDM modulation in WiFi/4G/5G

Related Topics

  • Prerequisites: Complex numbers, Euler's formula, linear algebra (matrix-vector product)
  • Builds on this: Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT), Wavelet Transform, Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT)
  • Key papers: Cooley & Tukey (1965) "An Algorithm for the Machine Calculation of Complex Fourier Series" — the landmark paper that made FFT practical. Gauss had discovered it ~1805 but it was unpublished.
Interview Corner

Q: Why does FFT need n to be a power of 2? The Cooley-Tukey algorithm splits at each level into two equal halves. If n is not a power of 2, Bluestein's algorithm (chirp-z transform) or mixed-radix FFT handles arbitrary sizes — but performance degrades. In practice, zero-pad to the next power of 2.

Q: What is the relationship between convolution and FFT? Convolution theorem: pointwise multiplication in frequency domain = circular convolution in time domain. This reduces O(n²) convolution to O(n log n): compute FFT of both signals, multiply elementwise, inverse FFT.
