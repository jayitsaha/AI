Understanding Expert Parallelism
▼

What is it? A Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) layer replaces one big feed-forward network with many smaller "expert" networks plus a small gating (router) network that picks which experts should process each token. Expert parallelism is the systems technique that makes this affordable at scale: instead of replicating every expert on every device, you place different experts on different devices (or GPUs), so the total parameter count can grow far beyond what a single device could hold, while the compute per token stays roughly constant.

Analogy: Imagine a huge customer-support center split across floors, where each floor specializes in a different topic (billing, tech support, returns, ...). A receptionist (the router) reads each incoming ticket and forwards it to the one or two floors best suited to handle it. If a floor becomes overloaded, tickets start queueing or getting turned away — that is exactly the "capacity factor" and "token dropping" problem MoE systems must manage.

💡 If you remember one thing

Expert parallelism trades a hard problem (fitting huge models in memory) for a communication problem (getting the right tokens to the right devices). Every token that is routed to an expert living on another device must be physically shipped there and back via an all-to-all collective — and every expert has a finite capacity, so overflow means tokens get dropped.

How It Works Internally
▼

The Gating Network

For each token \(x_t\), a learned linear router produces logits over \(N\) experts, \(g(x_t) = x_t W_g\), which are turned into a probability distribution with softmax. The router then keeps only the top-\(k\) experts (commonly \(k{=}1\) for Switch Transformer or \(k{=}2\) for GShard-style routing):

$$y_t = \sum_{e \,\in\, \text{TopK}(g(x_t),\,k)} p_e(x_t) \cdot E_e(x_t)$$

Only the selected experts run on token \(x_t\) — this sparse activation is what keeps FLOPs per token roughly constant even as the total parameter count (across all experts) grows into the hundreds of billions.

Sharding Experts Across Devices

With \(N\) experts and \(D\) devices, each device typically hosts \(N/D\) experts (expert parallelism), often combined with data parallelism for the non-expert layers. Tokens live on their "home" device as part of a data-parallel batch shard; the experts they need may live elsewhere.

Dispatch → Compute → Combine

Every MoE forward pass has three communication-bound phases: (1) dispatch — an all-to-all exchange that ships each token to the device(s) hosting its selected expert(s); (2) expert compute — each device runs its local experts on the tokens it just received; (3) combine — a reverse all-to-all that ships expert outputs back to each token's home device, where they are weighted by the gate probability \(p_e(x_t)\) and summed.

Capacity Factor

Because all-to-all communication needs statically-shaped buffers, each expert is given a fixed capacity \(C = \lceil \text{cf} \times T \times k / N \rceil\), where \(T\) is the number of tokens in the batch and \(\text{cf}\) (capacity factor, typically 1.0–2.0) is a slack multiplier above the perfectly-balanced load \(Tk/N\). Tokens routed to an already-full expert beyond \(C\) are dropped for that expert (they skip it, sometimes falling back to a residual connection).

🔑 Key Property

Raising the capacity factor reduces dropped tokens but wastes compute/memory on padding for under-filled experts; lowering it saves resources but discards more tokens. This is a genuine systems trade-off, not just a hyperparameter — it directly trades accuracy for throughput.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Why Routers Collapse Without Regularization

If trained with only the task loss, the router tends to converge to a "rich get richer" regime: a few experts receive most tokens (good gradients, keep improving), while the rest are starved and never learn. This is the load-imbalance failure mode expert-parallel systems must prevent, both for quality (unused expert capacity) and for speed (overloaded experts become the straggler that the whole all-to-all must wait for).

The Auxiliary Load-Balancing Loss

Switch Transformer / GShard add an auxiliary loss that pushes the router towards a uniform distribution over experts. Define, over a batch of \(T\) tokens and \(N\) experts:

$$f_e = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbb{1}\!\left[\arg\max_{e'} g_{e'}(x_t) = e\right] \qquad P_e = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} p_e(x_t)$$

\(f_e\) is the *fraction of tokens* whose top choice is expert \(e\) (a hard, non-differentiable count), while \(P_e\) is the *average soft probability* mass expert \(e\) receives (differentiable). The auxiliary loss multiplies them together:

$$L_{aux} = \alpha \cdot N \cdot \sum_{e=1}^{N} f_e \cdot P_e$$

Multiplying a hard count by a soft probability lets gradients flow (through \(P_e\)) while the loss magnitude still reflects the true hard-routing imbalance (through \(f_e\)). When routing is perfectly uniform, \(f_e = P_e = 1/N\) for every expert, giving \(L_{aux} = N \cdot N \cdot (1/N)^2 = 1\) — the theoretical minimum. Any imbalance pushes \(L_{aux}\) above 1.

Communication Cost

The all-to-all dispatch/combine volume scales with the number of tokens that cross device boundaries, not just the total token count. If tokens were routed uniformly at random, roughly \(\frac{D-1}{D}\) of all routed tokens would need to leave their home device — this is why expert-parallel MoE training is often communication-bound rather than compute-bound, and why interconnect bandwidth (NVLink/InfiniBand) is as important as FLOPs for these systems.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

Setting the capacity factor to exactly 1.0 ("no slack") looks efficient on paper but in practice drops a large fraction of tokens the moment routing is even slightly imbalanced — which it always is early in training. Most production systems use \(\text{cf} \in [1.25, 2.0]\) and still rely on the auxiliary loss to keep the router balanced.

Key Takeaways
▼

When expert parallelism helps:

  • Model has far more parameters than fit on one device, but sparse (top-k) activation keeps per-token FLOPs manageable
  • You have high-bandwidth interconnect (NVLink/NVSwitch, InfiniBand) to absorb the all-to-all dispatch/combine traffic
  • You can tolerate a small, tunable fraction of dropped tokens in exchange for much larger effective capacity

Watch out for:

  • Capacity factor too low → high drop rate → quality loss, especially early in training when routing is unbalanced
  • Capacity factor too high → wasted compute/memory on padding, and a false sense of "no drops" that hides a badly balanced router
  • No auxiliary load-balancing loss → router collapse onto a handful of experts, starving the rest
  • All-to-all is a global synchronization point — one slow/overloaded device stalls every other device's training step
💡 Mental Model

Think of expert parallelism as turning a memory-bound scaling problem into a load-balancing and networking problem. The router's job is not just "pick the best expert" — it is "pick the best expert subject to keeping every device's queue roughly equally full."

# Simplified expert-parallel MoE forward pass (pseudo-PyTorch)
logits = tokens @ W_gate # [T, N] router logits
probs = softmax(logits, dim=-1) # [T, N] routing probabilities
topk_idx, topk_gate = topk(probs, k=2) # [T, 2] chosen experts + weights
capacity = ceil(cap_factor * T * k / N) # per-expert buffer size
dispatch_mask, drop_mask = build_masks(topk_idx, capacity)
# --- DISPATCH: all-to-all send tokens to their expert's device ---
expert_inputs = all_to_all(tokens, dispatch_mask, device_of_expert)
# --- EXPERT COMPUTE: local experts run only on received tokens ---
expert_outputs = [E[e](expert_inputs[e]) for e in local_experts]
# --- COMBINE: reverse all-to-all back to home devices ---
gathered = all_to_all(expert_outputs, dispatch_mask, device_of_token)
y = sum(topk_gate[:, i] * gathered[:, i] for i in range(k))
# --- AUXILIARY LOAD-BALANCING LOSS ---
f = mean(one_hot(argmax(probs, dim=-1), N), dim=0) # hard fraction per expert
P = mean(probs, dim=0) # soft mass per expert
L_aux = alpha * N * sum(f * P)
loss = task_loss + L_aux
