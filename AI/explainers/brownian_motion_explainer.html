Understanding Brownian Motion
▼

What is it? Imagine a pollen grain floating on still water, jostled by invisible water molecules from every direction. Its erratic, unpredictable path is Brownian motion — named after botanist Robert Brown who observed it in 1827. Mathematically, the Wiener process (named after Norbert Wiener) is the precise continuous-time model of this randomness.

Analogy — The Drunk Walk: Picture someone flipping a coin every second: heads → step right, tails → step left. After many tiny steps taken very rapidly, their position traces a continuous, jagged curve. As steps get smaller and faster while holding total time fixed, this curve converges to Brownian motion. Key insight: you cannot predict where they'll be, but you know exactly how spread out all possible positions are — the spread grows as the square root of time.

Why it matters for AI/ML: Brownian motion is the engine powering diffusion generative models (DALL-E, Stable Diffusion, Sora). These models add Gaussian noise in a way that precisely mimics the BM forward process, then learn to reverse it. Score matching — the mathematical heart of modern generative AI — estimates the gradient of the BM transition density. Understanding Brownian motion reveals why diffusion models work at a fundamental level.

💡 If you remember one thing

A Wiener process \(W(t)\) starts at zero, has independent Gaussian increments with \(W(t)-W(s) \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\, t-s)\), and is continuous but differentiable nowhere. Its variance grows linearly: \(\operatorname{Var}(W(t)) = t\). This single property — variance ∝ time — drives everything from the heat equation to modern diffusion models.

Formal Definition & Properties
▼

The Four Axioms (Wiener Process)

A stochastic process \(\{W(t)\}_{t \geq 0}\) is a standard Wiener process if and only if:

🔑 Wiener Process Axioms

W1. Initialization: \(W(0) = 0\) almost surely.

W2. Independent increments: For \(0 \leq s < t \leq u < v\), \(W(t)-W(s)\) is independent of \(W(v)-W(u)\). Non-overlapping intervals give statistically independent changes.

W3. Gaussian increments: For \(0 \leq s < t\):

$$W(t) - W(s) \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}(0,\; t - s)$$

W4. Continuous paths: The sample paths \(t \mapsto W(t)\) are continuous almost surely.

Key Consequence: Marginal & Covariance

From axioms W1+W3: \(W(t) = W(t)-W(0) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t)\). So the marginal at time \(t\) has mean 0 and variance \(t\). The 1-sigma band is \(\pm\sqrt{t}\); at \(t=0.25\): \(\pm 0.5\); at \(t=1.0\): \(\pm 1.0\).

The covariance structure: for \(s \leq t\),

$$\operatorname{Cov}(W(s), W(t)) = \mathbb{E}[W(s)W(t)] = \min(s, t) = s$$

This makes BM a Gaussian process with kernel \(k(s,t) = \min(s,t)\).

Nowhere Differentiability — The Central Paradox

BM is continuous everywhere but differentiable nowhere (proved by Paley, Wiener, and Zygmund, 1933). The reason: the quadratic variation over \([0, T]\) equals \(T\), not zero. For any differentiable function \(f\), squared increments \((f(t_{k+1})-f(t_k))^2 = O(\Delta t^2)\) sum to zero as \(\Delta t \to 0\). For BM, \((W(t_{k+1})-W(t_k))^2 \approx \Delta t\), so the sum stays at \(T\). In differential notation: \((dW)^2 = dt\) — the cornerstone of Itô calculus.

⚠️ Common Confusion

\(W(t) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t)\) is the marginal at a single time. BM is a joint process over all \(t\): its paths are correlated as \(\operatorname{Cov}(W(s),W(t)) = \min(s,t)\). Knowing \(W(0.5)\) updates your belief about \(W(0.3)\) even though increments are independent — these are different statements.

Mathematical Derivation & Key Results
▼

Construction: Donsker's Invariance Principle (Functional CLT)

Define a symmetric random walk \(S_n = \sum_{k=1}^n \xi_k\) where \(\xi_k \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} \{-1, +1\}\) with equal probability. Rescale in space and time by defining the interpolated process:

$$W^{(n)}(t) \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} S_{\lfloor nt \rfloor}, \quad t \in [0,1]$$

The space scaling \(1/\sqrt{n}\) is forced by variance preservation: after \(\lfloor nt \rfloor\) steps, \(\operatorname{Var}(S_{\lfloor nt \rfloor}) = \lfloor nt \rfloor \approx nt\), so \(\operatorname{Var}(W^{(n)}(t)) = nt/n = t\), matching the target \(\operatorname{Var}(W(t)) = t\). By Donsker's theorem:

$$W^{(n)} \;\xrightarrow{d}\; W \quad \text{weakly in } C[0,1] \text{ as } n \to \infty$$

Transition Density and the Heat Equation

The conditional density of \(W(t)\) given \(W(s) = x\) is:

$$p(w, t \;\mid\; x, s) \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(t-s)}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(w-x)^2}{2(t-s)}\right)$$

This density satisfies the heat equation (Fokker–Planck for pure diffusion) forward in time:

$$\frac{\partial p}{\partial t} \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\partial^2 p}{\partial w^2}, \qquad p(w, s \mid x, s) = \delta(w - x)$$

This is why diffusion models are named "diffusion" — the forward noising process literally diffuses the data distribution like heat spreading in a medium.

Covariance Derivation (Proof)

For \(s \leq t\), write \(W(t) = [W(t)-W(s)] + W(s)\). By independence of increments \(\mathbb{E}[W(s)\cdot(W(t)-W(s))] = \mathbb{E}[W(s)]\cdot\mathbb{E}[W(t)-W(s)] = 0\). Therefore:

$$\mathbb{E}[W(s)W(t)] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[W(s)\bigl(W(t)-W(s)\bigr)\right] + \mathbb{E}\!\left[W(s)^2\right] = 0 + s = s$$

Symmetrically for \(s > t\) the result is \(t\), giving the kernel \(\min(s,t)\).

📐 Key Formulas at a Glance

Marginal: \(W(t) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t)\)

Covariance kernel: \(\operatorname{Cov}(W(s),W(t)) = \min(s,t)\)

Quadratic variation: \([W]_T = T\)  |  Itô rule: \((dW_t)^2 = dt\)

Reflection principle: \(\mathbb{P}(\sup_{0\leq s\leq t} W(s) \geq a) = 2\,\mathbb{P}(W(t)\geq a)\) for \(a>0\)

Connections: SDEs, Diffusion Models & Finance
▼

Stochastic Differential Equations (SDEs)

BM is the noise source in all SDEs. A general Itô SDE is:

$$dX_t = \mu(X_t, t)\,dt + \sigma(X_t, t)\,dW_t$$

where \(dW_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, dt)\). Pure BM corresponds to \(\mu=0\), \(\sigma=1\). Itô's lemma is the chain rule for SDE functions \(f(X_t, t)\):

$$df = \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\,dt + \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,dX_t + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}\,dt$$

The extra \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\partial^2 f/\partial x^2\) term arises because \((dW)^2 = dt\) is non-negligible — this is Itô's correction and has no classical calculus analogue.

Diffusion Generative Models (DDPM / Score SDE)

Modern image generators define a forward process that gradually adds Gaussian noise:

$$q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_0,\;(1-\bar{\alpha}_t)\,I\right)$$

As \(t \to T\), \(\bar{\alpha}_t \to 0\) so \(x_T \approx \mathcal{N}(0, I)\). This is precisely a variance-preserving version of BM. The score function — the training target — is:

$$\nabla_{x_t} \log q(x_t \mid x_0) = -\frac{x_t - \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t}\,x_0}{1-\bar{\alpha}_t} = -\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_t}}$$

where \(\epsilon\) is the Gaussian noise added. Training a diffusion model = learning to predict \(\epsilon\) = score matching for a BM-based diffusion. The reverse SDE (Anderson, 1982):

$$dx_t = \bigl[\mu(x_t,t) - \sigma^2 \nabla_x \log p_t(x_t)\bigr]\,dt + \sigma\,d\bar{W}_t$$

where \(\bar{W}_t\) is a reverse-time BM, runs the forward diffusion backwards.

Black–Scholes (Mathematical Finance)

Stock prices follow geometric BM (ensuring non-negative prices):

$$dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t \;\implies\; S_t = S_0 \exp\!\left[\left(\mu - \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\right)t + \sigma W_t\right]$$

The log-return \(\log(S_t/S_0) \sim \mathcal{N}((\mu-\sigma^2/2)t,\, \sigma^2 t)\) has variance \(\sigma^2 t\) — variance ∝ time again.

Key Takeaways & Interview Preparation
▼

When to use Brownian motion:

  • Any continuous-time process with stationary, independent, Gaussian increments
  • Forward noising in diffusion models (DDPM, DDIM, Score SDE)
  • Log-return models in quantitative finance (Black–Scholes)
  • Particle diffusion in physics/biology
  • Prior over continuous functions in Gaussian process regression (Wiener kernel)

When to use alternatives:

  • Fractional BM — correlated increments (long memory: finance, hydrology)
  • Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process — mean-reverting noise (interest rates, OU diffusion for AI)
  • Poisson / Lévy processes — discrete jumps, heavy tails, rare events
  • Geometric BM — when positivity is required (prices, volatility)
💡 Interview Must-Knows

Must know: The 4 axioms; why variance = \(t\) (not \(t^2\)); covariance \(\min(s,t)\); nowhere differentiable and why. Numerical trace: \(W(0.7)-W(0.3)\sim\mathcal{N}(0, 0.4)\), std \(\approx 0.632\).

Often asked in diffusion model interviews: Why does noise prediction = score matching? What is the reverse SDE? Why does the forward process converge to Gaussian? How does Itô's lemma give the extra \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\partial^2_x\) term?

Prerequisites: Probability theory, Gaussian distributions, Central Limit Theorem, basic measure theory. What to study next: Itô's lemma and stochastic calculus → SDEs and Fokker-Planck → Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process → DDPM (Ho et al. 2020) → Score-based generative models (Song et al. 2021).
