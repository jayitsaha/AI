Understanding Hierarchical Bayesian Models
▼

What is it? Imagine you're a teacher with 8 classrooms. You want to estimate how smart each classroom is, but some classrooms have only 3 students while others have 30. If you only use each classroom's own test scores, the small classrooms give wild, unreliable estimates. If you ignore the classroom structure and lump everyone together, you miss real differences. A hierarchical (multilevel) Bayesian model finds the sweet spot: it uses a shared "prior" that pulls all classroom estimates toward a grand average — but only as much as the data within each classroom is noisy.

Analogy: Think of it as a recipe for blending "local" knowledge (this classroom's actual scores) with "global" knowledge (what classrooms generally look like). A classroom with 30 data points is trusted almost entirely; a classroom with 3 points gets gently nudged toward the overall average. This is called partial pooling or shrinkage.

💡 If you remember one thing

Hierarchical models sit between two extremes: no pooling (treat each group independently) and complete pooling (ignore group structure). Partial pooling shrinks each group's estimate toward the grand mean — more shrinkage for small/noisy groups, less for large/precise ones. The shrinkage factor is \(\mathcal{B}_j = \frac{\sigma^2/n_j}{\sigma^2/n_j + \tau^2}\), where \(\sigma^2\) is within-group noise and \(\tau^2\) is between-group variance.

2
How Hierarchical Models Work Internally
▼

The Three-Level Generative Story

A hierarchical model defines a joint distribution with three levels. For \(J\) groups and \(n_j\) observations in group \(j\):

$$\text{Level 3 (Hyperprior):} \quad \mu \sim p(\mu), \quad \tau \sim p(\tau)$$
$$\text{Level 2 (Group prior):} \quad \theta_j \mid \mu, \tau \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}(\mu, \tau^2) \quad j=1,\ldots,J$$
$$\text{Level 1 (Likelihood):} \quad y_{ij} \mid \theta_j, \sigma \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}(\theta_j, \sigma^2) \quad i=1,\ldots,n_j$$

The key insight: the \(\theta_j\) are neither fixed (no-pooling) nor shared (complete-pooling). They are drawn from a common distribution parameterized by \((\mu, \tau)\). This creates a statistical link between groups — groups inform each other through the hyperparameters.

The Posterior — What Inference Gives You

After observing data \(\{y_{ij}\}\), Bayesian inference yields the joint posterior \(p(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_J, \mu, \tau \mid \mathbf{y})\). If we treat \(\sigma\) as known and put a flat hyperprior on \(\mu\) and a uniform prior on \(\tau\), the conditional posterior of each group mean is:

$$\theta_j \mid \mathbf{y}, \mu, \tau^2, \sigma^2 \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\!\left(\hat{\mu}_j,\; V_j\right)$$

where the posterior mean and variance are:

$$\hat{\mu}_j = \frac{\frac{\bar{y}_j}{\sigma^2/n_j} + \frac{\mu}{\tau^2}}{\frac{1}{\sigma^2/n_j} + \frac{1}{\tau^2}} = \mu + (1-\mathcal{B}_j)(\bar{y}_j - \mu), \qquad \mathcal{B}_j = \frac{\sigma^2/n_j}{\sigma^2/n_j + \tau^2}$$
$$V_j = \frac{1}{\frac{n_j}{\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{\tau^2}}$$

Here \(\mathcal{B}_j \in [0,1]\) is the shrinkage factor. When \(n_j\) is small or \(\sigma^2\) is large, \(\mathcal{B}_j \to 1\) (more shrinkage to grand mean). When \(n_j\) is large or \(\tau^2\) is large (groups truly differ), \(\mathcal{B}_j \to 0\) (stay near the group's own data).

🔑 Key Mechanism: Adaptive Shrinkage

The model automatically determines how much to pool. Groups with few observations and high within-group noise are shrunk aggressively toward the grand mean \(\hat{\mu}\). Groups with many observations resist shrinkage and stay near their own \(\bar{y}_j\). No tuning knob required — the data estimates \(\tau\) and does the balancing.

Why Not Just Use a Fixed Prior?

A non-hierarchical Bayesian model would use a fixed prior \(\theta_j \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \tau_0^2)\) with pre-chosen \(\mu_0, \tau_0\). The hierarchical model goes further: it learns \(\mu\) and \(\tau\) from the data itself (either by full MCMC sampling of the joint posterior, or via empirical Bayes where \(\mu, \tau\) are point-estimated from the marginal likelihood). This is the defining feature — the hyperprior is informed by all groups jointly.

3
Why It Works — The Math of Partial Pooling
▼

Deriving the Shrinkage Estimator

Assume \(\sigma^2\) known. The precision-weighted posterior mean for \(\mu\) (the grand mean) given all \(\theta_j\) is:

$$\hat{\mu} = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^J \bar{y}_j / (\sigma^2/n_j + \tau^2)}{\sum_{j=1}^J 1/(\sigma^2/n_j + \tau^2)}$$

Each group \(j\) then has its posterior mean derived by combining its own data with \(\hat{\mu}\) as a prior. Using precision-weighted combination (precision = 1/variance):

$$\hat{\theta}_j = \frac{\text{precision from data} \cdot \bar{y}_j + \text{precision from prior} \cdot \mu}{\text{total precision}} = \frac{\frac{n_j}{\sigma^2}\bar{y}_j + \frac{1}{\tau^2}\mu}{\frac{n_j}{\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{\tau^2}}$$

Rearranging using \(\mathcal{B}_j = \frac{\sigma^2/n_j}{\sigma^2/n_j + \tau^2}\):

$$\hat{\theta}_j = (1-\mathcal{B}_j)\bar{y}_j + \mathcal{B}_j \mu \;=\; \mu + (1-\mathcal{B}_j)(\bar{y}_j - \mu)$$

This shows the estimate is a convex combination of the group sample mean \(\bar{y}_j\) and the grand mean \(\mu\), weighted by data quality.

The James-Stein Connection

The shrinkage estimator is closely related to the James-Stein estimator (Stein 1956, James & Stein 1961). Stein's shocking result: for \(J \geq 3\) groups, the no-pooling estimator \(\hat{\theta}_j = \bar{y}_j\) is inadmissible under squared error loss — there always exists a shrinkage estimator with lower total MSE. Hierarchical models provide the Bayesian justification: the shrinkage is optimal because it correctly models the generative process.

📐 Posterior Variance

Each group's posterior variance \(V_j = \left(\frac{n_j}{\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{\tau^2}\right)^{-1}\) is always smaller than either the likelihood variance \(\sigma^2/n_j\) alone or the prior variance \(\tau^2\) alone. For group 1 with \(n_1=3\), \(\sigma=15\), \(\tau=10\): \(V_1 = (3/225 + 1/100)^{-1} = (0.0133 + 0.01)^{-1} = 42.9\). SD = 6.55 — much tighter than the no-pooling SE of 8.66.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

The shrinkage estimator is biased for each individual \(\theta_j\). This is intentional and acceptable: the bias-variance tradeoff means total MSE is lower than the unbiased no-pooling estimator. The bias vanishes as \(n_j \to \infty\) (\(\mathcal{B}_j \to 0\)). Do not confuse "biased" with "wrong" — hierarchical estimators are optimal in the expected-total-loss sense.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use hierarchical models:

  • Grouped/clustered data (students in schools, patients in hospitals, items in stores)
  • Some groups have very few observations — borrowing strength reduces estimation error
  • You care about both group-specific estimates AND the overall population distribution
  • Small-area estimation, meta-analysis, A/B testing across many variants

When NOT to use:

  • All groups have large \(n_j\) — no-pooling is already efficient
  • Groups are from completely different populations (unrelated units shouldn't share a prior)
  • You need exact frequentist guarantees — shrinkage introduces bias
💡 The Pooling Spectrum

No pooling (\(\tau \to \infty\), all \(\mathcal{B}_j = 0\)): independent estimates, max variance, zero bias. Complete pooling (\(\tau = 0\), all \(\mathcal{B}_j = 1\)): one shared estimate, min variance, max bias. Partial pooling (\(0 < \tau < \infty\)): interpolates — the data-driven optimum. The hierarchical model discovers where on this spectrum to sit.

Code Trace
▼
import numpy as np
import pymc as pm
# Data: 8 groups with varying sample sizes
n_j = np.array([3, 3, 5, 10, 10, 20, 20, 30])
y_bar = np.array([50.9, 53.3, 53.4, 63.0, 48.8, 41.2, 60.0, 56.1])
sigma = 15.0 # known within-group SD
# ── Empirical Bayes (analytic) ─────────────────────
se_j = sigma / np.sqrt(n_j) # group-level SE
tau = 10.0 # known/estimated between-group SD
B_j = (se_j**2) / (se_j**2 + tau**2) # shrinkage factors
# Precision-weighted grand mean
w_j = 1.0 / (se_j**2 + tau**2) # marginal precisions
mu_hat = np.sum(w_j * y_bar) / np.sum(w_j)
# Partial pooling estimates
theta_hat = mu_hat + (1 - B_j) * (y_bar - mu_hat)
# ── Full MCMC with PyMC ────────────────────────────
with pm.Model() as hier_model:
mu = pm.Normal("mu", mu=50, sigma=20) # hyperprior on grand mean
tau_rv = pm.HalfNormal("tau", sigma=15) # hyperprior on group SD
theta = pm.Normal("theta", mu=mu, sigma=tau_rv, shape=8) # group means
# Likelihood: observed group means with known SE
obs = pm.Normal("obs", mu=theta, sigma=se_j, observed=y_bar)
trace = pm.sample(2000, tune=1000, return_inferencedata=True)
print(f"Partial pooling (analytic): {np.round(theta_hat, 1)}")
print(f"Grand mean estimate: {mu_hat:.2f}")
print(f"Shrinkage factors B_j: {np.round(B_j, 3)}")
