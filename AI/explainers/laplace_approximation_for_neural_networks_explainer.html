Understanding Laplace Approximation
▼

What is it? Laplace Approximation (LA) is a method to approximate a complex probability distribution with a simpler Gaussian (normal) distribution. In the context of neural networks, we use it to approximate the posterior distribution over network weights, \(p(\theta | \mathcal{D})\), where \(\theta\) are the weights and \(\mathcal{D}\) is the training data. This approximation is centered at the Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) estimate of the weights, \(\theta_{MAP}\), and its covariance is determined by the local curvature of the negative log-posterior at that point.

Analogy: Imagine you're trying to map a complex, rugged mountain range, but you only have the resources to map one single peak. You find the highest peak (this is your MAP estimate, \(\theta_{MAP}\)). Instead of mapping the entire range, you approximate it by a simple, perfectly bell-shaped hill centered exactly on that peak. The width and orientation of this bell-shaped hill reflect how steep and elongated the original peak was. This bell-shaped hill is your Gaussian approximation, and it's much easier to work with than the real mountain range.

💡 If you remember one thing

Laplace Approximation provides a computationally tractable way to estimate uncertainty in neural network predictions. It replaces the true (often intractable) posterior \(p(\theta | \mathcal{D})\) with a Gaussian \(\mathcal{N}(\theta_{MAP}, H^{-1})\), where \(H\) is the Hessian matrix of the negative log-posterior evaluated at the MAP estimate.

How It Works Internally
▼

The Three Steps of Laplace Approximation

Applying Laplace Approximation to a neural network involves these core steps:

  1. Train the Neural Network (Find \(\theta_{MAP}\)): First, train a standard neural network using maximum likelihood or maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation. This typically means minimizing a loss function (e.g., negative log-likelihood) augmented with a regularization term (e.g., L2 regularization, which corresponds to a Gaussian prior). The set of weights \(\theta\) that minimizes this objective is our \(\theta_{MAP}\).
  2. Compute the Hessian Matrix: Once \(\theta_{MAP}\) is found, compute the Hessian matrix of the negative log-posterior (which is the objective function you minimized) with respect to the weights \(\theta\), evaluated at \(\theta_{MAP}\). The Hessian, \(H = \nabla^2 (-\log p(\theta | \mathcal{D}))\), captures the local curvature of the posterior distribution around its mode. A sharp curvature implies high certainty, while a flat curvature implies high uncertainty.
  3. Form the Gaussian Approximation & Predict: The Laplace approximation to the posterior is then a Gaussian distribution: \(p(\theta | \mathcal{D}) \approx \mathcal{N}(\theta_{MAP}, H^{-1})\). To make predictions for a new input \(x_*\), we need to integrate over this approximated posterior: \(p(y_* | x_*, \mathcal{D}) = \int p(y_* | x_*, \theta) p(\theta | \mathcal{D}) d\theta\). This integral is often approximated by drawing multiple samples of \(\theta\) from \(\mathcal{N}(\theta_{MAP}, H^{-1})\) and averaging their predictions.

Design Decision: Why use the Hessian?

The Hessian matrix is the matrix of second-order partial derivatives. It naturally arises from a second-order Taylor expansion of the log-posterior around its mode. For a Gaussian distribution, the inverse of its covariance matrix (also known as the precision matrix) is precisely the negative of the second derivative of its log-density. By matching the first and second derivatives of the log-posterior at its mode, we get the best possible Gaussian fit locally.

🔑 Key Property

The Laplace approximation is a local approximation. It perfectly captures the curvature at a single mode of the posterior. This makes it computationally efficient compared to Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods, but also means it can fail to represent the true posterior if it is highly multimodal or has complex, non-Gaussian shapes far from the mode.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Derivation from Taylor Expansion

We begin with Bayes' theorem for the posterior distribution over weights \(\theta\):

$$p(\theta | \mathcal{D}) = \frac{p(\mathcal{D} | \theta) p(\theta)}{p(\mathcal{D})}$$

Taking the natural logarithm, we get the log-posterior:

$$\log p(\theta | \mathcal{D}) = \log p(\mathcal{D} | \theta) + \log p(\theta) - \log p(\mathcal{D})$$

Let's define the unnormalized negative log-posterior (the objective function we minimize to find \(\theta_{MAP}\)) as \(f(\theta) = - \log p(\theta | \mathcal{D})\). We can then perform a second-order Taylor expansion of \(f(\theta)\) around its minimum, \(\theta_{MAP}\):

$$f(\theta) \approx f(\theta_{MAP}) + \nabla f(\theta_{MAP})^\top (\theta - \theta_{MAP}) + \frac{1}{2}(\theta - \theta_{MAP})^\top H (\theta - \theta_{MAP})$$

Here, \(H = \nabla^2 f(\theta_{MAP})\) is the Hessian matrix of \(f(\theta)\) evaluated at \(\theta_{MAP}\). Since \(\theta_{MAP}\) is a minimum, the gradient \(\nabla f(\theta_{MAP})\) is zero. Thus, the expansion simplifies to:

$$f(\theta) \approx f(\theta_{MAP}) + \frac{1}{2}(\theta - \theta_{MAP})^\top H (\theta - \theta_{MAP})$$

Now, substitute this back into the log-posterior expression (ignoring the constant term \(f(\theta_{MAP})\) which will be absorbed into the normalization constant):

This is precisely the functional form of a multivariate Gaussian distribution \(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \Sigma)\) with mean \(\mu = \theta_{MAP}\) and covariance matrix \(\Sigma = H^{-1}\). Therefore, the Laplace approximation approximates the posterior as \(\mathcal{N}(\theta_{MAP}, H^{-1})\).

The Covariance of the Approximation

The covariance matrix of the Laplace approximation is \(\Sigma_{LA} = H^{-1}\). This means that the uncertainty in the parameters is directly linked to the inverse of the curvature of the negative log-posterior. A large curvature (large Hessian eigenvalues) means low variance (high certainty), and a small curvature (small Hessian eigenvalues) means high variance (low certainty).

⚠️ Common Pitfall

The biggest limitation of Laplace approximation is its reliance on a single mode. If the true posterior is highly multimodal (which is common in deep neural networks due to symmetries and complex loss landscapes), LA will only approximate one of these modes, completely missing the uncertainty and probability mass associated with other modes. This can lead to overconfident predictions.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use Laplace Approximation:

  • You need computationally efficient uncertainty estimates for neural network predictions.
  • The posterior distribution is believed to be approximately unimodal and Gaussian-like around the MAP estimate.
  • For small to medium-sized networks where computing and inverting the Hessian is feasible (for very large networks, approximations to the Hessian might be needed, like KFAC or diagonal approximations).
  • Applications requiring uncertainty quantification, such as active learning, out-of-distribution detection, or safety-critical systems.

When NOT to use or use with caution:

  • The true posterior is highly multimodal (e.g., in very deep or overparameterized networks, where many equivalent minima can exist). LA will only capture one mode and provide overconfident uncertainty estimates.
  • For extremely large networks where computing the full Hessian is prohibitively expensive (complexity is \(O(D^2)\) for \(D\) parameters).
  • When high-fidelity, global uncertainty quantification is paramount and the computational budget allows for more expensive methods like MCMC or variational inference.
  • If the loss landscape around \(\theta_{MAP}\) is highly non-quadratic (e.g., very sharp peaks or flat plateaus), the Gaussian approximation may be poor.
💡 Big Picture

Laplace Approximation sits as a valuable middle ground between point estimation (like standard MAP training) and full Bayesian inference (like MCMC). It provides a more complete picture of uncertainty than a single point estimate, at a fraction of the computational cost of sampling-based methods, making Bayesian neural networks more accessible in practice.

Code Trace (PyTorch Example)
▼
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
from torch.distributions import MultivariateNormal
from torch.autograd.functional import hessian
# 1. Define a simple neural network
class SimpleNN(nn.Module):
def __init__(self):
super().__init__()
self.linear = nn.Linear(2, 1) # 2 input features, 1 output
def forward(self, x):
return self.linear(x)
# 2. Simulate training data
X_train = torch.randn(100, 2)
y_train = (X_train[:, 0] * 2.0 - X_train[:, 1] * 0.5 + 1.0).unsqueeze(1) + torch.randn(100, 1) * 0.1
# 3. Train the model to find theta_MAP
model = SimpleNN()
optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=0.01)
loss_fn = nn.MSELoss()
# Flatten parameters into a single vector for convenience
def get_flat_params(model): return torch.cat([p.view(-1) for p in model.parameters()])
def set_flat_params(model, flat_params):
offset = 0
for p in model.parameters():
p.data = flat_params[offset : offset + p.numel()].view(p.size())
offset += p.numel()
for epoch in range(500):
optimizer.zero_grad()
outputs = model(X_train)
loss = loss_fn(outputs, y_train)
loss.backward()
optimizer.step()
theta_MAP = get_flat_params(model)
# 4. Define the negative log-posterior function for Hessian computation
def neg_log_posterior(flat_params):
set_flat_params(model, flat_params)
outputs = model(X_train)
nll = loss_fn(outputs, y_train) # Negative Log-Likelihood (MSE for Gaussian likelihood)
# Add log-prior term (e.g., L2 regularization for Gaussian prior)
prior_mean = torch.zeros_like(flat_params)
prior_cov_inv = torch.eye(flat_params.numel()) * 0.1 # Example precision
log_prior = -0.5 * (flat_params - prior_mean).T @ prior_cov_inv @ (flat_params - prior_mean)
return nll - log_prior # Minimize negative log-posterior
# 5. Compute the Hessian at theta_MAP
H = hessian(neg_log_posterior, theta_MAP)
Sigma_LA = torch.inverse(H) # Covariance matrix
# 6. Make predictions with uncertainty using LA
# Sample from the Gaussian approximation of the posterior
posterior_approx = MultivariateNormal(loc=theta_MAP, covariance_matrix=Sigma_LA)
num_samples = 100
theta_samples = posterior_approx.sample(sample_shape=(num_samples,))
# For a new input x_test, collect predictions from sampled models
x_test = torch.tensor([[0.5, 0.5]])
predictions = []
for i in range(num_samples):
set_flat_params(model, theta_samples[i])
predictions.append(model(x_test).item())
# Mean prediction and standard deviation (uncertainty)
mean_pred = torch.tensor(predictions).mean().item()
std_pred = torch.tensor(predictions).std().item()
print(f"Prediction for {x_test.tolist()}: Mean={mean_pred:.3f}, Std={std_pred:.3f}")
