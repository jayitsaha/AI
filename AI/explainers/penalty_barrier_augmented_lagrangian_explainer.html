Penalty, Barrier & Augmented Lagrangian Methods

Turning constrained optimization into unconstrained problems — three principled strategies

1
The Core Idea: Constraints as Cost
▼

The fundamental problem. We want to minimize \(f(x)\) subject to constraints \(g_i(x) \le 0\) and \(h_j(x) = 0\). Constrained solvers are hard to build and often slow. The key insight shared by all three methods here: encode the constraints directly into the objective, then call any unconstrained solver.

The analogy. Imagine hiking to the lowest point in a valley, but you're not allowed to cross a fence. Three strategies: (1) Exterior penalty: you're allowed to cross, but every step past the fence costs you energy proportional to how far you've gone — the fence becomes infinitely expensive as the penalty grows. (2) Interior barrier: the fence emits a repulsive field that grows to infinity as you approach — you can never cross, only asymptotically touch. (3) Augmented Lagrangian: you pay a fine per violation and a companion "Lagrange multiplier" estimates how much the constraint is worth — so the penalty stays finite but convergence is exact.

💡 If you remember one thing

All three methods transform a constrained problem \(\min_x f(x)\ \text{s.t.}\ g(x)\le 0\) into a sequence of unconstrained subproblems. They differ in how they enforce feasibility and whether they need a parameter to blow up to infinity.

2
Exterior Penalty Method
▼

Formulation. For an inequality-constrained problem \(\min_x f(x)\) s.t. \(g(x) \le 0\), define the penalized objective:

$$P_\mu(x) = f(x) + \mu \sum_i \max(0, g_i(x))^2$$

For equality constraints \(h_j(x) = 0\), add \(\mu \sum_j h_j(x)^2\). Solve \(\min_x P_\mu(x)\) for an increasing sequence \(\mu_1 < \mu_2 < \cdots \to \infty\), warm-starting each subproblem from the previous solution.

Why it works

When \(x\) violates a constraint, the penalty \(\mu\,\max(0,g_i(x))^2\) grows. As \(\mu \to \infty\), any violation incurs infinite cost, so the unconstrained minimizer is forced toward the feasible set. Formally, if \(x^*_\mu\) minimizes \(P_\mu\), then \(x^*_\mu \to x^*\) (the constrained optimum) as \(\mu \to \infty\).

🔑 Closed-form example: \(\min x^2\) s.t. \(x \ge 1\)

Write \(g(x) = 1 - x \le 0\). Then \(P_\mu(x) = x^2 + \mu\max(0,1-x)^2\). For \(x < 1\): \(\frac{d}{dx}P_\mu = 2x - 2\mu(1-x) = 0 \implies x^*_\mu = \dfrac{\mu}{1+\mu}\). As \(\mu \to \infty\), \(x^*_\mu \to 1\). The exact minimizer is never achieved for finite \(\mu\).

Ill-conditioning issue

Large \(\mu\) makes the Hessian of \(P_\mu\) stiff — its condition number grows as \(O(\mu)\), making gradient descent slow and Newton's method susceptible to numerical error. This is the central weakness: you need \(\mu \to \infty\) for exact feasibility, but that breaks the subproblem solvers.

⚠️ Practical limit

Pure exterior penalty is rarely used alone in production. It is primarily a conceptual stepping stone. Augmented Lagrangian (below) fixes this by maintaining a multiplier so \(\mu\) can stay modest.

3
Interior Barrier Method (Log-Barrier / IPM)
▼

Formulation. For inequality constraints \(g_i(x) \le 0\) (equivalently, \(-g_i(x) \ge 0\)), define the log-barrier objective:

$$B_t(x) = f(x) - t \sum_i \log(-g_i(x)), \quad t > 0$$

The barrier term \(-t\log(-g_i(x))\) is \(+\infty\) when \(g_i(x) = 0\) (boundary) and finite inside the feasible set. As \(x\) approaches a constraint boundary, the log-barrier blows up, repelling the iterates. We solve for decreasing \(t_1 > t_2 > \cdots \to 0\).

The central path

The minimizer \(x^*(t)\) of \(B_t(x)\) traces a smooth curve through the feasible interior called the central path. As \(t \to 0\), \(x^*(t) \to x^*\) (the constrained optimum). Interior-point methods (IPM) follow this path using Newton's method at each \(t\).

🔑 Closed-form example: \(\min x^2\) s.t. \(x \ge 1\)

Write \(g(x) = 1-x \le 0\), so \(-g(x) = x-1 \ge 0\). Then \(B_t(x) = x^2 - t\log(x-1)\). Setting \(\frac{dB_t}{dx} = 2x - \frac{t}{x-1} = 0\) gives \(2x(x-1) = t\), so \(x^*(t) = \frac{1+\sqrt{1+2t}}{2}\). Values: \(t=2 \Rightarrow x^*\approx1.618\), \(t=0.1 \Rightarrow x^*\approx1.048\), \(t=0.01 \Rightarrow x^*\approx1.005\).

KKT conditions via barrier

The optimality condition for \(B_t\) is:

$$\nabla f(x) + \sum_i \frac{t}{-g_i(x)}\nabla g_i(x) = 0$$

Defining \(\lambda_i = t / (-g_i(x)) > 0\), this is exactly the KKT stationarity condition \(\nabla f + \sum_i \lambda_i \nabla g_i = 0\) with complementary slackness \(\lambda_i(-g_i(x)) = t \to 0\). The barrier method therefore converges to a KKT point.

🎓 PhD note: self-concordance

The log-barrier is a self-concordant function (Nesterov & Nemirovskii 1994). This property guarantees that Newton's method applied to \(B_t\) converges in \(O(\log(1/\epsilon))\) steps, enabling the polynomial-time complexity of interior-point methods. The total complexity to reach \(\epsilon\)-optimality is \(O(\sqrt{n}\log(1/\epsilon))\) Newton steps for an \(n\)-dimensional problem.

4
Augmented Lagrangian (Method of Multipliers)
▼

Motivation. Pure penalty requires \(\mu \to \infty\). The augmented Lagrangian fixes this by tracking the dual variable (Lagrange multiplier) \(\lambda\), allowing the penalty parameter \(\rho\) to remain bounded while still achieving exact feasibility.

Formulation for equality constraints

For \(\min_x f(x)\) s.t. \(h(x) = 0\), the classical Lagrangian is \(\mathcal{L}(x,\lambda) = f(x) + \lambda h(x)\). The augmented Lagrangian adds a quadratic penalty:

$$\mathcal{L}_\rho(x,\lambda) = f(x) + \lambda^\top h(x) + \frac{\rho}{2}\|h(x)\|^2$$

The method of multipliers algorithm

Initialize \(\lambda^0, \rho > 0\). At each iteration \(k\):

$$x^{k+1} = \arg\min_x\ \mathcal{L}_\rho(x, \lambda^k)$$
$$\lambda^{k+1} = \lambda^k + \rho\, h(x^{k+1})$$

The multiplier update is a gradient ascent step on the dual — it corrects the running estimate of how expensive the constraint is.

🔑 Why no \(\mu \to \infty\)?

The Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda^k\) converges to the true dual variable \(\lambda^*\). Once \(\lambda^k \approx \lambda^*\), the term \(\lambda^k h(x) + \frac{\rho}{2}h(x)^2\) is minimized at \(h(x)=0\) without needing large \(\rho\). Exact feasibility is achieved in the limit even for fixed \(\rho\).

Convergence: closed-form trace for \(\min x^2\) s.t. \(x = 1\)

With \(h(x) = x - 1\) and \(\rho = 2\): minimizing \(\mathcal{L}_\rho\) over \(x\) gives \(x^k = (\rho - \lambda^k)/(2 + \rho)\). The multiplier updates: \(\lambda^{k+1} = \lambda^k + \rho(x^k - 1)\). Starting from \(\lambda^0 = 0\): \(x^1 = 0.5\), \(x^2 = 0.75\), \(x^3 = 0.875\), ..., \(x^k \to 1.0\) at rate \(1/2^k\).

Extension: ADMM

The Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) splits \(x\) into blocks and alternates the minimization — enabling distributed optimization across machines. ADMM is the backbone of many large-scale ML pipelines (LASSO, SVM, network flow).

🎓 PhD note: dual ascent interpretation

The multiplier update \(\lambda^{k+1} = \lambda^k + \rho\, h(x^{k+1})\) is gradient ascent on the dual function \(q(\lambda) = \min_x \mathcal{L}_\rho(x,\lambda)\). The augmented term \(\frac{\rho}{2}\|h\|^2\) makes \(q(\lambda)\) strongly concave (under mild conditions), giving linear convergence of the dual. Rockafellar (1973) showed that the augmented Lagrangian method is equivalent to applying the proximal point algorithm to the dual problem.

Interview Q&A & Key Takeaways
▼

Q1: Why does exterior penalty need \(\mu \to \infty\) for exact feasibility?

Because for any finite \(\mu\), the penalized minimizer \(x^*_\mu = \mu/(1+\mu)\) is strictly inside the infeasible region (\(x < 1\)). Only in the limit does it reach the constraint boundary. The multiplier update in augmented Lagrangian compensates so that finite \(\rho\) suffices.

Q2: What is the central path and why does it matter?

The central path is the trajectory \(\{x^*(t)\}_{t>0}\) of barrier minimizers as \(t \to 0\). It is a smooth curve that ends at the constrained optimum \(x^*\). Interior-point methods follow this path efficiently via Newton's method, achieving polynomial-time complexity for linear and convex programs.

Q3: How does the augmented Lagrangian avoid ill-conditioning?

The Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda^k\) absorbs the "correction" needed to enforce the constraint. Once \(\lambda^k\) is close to the true dual \(\lambda^*\), even a modest \(\rho\) yields a near-feasible minimizer. The Hessian of \(\mathcal{L}_\rho\) has condition number \(O(1)\) rather than \(O(\mu)\).

Q4: When would you choose each method?

  • Exterior penalty: Conceptual teaching tool; simple to implement; useful when infeasibility is temporarily acceptable.
  • Interior barrier / IPM: Gold standard for convex QPs and LPs; used in CVXPY, Gurobi, MOSEK. Requires strict interior feasibility (Slater's condition).
  • Augmented Lagrangian / ADMM: Large-scale, distributed, or non-smooth problems; statistical learning (LASSO, group LASSO); does not require strict feasibility of starting point.

Q5: What is ADMM and how does it relate?

ADMM (Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers) applies the augmented Lagrangian to a separable problem \(\min_{x,z} f(x)+g(z)\) s.t. \(Ax+Bz=c\), alternating minimization over \(x\) and \(z\) separately. Each subproblem is often easier (or closed-form), enabling distributed computation.

💡 The grand unified view

All three methods are instances of penalty-based Lagrangian duality. Exterior penalty approximates the indicator function of the feasible set from outside. Interior barrier approximates it from inside. Augmented Lagrangian combines a penalty with a dual estimate, achieving the best of both worlds: exact convergence without parameter blow-up.
