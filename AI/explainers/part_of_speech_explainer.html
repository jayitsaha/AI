1
Understanding POS Tagging
▼

What is it? Part-of-speech tagging assigns each word in a sentence its grammatical category — noun, verb, adjective, determiner, and so on. It sounds trivial until you notice that most common words are ambiguous: "can" is usually a modal verb ("I can swim") but sometimes a noun ("a can of soup") or even a main verb ("can the vegetables"). A tagger can't just look up a word in a dictionary — it has to use the surrounding words to resolve the ambiguity.

Analogy: Think of POS tagging like assigning job titles to people in a photo based on where they're standing and who's next to them, not just their appearance. The word "fish" standing after a modal verb ("can fish") is probably doing a verb's job (to fish); the same word after "the" ("the fish") is doing a noun's job. Context — the words immediately before it — reveals its role.

💡 If you remember one thing

POS tagging is sequence labeling: find the tag sequence \(t_{1:n}\) that maximizes \(P(t_{1:n} \mid w_{1:n})\). The classical solution factors this via Bayes' rule into a chain of transition probabilities \(P(t_i \mid t_{i-1})\) and emission probabilities \(P(w_i \mid t_i)\), and finds the optimal chain efficiently with the Viterbi dynamic program — never by enumerating all \(N^T\) possible tag sequences.

How POS Tagging Works Internally
▼

The Hidden Markov Model View

Treat the tag sequence as a hidden Markov chain: the true tags \(t_1, \dots, t_n\) are unobserved "states," and the words \(w_1, \dots, w_n\) are the observed emissions. Three tables define the model: an initial distribution \(\pi(t)\) over the first tag, a transition matrix \(A(t_{i-1}, t_i)\) giving how likely tag \(t_i\) is to follow tag \(t_{i-1}\), and an emission matrix \(B(t_i, w_i)\) giving how likely tag \(t_i\) is to produce word \(w_i\). Both matrices are estimated from a hand-tagged corpus (e.g. the Penn Treebank) by counting bigram tag transitions and word/tag co-occurrences, with smoothing for unseen pairs.

Why This Is Tractable: The Markov + Output Independence Assumptions

There are \(N^T\) possible tag sequences for \(T\) words and \(N\) tags — enumerating them is exponential. The HMM makes two simplifying assumptions that make the problem tractable: (1) Markov assumption — a tag depends only on the immediately preceding tag, not the whole history; (2) output independence — a word depends only on its own tag, not on neighboring words or tags. These assumptions are what let a dynamic program replace brute-force search: the best tag sequence has optimal substructure — the best path ending at tag \(j\) at position \(t\) must extend some best path ending at position \(t-1\), because splicing in a worse prefix could never make the overall path better.

Design Decision: Why Viterbi, Not Greedy Left-to-Right Tagging

A greedy tagger picks the single best tag for each word left-to-right and never reconsiders — fast, but it can lock in a locally-best choice that turns out to be globally wrong once later context arrives. Viterbi instead keeps, at every step, the best-scoring path for every possible current tag (not just the single best tag), and only commits to one final path once it has seen the whole sentence and can compare full-sentence scores. This costs \(O(T N^2)\) instead of \(O(TN)\) for greedy — a small price for guaranteeing the globally optimal tag sequence under the model.

Modern Approach: Neural Sequence Taggers

Contemporary taggers (BiLSTM-CRF, or a transformer encoder like BERT with a token-classification head) replace the hand-built \(A\) and \(B\) tables with learned, contextual representations: a bidirectional encoder reads the whole sentence and produces a context-aware vector for every token, then a linear layer scores each tag. A CRF (Conditional Random Field) layer on top plays the same role as the HMM's transition matrix — it still runs Viterbi decoding at inference time, but now the emission scores come from a neural network that has seen the entire sentence (not just word identity), and the transition scores are learned jointly with everything else rather than counted from raw frequencies.

🔑 Key Property

Every state-of-the-art tagger — HMM, MEMM, CRF, or BiLSTM/transformer-CRF — is still solving the same optimization problem: find the highest-scoring tag sequence under a model that scores local transitions and local emissions. What changes across decades is only how the emission and transition scores are computed, not the Viterbi decoding algorithm that finds the best sequence from them.

Why It Works — The Math
▼

From Bayes' Rule to a Product of Local Scores

We want the most probable tag sequence given the words:

$$\hat{t}_{1:n} = \underset{t_{1:n}}{\operatorname{argmax}} \; P(t_{1:n} \mid w_{1:n}) = \underset{t_{1:n}}{\operatorname{argmax}} \; P(w_{1:n} \mid t_{1:n}) \, P(t_{1:n})$$

(the denominator \(P(w_{1:n})\) is dropped — it doesn't depend on \(t_{1:n}\)). Applying the two HMM independence assumptions factors both terms into per-position products:

$$P(w_{1:n}\mid t_{1:n}) = \prod_{i=1}^n P(w_i \mid t_i) \qquad P(t_{1:n}) = \prod_{i=1}^n P(t_i \mid t_{i-1})$$

with \(t_0 = \text{START}\). Substituting back gives the objective actually optimized:

$$\hat{t}_{1:n} = \underset{t_{1:n}}{\operatorname{argmax}} \; \prod_{i=1}^n P(w_i \mid t_i)\, P(t_i \mid t_{i-1})$$

The Viterbi Recurrence

Define \(v_t(j)\) as the probability of the best tag path ending in tag \(j\) at position \(t\). Because of optimal substructure, this satisfies a clean recurrence:

$$v_1(j) = \pi(j)\,B(j, w_1) \qquad\qquad v_t(j) = \Big[\max_{i} \, v_{t-1}(i)\,A(i,j)\Big]\,B(j, w_t)$$

alongside a backpointer \(bp_t(j) = \operatorname{argmax}_i \, v_{t-1}(i) A(i,j)\) recording which predecessor achieved the max. At the end, the best final score is \(P^\star = \max_j v_T(j)\), and the full path is recovered by walking the backpointers from \(\operatorname{argmax}_j v_T(j)\) back to \(t{=}1\).

📐 Complexity

Filling the \(T \times N\) table costs \(O(N)\) work per cell (the max over \(N\) predecessors), so total time is \(O(T N^2)\) — linear in sentence length, quadratic in tag-set size — versus \(O(N^T)\) for brute-force enumeration of every tag sequence.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

Multiplying many probabilities \((<1)\) together underflows floating point for long sentences, so production implementations run Viterbi in log space — summing \(\log P(w_i\mid t_i) + \log P(t_i \mid t_{i-1})\) instead of multiplying. Also, the first-order Markov assumption only looks one tag back; genuine long-range agreement (e.g. subject-verb agreement across a long clause) is invisible to a bigram HMM, which is exactly the gap neural contextual taggers close.

Key Takeaways
▼

Comparison of tagging approaches:

MethodContext usedDecodingTypical WSJ accuracy
Rule-based (hand rules)Local hand patternsDeterministic~90%
HMM (bigram)Previous tag onlyViterbi~95-96%
MEMM / CRF (features)Rich hand featuresViterbi~96-97%
BiLSTM-CRFFull sentence (learned)Viterbi over neural scores~97.5%
Transformer token classifierFull sentence + subwordsGreedy/CRF over contextual scores~97-98%

When a simple HMM tagger is still a good choice:

  • Low-resource settings with little labeled data or compute
  • Interpretability matters — transition/emission tables are human-readable
  • Extremely low-latency requirements (no neural forward pass)

When to reach for a neural tagger instead:

  • Rare/unseen words — subword and contextual embeddings generalize where a frequency table cannot
  • Long-range or non-local disambiguation cues
  • Tagging is a component of a larger pretrained pipeline (e.g. dependency parsing, NER)
💡 Hierarchy

Every method here is answering the same question — score each possible tag sequence and pick the best one — with Viterbi decoding as the common thread. Rule-based → HMM → CRF → neural-CRF is a steady progression in how the local scores are produced, not in how the optimal sequence is found.

Code Trace — Viterbi from Scratch
▼
import numpy as np
tags = ['PRON', 'MODAL', 'NOUN', 'VERB']
words = ['I', 'can', 'fish']
N, T = len(tags), len(words)
# pi[j]: P(first tag = j); A[i][j]: P(tag j | tag i); B[j][w]: P(w | tag j)
pi = np.array([0.50, 0.10, 0.30, 0.10])
A = np.array([[.01,.40,.20,.39], [.01,.05,.30,.64],
[.05,.10,.20,.65], [.10,.05,.60,.25]])
B = {'I':[.90,0,.01,0], 'can':[0,.80,.05,.10], 'fish':[0,0,.40,.30]}
V = np.zeros((T, N)) # V[t][j] = best path prob ending in tag j at t
BP = np.zeros((T, N), dtype=int) # backpointers
# --- initialization: t = 1 ---
for j in range(N):
V[0][j] = pi[j] * B[words[0]][j]
# --- recursion: t = 2..T ---
for t in range(1, T):
for j in range(N):
scores = V[t-1] * A[:, j] # V[t-1][i] * A[i][j] for all i
BP[t][j] = np.argmax(scores)
V[t][j] = scores.max() * B[words[t]][j]
# --- termination + backtrace ---
best_last = np.argmax(V[T-1])
path = [0] * T
path[T-1] = best_last
for t in range(T-2, -1, -1):
path[t] = BP[t+1][path[t+1]]
print([tags[i] for i in path]) # -> ['PRON', 'MODAL', 'VERB']
