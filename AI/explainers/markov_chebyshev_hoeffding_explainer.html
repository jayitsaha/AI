1
Understanding the Concept
▼

Concentration inequalities answer one question: how far can a random variable stray from its expected value? They give you upper bounds on the probability that a quantity deviates by more than some threshold \(\varepsilon\).

Think of it like safety margins in engineering. You can't know exactly how many cars will cross a bridge today, but you can bound the worst case. The three classic inequalities form a ladder — each rung uses one more assumption about the distribution, and in return gives a strictly tighter (smaller) bound.

The Ladder

Markov: uses only \(\mathbb{E}[X] \geq 0\). Very weak, but universal.
Chebyshev: uses \(\mathbb{E}[X]\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X)\). Much tighter.
Hoeffding: uses bounded support \([a, b]\). Exponentially tight — transforms the polynomial Chebyshev bound into an exponential decay.

In ML, these three inequalities appear everywhere: PAC learning theory uses Hoeffding to prove generalization bounds; bandit algorithms (UCB, Thompson Sampling) use Hoeffding to build confidence intervals; Chebyshev powers Cantelli's inequality; Markov underpins the first-moment method in probabilistic analysis.

One Thing to Remember

Each step up the ladder costs one assumption and buys one order of magnitude of tightness. Markov gives \(O(1/\varepsilon)\), Chebyshev gives \(O(1/\varepsilon^2)\), Hoeffding gives \(O(e^{-n\varepsilon^2})\) — the jump from polynomial to exponential decay is the key insight.

2
The Three Inequalities — Precise Statements
▼

Markov's Inequality

Let \(X \geq 0\) be a non-negative random variable with finite expectation. For any \(a > 0\):

$$P(X \geq a) \leq \frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{a}$$

Only requires: \(X \geq 0\) and \(\mathbb{E}[X] < \infty\). No distributional assumptions whatsoever.

Chebyshev's Inequality

For any random variable \(X\) with mean \(\mu = \mathbb{E}[X]\) and variance \(\sigma^2 = \operatorname{Var}(X) < \infty\). For any \(\varepsilon > 0\):

$$P(|X - \mu| \geq \varepsilon) \leq \frac{\sigma^2}{\varepsilon^2}$$

Derived from Markov: Apply Markov to the non-negative r.v. \((X - \mu)^2\) with \(a = \varepsilon^2\).

For a sample mean \(\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\) where i.i.d. \(X_i\) have variance \(\sigma^2\):

$$P(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| \geq \varepsilon) \leq \frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2}$$

The bound decays as \(1/n\) — slower than exponential.

Hoeffding's Inequality

Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) be independent random variables with \(X_i \in [a_i, b_i]\). Then for the sum \(S_n = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i\):

$$P(S_n - \mathbb{E}[S_n] \geq t) \leq \exp\!\left(-\frac{2t^2}{\sum_{i=1}^n (b_i - a_i)^2}\right)$$

For the sample mean \(\bar{X}_n\) of i.i.d. \(X_i \in [0, 1]\):

$$P(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| \geq \varepsilon) \leq 2\exp(-2n\varepsilon^2)$$
Common Misconception

Hoeffding does NOT use the variance. It only requires bounded support. This makes it weaker when variance is very small — but when all you know is the range \([a_i, b_i]\), it's the best you can do. Bernstein's inequality refines Hoeffding by also using a variance term, achieving even tighter bounds.

3
Proof Sketches — Where They Come From
▼

Proof of Markov's Inequality

For any \(a > 0\) and non-negative \(X\):

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \int_0^\infty x \, f(x)\,dx \geq \int_a^\infty x \, f(x)\,dx \geq a \int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx = a \cdot P(X \geq a)$$

Dividing both sides by \(a\) gives the result. The key step is replacing \(x\) with its lower bound \(a\) on the integration region \([a, \infty)\).

Proof of Chebyshev from Markov

Apply Markov to the non-negative random variable \(Y = (X - \mu)^2\):

$$P(|X - \mu| \geq \varepsilon) = P((X-\mu)^2 \geq \varepsilon^2) \leq \frac{\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2]}{\varepsilon^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{\varepsilon^2}$$

This elegant reduction is why Chebyshev is sometimes called "second-moment Markov".

Proof Sketch of Hoeffding's Inequality

The proof uses the Chernoff bounding technique (moment generating function method). For any \(s > 0\):

$$P(\bar{X}_n - \mu \geq \varepsilon) = P(e^{s(\bar{X}_n - \mu)} \geq e^{s\varepsilon}) \leq \frac{\mathbb{E}[e^{s(\bar{X}_n - \mu)}]}{e^{s\varepsilon}}$$

The key is bounding the MGF of a bounded r.v. \(X_i \in [a_i, b_i]\) using Hoeffding's lemma:

$$\mathbb{E}[e^{s(X_i - \mathbb{E}[X_i])}] \leq \exp\!\left(\frac{s^2(b_i - a_i)^2}{8}\right)$$

Combining over \(n\) independent variables and optimizing \(s\) gives the exponential bound. The factor of 8 in the denominator comes from the tightest sub-Gaussian constant for bounded random variables.

The Chernoff Trick Unifies All Three

Markov uses the identity function \(x \mapsto x\). Chebyshev uses \(x \mapsto x^2\). Hoeffding uses the exponential \(x \mapsto e^{sx}\). Exponentials grow faster than polynomials, so optimizing over \(s\) gives exponentially tight bounds — this is the essence of why Hoeffding beats Chebyshev.

ML Applications: Generalization Bounds & Bandits
▼

PAC Learning and Generalization

In PAC (Probably Approximately Correct) learning, we want: with probability at least \(1 - \delta\), the empirical risk \(\hat{R}(h)\) is within \(\varepsilon\) of the true risk \(R(h)\). Applying Hoeffding (0-1 loss is bounded in \([0,1]\)):

$$P(|\hat{R}(h) - R(h)| \geq \varepsilon) \leq 2e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}$$

Setting this equal to \(\delta\) and solving: to achieve generalization gap \(\varepsilon\) with confidence \(1-\delta\), we need at least:

$$n \geq \frac{1}{2\varepsilon^2} \log \frac{2}{\delta}$$

This is the fundamental sample complexity bound. For a finite hypothesis class \(\mathcal{H}\) with \(|\mathcal{H}|\) hypotheses, a union bound gives:

$$P\!\left(\exists h \in \mathcal{H}: |\hat{R}(h) - R(h)| \geq \varepsilon\right) \leq 2|\mathcal{H}| e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}$$

Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) for Bandits

In the multi-armed bandit problem, after pulling arm \(i\) for \(n_i\) times with observed mean reward \(\hat{\mu}_i\), Hoeffding gives:

$$P\!\left(\hat{\mu}_i - \mu_i \geq \sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{2n_i}}\right) \leq \delta$$

The UCB1 algorithm sets \(\delta = 1/t^4\) and selects arm \(i^* = \arg\max_i \left[\hat{\mu}_i + \sqrt{\frac{2\log t}{n_i}}\right]\). This confidence interval shrinks as \(1/\sqrt{n_i}\), exactly the Hoeffding rate.

Why not Chebyshev for bandits?

Chebyshev's \(1/n\varepsilon^2\) bound gives confidence intervals that shrink as \(1/\sqrt{n}\) but with a constant \(\sigma\) that's unknown. Hoeffding's bound is distribution-free (only needs boundedness) and still achieves \(O(1/\sqrt{n})\) tightness without needing to estimate variance. That's why it dominates in practice.

Key Takeaways & Numerical Example
▼

Worked Example: \(n=100\), \(\varepsilon=0.15\), Bernoulli\((0.3)\)

Let \(X_1,\ldots,X_{100} \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Bernoulli}(0.3)\). Then \(\mu = 0.3\), \(\sigma^2 = \mu(1-\mu) = 0.21\).

What is \(P(|\bar{X}_{100} - 0.3| \geq 0.15)\)?

Markov (one-tail upper): P(X̄ ≥ 0.3+0.15) ≤ μ/(μ+ε) = 0.3/0.45 = 0.6667 Chebyshev (two-tail): P(|X̄-μ| ≥ 0.15) ≤ σ²/(n·ε²) = 0.21/(100·0.0225) = 0.0933 Hoeffding (two-tail): P(|X̄-μ| ≥ 0.15) ≤ 2·exp(-2·100·0.0225) = 2·e^{-4.5} = 0.0222 True (CLT approx): P(|X̄-μ| ≥ 0.15) ≈ 2·Φ(-0.15/0.04583) ≈ 2·Φ(-3.27) = 0.00106
  • Markov: 66.7% — nearly useless, but requires no distributional info
  • Chebyshev: 9.3% — 7× tighter, uses second moment
  • Hoeffding: 2.2% — 4× tighter than Chebyshev, exponential decay enabled by \([0,1]\) boundedness
  • True tail: 0.11% — Hoeffding is still 20× conservative, but upper bounds are never violated
Why Are All Bounds Loose?

These are distribution-free worst-case bounds. For the specific Uniform distribution, the CLT gives a much better answer. Hoeffding is tight only for distributions that are the hardest case for bounded r.v.s: Bernoulli with \(p=1/2\), which achieves the maximum variance \((b-a)^2/4\) for a bounded r.v. in \([a,b]\). In practice, "the bound is loose" means the algorithm is more conservative than necessary — it's correct, never overconfident.

When to Use Which

  • Markov: you only know \(\mathbb{E}[X] \geq 0\); or as a stepping stone to Chebyshev
  • Chebyshev: you know variance but not the support; unbounded distributions (Gaussian, Poisson)
  • Hoeffding: you know bounded support; need exponential decay; PAC/bandit settings
  • Bernstein: you know both variance AND bounded support — strictly better than Hoeffding
