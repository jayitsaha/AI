Mirror Descent

Gradient descent generalised to non-Euclidean geometry via Bregman divergences
Advanced Optimization Theory Non-Smooth & Constrained
1
Understanding Mirror Descent
▼

Gradient descent is simple: subtract a scaled gradient from your current point. But what if your domain is not flat Euclidean space? What if you are optimising over the set of probability distributions, or a convex polytope, or a spectrahedron? In those settings, the Euclidean step lands outside the domain and projection is expensive — or destroys the structure you care about.

Mirror descent solves this by replacing the Euclidean geometry with a custom geometry tailored to the domain. Instead of measuring distance with \(\|x - y\|_2^2\), it uses a Bregman divergence \(D_\psi(x, y)\) generated by a strongly convex mirror map \(\psi\). The update lifts the current iterate into a dual space (via \(\nabla\psi\)), takes a gradient step there, then maps back — like looking in a mirror that warps Euclidean space into the shape of your problem.

Core Insight

Mirror descent subsumes both gradient descent (choose \(\psi = \tfrac{1}{2}\|x\|_2^2\), Bregman divergence = squared Euclidean distance) and the multiplicative weights update (choose \(\psi = \sum_i x_i \log x_i\), Bregman divergence = KL divergence). One framework, infinitely many algorithms.

The Update Rule
$$x_{t+1} = \underset{x \in \mathcal{X}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\;\bigl\langle g_t,\, x \bigr\rangle + \tfrac{1}{\eta}\,D_\psi(x,\, x_t)$$

where \(g_t = \nabla f(x_t)\) (or a subgradient), \(\eta\) is the step size, and \(D_\psi\) is the Bregman divergence of mirror map \(\psi\).

Analogy. Imagine you're navigating a non-flat terrain (the simplex, or a ball in \(\ell_1\) norm). Euclidean GPS gives you directions as if the terrain were flat — you keep falling off cliffs (boundary of the domain). Mirror descent uses a topographic map that already knows the terrain's curvature: each step stays naturally inside the domain without explicit projection.

Bregman Divergences & Mirror Maps
▼

What is a Bregman Divergence?

Let \(\psi : \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}\) be a continuously differentiable, strictly convex function. The Bregman divergence it generates is:

$$D_\psi(x,\, y) = \psi(x) - \psi(y) - \langle \nabla\psi(y),\, x - y \rangle$$

Geometrically, \(D_\psi(x,y)\) is the gap between \(\psi(x)\) and the first-order Taylor approximation of \(\psi\) at \(y\) evaluated at \(x\). By strict convexity, \(D_\psi(x,y) \geq 0\), with equality iff \(x = y\). Note that Bregman divergences are generally not symmetric: \(D_\psi(x,y) \neq D_\psi(y,x)\).

Two Canonical Choices

Choice 1 — Euclidean (recovers gradient descent)

\(\psi(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}\|x\|_2^2\), so \(\nabla\psi(x) = x\) and \(D_\psi(x,y) = \tfrac{1}{2}\|x-y\|_2^2\). Mirror descent becomes: \(x_{t+1} = \Pi_{\mathcal{X}}(x_t - \eta g_t)\) — exactly projected gradient descent.

Choice 2 — Negative Entropy (gives multiplicative weights)

\(\psi(x) = \sum_i x_i \log x_i\) on the probability simplex \(\Delta_n\). Then \(\nabla\psi(x) = \log x\) (componentwise) and \(D_\psi(x,y) = \sum_i x_i \log(x_i / y_i) = \mathrm{KL}(x \| y)\). The mirror descent update becomes the multiplicative weights update: $$x_{t+1,i} = \frac{x_{t,i}\, e^{-\eta g_{t,i}}}{\sum_j x_{t,j}\, e^{-\eta g_{t,j}}}$$

The Dual-Space View

Mirror descent can equivalently be written as two steps. First, a gradient step in the dual space (the space of linear functions):

$$\theta_{t+1} = \nabla\psi(x_t) - \eta\, g_t$$

Then, mapping back to the primal via the inverse mirror map \(\nabla\psi^* = (\nabla\psi)^{-1}\):

$$x_{t+1} = \nabla\psi^*(\theta_{t+1}) = \underset{x}{\operatorname{argmax}}\;\bigl\{\langle \theta_{t+1}, x\rangle - \psi(x)\bigr\}$$

For negative entropy: \(\nabla\psi(x) = \log x\), \(\nabla\psi^*(\theta) = \operatorname{softmax}(\theta)\). So the dual step is just a linear update, and mapping back is a softmax — no explicit projection onto the simplex needed, yet the iterate always stays on \(\Delta_n\).

Common Confusion

\(D_\psi(x,y)\) is the Bregman divergence from \(y\) to \(x\) (note the argument order convention differs across papers). Always check which argument is the "reference point" in the publication you are reading.

Full Derivation & Regret Bounds
▼

Regret Analysis Setup

Mirror descent is analysed in the online convex optimisation framework. At each round \(t\), we pick \(x_t \in \mathcal{X}\), observe gradient \(g_t\), and incur loss \(\langle g_t, x_t\rangle\). The cumulative regret against the best fixed point \(u \in \mathcal{X}\) is:

$$\mathcal{R}_T(u) = \sum_{t=1}^T \langle g_t, x_t \rangle - \sum_{t=1}^T \langle g_t, u \rangle$$

One-Step Descent Lemma

The key technical step uses the three-point identity for Bregman divergences. For any \(u \in \mathcal{X}\), the mirror descent update satisfies (by the optimality condition of the argmin and Pythagoras for Bregman divergences):

$$\langle g_t,\, x_t - u\rangle \;\leq\; \frac{1}{\eta}\bigl[D_\psi(u, x_t) - D_\psi(u, x_{t+1}) - D_\psi(x_{t+1}, x_t)\bigr] + \eta\,\|g_t\|_*^2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}$$

where \(\|g\|_*\) is the dual norm to the norm used in the Bregman divergence, and we use \(\sigma\)-strong convexity of \(\psi\) (with \(\sigma = 1\) absorbed into step size) to bound the Bregman term from below.

Telescoping to a Regret Bound

Summing over \(t = 1,\ldots,T\) and telescoping:

$$\mathcal{R}_T(u) \;\leq\; \frac{D_\psi(u, x_1)}{\eta} + \frac{\eta}{2}\sum_{t=1}^T \|g_t\|_*^2$$

Assuming \(\|g_t\|_* \leq G\) and choosing \(\eta = \sqrt{2 D_\psi(u, x_1) / (G^2 T)}\):

$$\mathcal{R}_T(u) \;\leq\; G\sqrt{2\,D_\psi(u, x_1)\cdot T}$$

Instantiation: Multiplicative Weights on the Simplex

With \(\psi = \sum_i x_i \log x_i\), \(D_\psi(u, x_1) \leq \log n\) for any \(u \in \Delta_n\) when \(x_1\) is uniform. With \(\ell_\infty\) gradient bound \(\|g\|_\infty \leq G\) (so \(\|g\|_* = \|g\|_1 \leq nG\), but using the entropy-adapted bound \(\|g\|_\infty \leq G\)):

$$\mathcal{R}_T \;\leq\; G\sqrt{2\,T\log n}$$
Why Entropy Beats Euclidean on the Simplex

Projected GD needs \(D = O(\sqrt{n})\) diameter (Euclidean radius of \(\Delta_n\)) giving regret \(O(\sqrt{nT})\). Entropic mirror descent achieves \(O(\sqrt{T \log n})\) — exponentially better in the dimension. This is because the entropy mirror map matches the simplex's natural geometry.

Numerical Trace

Minimise \(f(x) = \langle c, x\rangle\) with \(c = [1, 3, 2]\) on \(\Delta_3\), \(\eta = 0.5\), \(x_0 = (\tfrac{1}{3}, \tfrac{1}{3}, \tfrac{1}{3})\).

Step 0 → 1 (Multiplicative weights):
\(\theta_1 = \log(x_0) - \eta\,c = (-1.0986,\,-1.0986,\,-1.0986) - 0.5\cdot(1,3,2) = (-1.5986,\,-2.5986,\,-2.0986)\)
\(x_1 = \operatorname{softmax}(\theta_1) \approx (0.5065,\; 0.1863,\; 0.3072)\), \(f(x_1) \approx 1.680\).

After 8 steps: \(x_8 \approx (0.982, 0.000, 0.018)\), \(f(x_8) \approx 1.019\). Optimal: \(x^* = e_1 = (1,0,0)\), \(f(x^*) = 1\). Mirror descent approaches the vertex geometrically, always staying strictly inside the simplex.

Multiplicative Weights & Key Takeaways
▼

Multiplicative Weights Update (MWU)

When \(\psi = \sum_i x_i \log x_i\), mirror descent on the simplex is:

$$x_{t+1,i} = \frac{x_{t,i}\, e^{-\eta g_{t,i}}}{\sum_j x_{t,j}\, e^{-\eta g_{t,j}}}$$

This is the Multiplicative Weights Update (MWU) or Hedge algorithm. Components with lower gradient are upweighted exponentially; components with higher gradient are downweighted. It appears throughout CS: boosting (AdaBoost), online portfolio optimisation, game theory (Nash equilibrium computation), and compressed sensing.

When to Use Mirror Descent

  • Constrained domains with natural geometry — simplex (entropy), \(\ell_1\) ball (entropy), positive semidefinite cone (log-det barrier).
  • Sparse or non-negative solutions — entropy mirror map encourages sparse iterates on the simplex.
  • High-dimensional problems — regret scales as \(\sqrt{\log n}\) rather than \(\sqrt{n}\).
  • Online / adversarial settings — MWU is optimal for expert advice, multi-armed bandits, zero-sum games.

When NOT to Use

  • Unconstrained smooth problems — standard gradient descent or Adam are simpler.
  • When the mirror map is hard to compute or invert — the advantage vanishes if \(\nabla\psi^*\) is as expensive as Euclidean projection.
Key Takeaway

Mirror descent is not just a theoretical construction — it is the algorithm behind AdaBoost, the Hedge online learning algorithm, and the natural gradient for exponential-family models. Choosing the right mirror map is equivalent to choosing the right geometry for your problem.
