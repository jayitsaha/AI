Understanding Golub-Kahan SVD
▼

What is it? The Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of a matrix \(A\) gives \(A = U\Sigma V^\top\) where \(\Sigma\) is diagonal with non-negative entries called singular values. Computing this numerically is a two-phase algorithm invented by Gene Golub and William Kahan in 1965 — and refined by Golub and Christian Reinsch in 1970 — that every major numerical library (LAPACK, NumPy, MATLAB, PyTorch) uses under the hood. It is one of the most important algorithms in all of applied mathematics.

Why not a direct approach? The characteristic polynomial route — form \(A^\top A\), compute eigenvalues — fails catastrophically for nearly-singular matrices. If the true smallest singular value is \(\sigma_n = 10^{-7}\) and the condition number of \(A\) is \(10^7\), then \(A^\top A\) has condition number \(10^{14}\), and you have lost all 14 significant digits in double precision. Golub-Kahan avoids this entirely.

Analogy: Imagine peeling an onion. Phase 1 (Householder bidiagonalization) peels away all the off-tridiagonal flesh in just \(2n-1\) orthogonal reflections, leaving only a thin upper-bidiagonal shell — entries only on the main diagonal and one superdiagonal. Phase 2 (implicit-shift QR) then gently squeezes that shell until the superdiagonal entries shrink to machine precision, leaving just the singular values on the diagonal. Each squeeze costs only \(O(n)\) operations because the bidiagonal structure constrains where fill can propagate.

💡 If you remember one thing

Computing SVD directly is numerically unstable. Instead: Phase 1 — use Householder reflectors to reduce \(A_{m \times n}\) to upper-bidiagonal \(B\) (preserving singular values exactly, \(\sigma_i(A) = \sigma_i(B)\)). Phase 2 — apply implicit Wilkinson-shifted QR (Golub-Reinsch) iterations on \(B\) until superdiagonals vanish. Cost: \(O(mn^2)\) for Phase 1, \(O(n^2)\) for Phase 2. The result is backward stable — errors are at machine precision level.

Historical Context & Key Papers
▼

The SVD itself was known since the 19th century (Beltrami 1873, Jordan 1874, Sylvester 1889), but computing it accurately on a computer was an open problem until the mid-1960s. Earlier attempts — such as forming \(A^\top A\) and using the power method — were numerically fragile.

1965 — Golub & Kahan introduced the bidiagonalization reduction in "Calculating the Singular Values and Pseudo-Inverse of a Matrix" (SIAM J. Numer. Anal.). The key insight: use orthogonal similarities to reduce to bidiagonal form first, then apply QR. This preserved numerical stability in a way that direct methods could not.

1970 — Golub & Reinsch refined the algorithm in "Singular Value Decomposition and Least Squares Solutions" (Numerische Mathematik). They added the implicit shift strategy (preventing explicit formation of \(B^\top B\)), proven cubic convergence, and deflation rules. This is the algorithm in LINPACK (1979) and LAPACK (1992) as dgesvd.

1981 — Cuppen introduced divide-and-conquer for tridiagonal eigenproblems. Applied to bidiagonal SVD by Gu & Eisenstat (1994) as LAPACK's dgesdd — faster for large matrices because it exploits the special structure of rank-1 updates.

2011 — Halko, Martinsson & Tropp published "Finding Structure with Randomness" (SIAM Review), giving the randomized SVD that dominates when only the top-\(k\) singular triplets are needed and \(k \ll \min(m,n)\).

∑ The Golub-Kahan Legacy

Virtually every numerical computation of SVD today — PCA, LSA, recommender systems, image compression, control theory — traces directly to the 1965 Golub-Kahan paper and the 1970 Golub-Reinsch refinement. Gene Golub's work on SVD has been called "the most important computation in science."

Two-Phase Mechanics
▼

Phase 1 — Householder Bidiagonalization

A Householder reflector \(H = I - 2uu^\top / \|u\|^2\) is an orthogonal matrix that zeroes out all but one entry of any column (or row) vector. We alternate: a left reflector zeroes everything below the diagonal in column \(j\); a right reflector zeroes everything to the right of the superdiagonal in row \(j\). After \(n\) left and \(n-1\) right reflectors, \(A\) becomes upper-bidiagonal \(B\).

The key insight: \(B = U_B^\top A V_B\) where \(U_B, V_B\) are orthogonal products of Householder matrices. So \(A = U_B B V_B^\top\). Crucially, \(\sigma_i(A) = \sigma_i(B)\) — orthogonal transformations never change singular values.

Phase 2 — Implicit-Shift QR (Golub-Reinsch)

We now have the bidiagonal matrix \(B\). Its singular values equal \(\sqrt{\lambda_i(B^\top B)}\). The matrix \(T = B^\top B\) is symmetric tridiagonal — a perfect target for QR iteration. The Golub-Reinsch algorithm applies Wilkinson-shifted QR iterations implicitly — directly on \(B\) via Givens rotations — without ever forming \(T\). Each iteration costs \(O(n)\) and the superdiagonal entries converge to zero cubically near each singular value.

🔑 Why Work on B, Not B⊤B?

Forming \(T = B^\top B\) squares the condition number: \(\kappa(T) = \kappa(B)^2\). For a nearly-singular matrix with \(\kappa(B) = 10^8\), \(T\) would lose 8 digits of precision. Working directly on \(B\) avoids this, making Golub-Reinsch numerically backward stable.

Numerical Trace: 4×3 Example

Starting from \(A = \begin{bmatrix} 3&2&1\\1&4&2\\0&1&3\\1&0&1 \end{bmatrix}\), true \(\sigma = [6.048, 2.591, 1.925]\):

$$A \xrightarrow{H_1^L} \begin{bmatrix}-3.317&-3.015&-1.809\\0&3.206&1.555\\0&1&3\\0&-0.794&0.555\end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{H_1^R} \begin{bmatrix}-3.317&3.516&0\\0&-3.549&-0.316\\0&-2.401&2.058\\0&0.395&0.885\end{bmatrix}$$
$$\xrightarrow{H_2^L, H_2^R} \begin{bmatrix}-3.317&3.516&0\\0&4.303&0.806\\0&0&2.114\\0&0&0\end{bmatrix} = B \quad \sigma(B) = \sigma(A) \checkmark$$
⚠️ Sign Convention

The diagonal of \(B\) may contain negative entries (e.g., \(-3.317\) above). Singular values are by convention non-negative, so the final step absorbs signs into the \(U\) and \(V\) columns. This is purely cosmetic — the algorithm is correct regardless.

Golub-Kahan Lanczos: Extension to Large Sparse Matrices

When \(A\) is too large to store explicitly (e.g., a 10⁶ × 10⁶ sparse web-graph matrix), the Householder approach is impractical. The Golub-Kahan Lanczos (GKL) process computes the same bidiagonalization using only matrix-vector products \(Av\) and \(A^\top u\). Starting from a random unit vector \(b\):

$$\beta_1 u_1 = b, \quad \alpha_j v_j = A^\top u_j - \beta_j v_{j-1}, \quad \beta_{j+1} u_{j+1} = A v_j - \alpha_j u_j$$

After \(k\) steps this builds \(k\)-dimensional subspaces \(U_k, V_k\) such that \(A V_k = U_k B_k\) where \(B_k\) is \(k \times k\) bidiagonal. The largest singular values of \(A\) are approximated by those of \(B_k\) — this is the core of ARPACK's SVDS and sklearn's TruncatedSVD with algorithm='arpack'.

🔑 Deflation: The Stopping Rule

In Phase 2, when \(|\beta_j^{(k)}| \leq \epsilon_{\text{mach}}(\alpha_j + \alpha_{j+1})\) for some \(j\), that entry is declared zero and the bidiagonal splits into two independent subproblems — this is deflation. It prevents wasted iterations on already-converged singular values and is the key efficiency feature of the Golub-Reinsch algorithm.

3
The Mathematics
▼

Householder Reflector

Given a vector \(x \in \mathbb{R}^k\), choose \(u = x + \text{sign}(x_1)\|x\|_2 e_1\). The reflector \(H = I - 2uu^\top/\|u\|^2\) satisfies \(Hx = -\text{sign}(x_1)\|x\|_2 e_1\), zeroing all entries except the first. \(H\) is symmetric and orthogonal: \(H^\top H = I\).

$$H = I - \frac{2}{\|u\|^2} u u^\top, \quad u = x + \operatorname{sign}(x_1)\|x\|_2 e_1$$

Bidiagonalization Invariant

After all \(2n-1\) reflectors, we obtain:

$$U_B^\top A V_B = B = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha_1 & \beta_1 & & \\ & \alpha_2 & \beta_2 & \\ & & \ddots & \beta_{n-1}\\ & & & \alpha_n \\ 0 & \cdots & & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

where \(\alpha_j = \|x^{(j)}\|\) from each left step and \(\beta_j = \|y^{(j)}\|\) from each right step.

Wilkinson Shift for QR

On the bottom \(2\times 2\) submatrix \(T_{22}\) of \(T = B^\top B\), the Wilkinson shift \(\mu\) is the eigenvalue of \(T_{22}\) closest to \(T_{n,n}\):

$$\mu = T_{n,n} - \frac{\beta_{n-1}^2}{\delta + \operatorname{sign}(\delta)\sqrt{\delta^2 + \beta_{n-1}^2}}, \quad \delta = \frac{T_{n-1,n-1} - T_{n,n}}{2}$$

The Wilkinson shift guarantees cubic convergence of the trailing superdiagonal entry \(|\beta_{n-1}^{(k)}| \to 0\).

Backward Stability

The Golub-Reinsch algorithm is backward stable: the computed singular values \(\hat{\sigma}_i\) satisfy

$$|\hat{\sigma}_i - \sigma_i| \leq \epsilon_{\text{mach}} \|A\|_2 + O(\epsilon_{\text{mach}}^2)$$

This means errors are at the level of floating-point rounding on the input data — you cannot do better without higher precision arithmetic. In contrast, eigendecomposing \(A^\top A\) gives errors \(O(\epsilon_{\text{mach}} \|A\|_2^2 / \sigma_i)\), which can be catastrophically large when \(\sigma_i \ll \|A\|_2\).

Givens Rotation (Phase 2 Tool)

Each QR step uses Givens rotations \(G(i,j,\theta)\) — 2×2 plane rotations that zero one off-diagonal entry without affecting other entries. For the bidiagonal case:

$$G = \begin{bmatrix} c & s \\ -s & c \end{bmatrix}, \quad c = \cos\theta, \; s = \sin\theta, \quad \text{chosen so} \; G\begin{bmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}r\\0\end{bmatrix}$$

where \(r = \sqrt{\alpha^2 + \beta^2}\), \(c = \alpha/r\), \(s = \beta/r\). A single Givens rotation costs only 6 multiplications and 2 additions — far cheaper than a full matrix multiply.

Complexity

∑ Cost Breakdown

Phase 1: \(O\!\left(2mn^2 - \tfrac{2}{3}n^3\right)\) flops (dominant for thin matrices \(m \gg n\)). Phase 2: \(O(n)\) flops per Givens sweep, \(O(n)\) sweeps to convergence → \(O(n^2)\) total for Phase 2. Accumulating \(U, V\): adds \(O(mn^2)\) and \(O(n^3)\) respectively. Full SVD of \(m \times n\) matrix: \(O(4mn^2 + 8n^3)\) for \(m \geq n\). By comparison, LAPACK dgesdd (divide-and-conquer) is \(O(mn\min(m,n))\) — faster but less memory-stable.

Relationship to Eigendecomposition

The singular values of \(A\) are the square roots of eigenvalues of \(A^\top A\) (or \(AA^\top\)). But note: \(A\) has a full SVD

$$A = U\Sigma V^\top \implies A^\top A = V\Sigma^2 V^\top, \quad AA^\top = U\Sigma^2 U^\top$$

so the right singular vectors \(V\) are eigenvectors of \(A^\top A\), and left singular vectors \(U\) are eigenvectors of \(AA^\top\). The Golub-Kahan algorithm exploits this by reducing to the bidiagonal form first — its \(B^\top B\) is symmetric tridiagonal, a much simpler eigenvalue problem than a general symmetric matrix.

From-Scratch NumPy Implementation
▼

Below is a pedagogical Golub-Kahan bidiagonalization in 30 lines of NumPy, matching the algorithm step-for-step:

import numpy as np
def householder_vec(x):
"""Return (v, beta) such that (I - beta*v*v') x = ||x||*e1."""
sigma = x[1:] @ x[1:] # sum of squares of x[1:]
v = x.copy(); v[0] = 1.0
if sigma < 1e-28 and x[0] >= 0: # already pointing right
return v, 0.0
mu = np.sqrt(x[0]**2 + sigma)
v[0] = x[0] - mu if x[0] <= 0 else -sigma / (x[0] + mu)
beta = 2 * v[0]**2 / (sigma + v[0]**2)
v /= v[0] # normalize so v[0]=1
return v, beta
def golub_kahan_bidiag(A):
"""Phase 1: Reduce A (m x n) to upper-bidiagonal B = Ul' @ A @ Vr."""
m, n = A.shape
B = A.astype(float).copy()
for j in range(n):
# Left Householder: zero B[j+1:, j]
v, beta = householder_vec(B[j:, j])
if beta != 0: # I - beta * v * v' applied left
B[j:, j:] -= beta * np.outer(v, v @ B[j:, j:])
if j < n - 1:
# Right Householder: zero B[j, j+2:]
v2, beta2 = householder_vec(B[j, j+1:].copy())
if beta2 != 0: # I - beta2 * v2 * v2' applied right
B[j:, j+1:] -= beta2 * (B[j:, j+1:] @ v2)[:, None] * v2
return B # upper-bidiagonal; sigma(B) == sigma(A)
# Usage
A = np.array([[3.,2.,1.],[1.,4.,2.],[0.,1.,3.],[1.,0.,1.]])
B = golub_kahan_bidiag(A)
print("Bidiagonal B:\n", np.round(B, 4))
print("sigma(B):", np.linalg.svd(B[:3,:], compute_uv=False))
print("sigma(A):", np.linalg.svd(A, compute_uv=False))
# → [6.0483, 2.5909, 1.9251] identical ✓
💡 Production Tip

In production, use numpy.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False) (thin SVD) or scipy.linalg.svd. These call LAPACK's dgesdd (divide-and-conquer SVD, even faster than Golub-Reinsch for large matrices) or dgesvd (the classical Golub-Reinsch algorithm). The bidiagonalization step is identical.

Numerical Check (run in Python)

import numpy as np
A = np.array([[3.,2.,1.],[1.,4.,2.],[0.,1.,3.],[1.,0.,1.]])
# Full SVD (Golub-Kahan internally)
U, sigma, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)
print("Singular values:", np.round(sigma, 4)) # [6.0483, 2.5909, 1.9251]
print("A ≈ U @ diag(σ) @ Vt:", np.allclose(A, U @ np.diag(sigma) @ Vt)) # True
print("U orthogonal:", np.allclose(U.T @ U, np.eye(3))) # True
print("V orthogonal:", np.allclose(Vt @ Vt.T, np.eye(3))) # True
# Condition number (ratio of largest to smallest singular value)
kappa = sigma[0] / sigma[-1]
print(f"Condition number κ(A) = {kappa:.4f}") # 3.141
print(f"κ(A^T A) would be {kappa**2:.2f} — squared!") # 9.87
# Truncated SVD (top-2 singular values only)
from sklearn.decomposition import TruncatedSVD
svd = TruncatedSVD(n_components=2, algorithm='randomized', random_state=0)
svd.fit(A)
print("Top-2 σ (randomized):", np.round(svd.singular_values_, 4)) # ≈ [6.048, 2.591]

Key Takeaways & When It Matters
▼

Why this matters for ML: Every PCA, LSA, recommender system, and truncated SVD solver (e.g., sklearn.decomposition.TruncatedSVD) ultimately relies on this two-phase structure. Understanding it helps you debug numerical failures and choose the right variant (full vs. truncated vs. randomized SVD). It also teaches you what "numerically stable" actually means at the algorithm level — not just floating-point tips, but the profound idea that a good algorithm should be backward stable: the computed result is the exact answer to a slightly perturbed problem.

  • Phase 1 is exact (up to floating-point): singular values of \(B\) are identical to those of \(A\). This is guaranteed by the orthogonality of Householder reflectors.
  • Phase 2 is iterative but cubically convergent near each singular value: the number of QR sweeps grows only linearly in \(n\).
  • Golub-Kahan Lanczos extends Phase 1 to large sparse matrices using only matrix-vector products — no explicit \(A\) storage needed.
  • Randomized SVD (Halko-Martinsson-Tropp) replaces Phase 1 with a random sketch — correct in expectation, \(O(mnk)\) instead of \(O(mn^2)\) for top-\(k\) components.
  • Never form \(A^\top A\) for SVD: the condition number is squared, losing \(\log_{10}(\kappa(A))\) decimal digits of accuracy.
🚨 Common Interview Trap

"How do you compute SVD?" — Wrong answer: "Form \(A^\top A\) and eigendecompose." This squares the condition number and loses half the significant digits. Correct: bidiagonalize first (Phase 1), then apply QR on the bidiagonal (Phase 2). LAPACK has done this since 1992.

Algorithm Comparison

Method Phase 2 Best For LAPACK routine
Golub-Reinsch (GR-SVD) QR iteration on \(B\) All singular values dgesvd
Divide & Conquer Recursive splitting Dense matrices, fastest dgesdd
Golub-Kahan Lanczos Lanczos bidiag. + GR-SVD on small \(B_k\) Large sparse, top-\(k\) only ARPACK/scipy svds
Randomized SVD QR + small SVD Very large, low-rank approx sklearn TruncatedSVD

All four methods start with the same bidiagonalization idea — they differ only in how they finish Phase 2 and whether they target all or just the top-\(k\) singular values.

Decision Guide

  • Dense, need all singular values: numpy.linalg.svd → LAPACK dgesdd (divide-and-conquer).
  • Dense, need top-k only (\(k \ll n\)): sklearn.utils.extmath.randomized_svd.
  • Sparse matrix, top-k: scipy.sparse.linalg.svds (Golub-Kahan Lanczos + ARPACK).
  • GPU workloads: torch.linalg.svd (cuSOLVER Jacobi or D&C on GPU).

Interview Deep-Dive
▼

Q1 (Medium): Why does LAPACK use bidiagonalization before QR for SVD?

A: Direct QR on a general matrix introduces fill (non-zeros propagate everywhere), making each iteration \(O(mn)\) and requiring many iterations. Bidiagonalization reduces \(A\) to a form where QR iterations only propagate within a 2-band structure, making each Givens rotation touch only 2 rows/columns — \(O(n)\) per sweep. More critically, working on \(B\) avoids squaring the condition number that would occur when forming \(A^\top A\).

Q2 (Medium): What is the Wilkinson shift and why does it give cubic convergence?

A: The Wilkinson shift \(\mu\) is the eigenvalue of the bottom \(2\times 2\) submatrix of \(T = B^\top B\) closest to \(T_{n,n}\). Applying QR with this shift to \(T - \mu I\) means the trailing diagonal entry starts extremely close to zero — the convergence rate is \(|\beta_{n-1}^{(k+1)}| = O(|\beta_{n-1}^{(k)}|^3)\). Without a shift, convergence is only linear (or quadratic with a simpler shift).

Q3 (Hard): When does Golub-Reinsch fail, and what is the fix?

A: The algorithm can stagnate when two singular values are nearly equal (clustered spectrum), causing the Wilkinson shift to oscillate. The fix is the "zero-shifting" strategy: when \(|\alpha_j|\) or \(|\beta_j|\) falls below \(\epsilon_{\text{mach}} \|B\|_F\), set it exactly to zero and split the problem. In LAPACK this is handled by the DSVDC subroutine's deflation check. For clusters, divide-and-conquer (\texttt{dgesdd}) is preferred as it handles clusters via secular equations rather than iterative convergence.

Q4 (Expert): How does randomized SVD compare to Golub-Kahan for ML workloads?

A: For exact SVD of a dense \(m \times n\) matrix, Golub-Kahan is optimal. For top-\(k\) SVD with \(k \ll n\), randomized SVD costs \(O(mnk + k^3)\) vs. \(O(mn^2)\) for full SVD — a factor of \(n/k\) speedup. The trade-off: randomized SVD has approximation error \(O(\sigma_{k+1})\) (best possible for rank-\(k\) approximation by Eckart-Young-Mirsky), whereas Golub-Kahan on full \(A\) is exact. In practice, scipy.sparse.linalg.svds (ARPACK, Golub-Kahan Lanczos) outperforms randomized SVD when \(A\) is sparse; sklearn.utils.extmath.randomized_svd dominates for dense matrices with fast matrix-vector products.

⚠️ Red Flag Answer

"I would compute SVD by finding eigenvalues of \(A^\top A\)." — This is not wrong in theory but demonstrates a lack of numerical awareness. In interviews for numerical ML roles (scientific computing, simulation, signal processing), this answer will raise concerns. Always mention bidiagonalization and the condition number squaring problem.

Q5 (PhD-level): What is the connection to Lanczos tridiagonalization?

A: Lanczos tridiagonalization reduces a symmetric matrix \(S\) to tridiagonal form \(T = Q^\top S Q\) using only matrix-vector products \(Sv\). Golub-Kahan bidiagonalization is essentially a non-symmetric generalization: by applying the process to the augmented symmetric matrix \(\begin{bmatrix}0 & A \\ A^\top & 0\end{bmatrix}\), one recovers both left and right singular vectors simultaneously. The bidiagonal \(B\) is related to the tridiagonal of the augmented matrix by \(T = \begin{bmatrix}0 & B \\ B^\top & 0\end{bmatrix}\), whose eigenvalues are \(\pm\sigma_i(B)\).
