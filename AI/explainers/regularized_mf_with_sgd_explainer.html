Learning latent user & item vectors from sparse ratings, one gradient step at a time
Foundational Recommender Systems Optimization O(k·|observed|) per epoch
1
Understanding Regularized MF
▼

What is it? Matrix Factorization (MF) is the workhorse of collaborative-filtering recommenders. Given a sparse user × item ratings matrix \(R\) (most entries unknown — nobody rates most movies), MF learns a low-rank approximation \(R \approx P Q^\top\), where every user \(u\) gets a latent "taste vector" \(p_u \in \mathbb{R}^k\) and every item \(i\) gets a latent "trait vector" \(q_i \in \mathbb{R}^k\). The predicted rating is simply the dot product \(\hat{r}_{ui} = p_u \cdot q_i\): the more a user's taste aligns with an item's traits, the higher the predicted score.

Analogy: imagine every user and every movie gets secretly plotted as a point in a small k-dimensional "flavor space" — one axis might informally correspond to "prefers action over romance," another to "prefers indie over blockbuster." A user sitting close (in direction) to a movie in that space will love it; one pointing the opposite way will hate it. Nobody tells the model what the axes mean — Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) discovers vectors that reconstruct the ratings we actually observed, and regularization keeps those vectors from growing wild and overfitting the sparse, noisy signal.

💡 If you remember one thing

For every observed rating \(r_{ui}\), SGD nudges \(p_u\) and \(q_i\) a little in the direction that reduces the prediction error \(e_{ui} = r_{ui} - p_u\cdot q_i\), while an L2 penalty \(\lambda(\lVert p_u\rVert^2 + \lVert q_i\rVert^2)\) constantly shrinks both vectors toward zero. The update: $$p_u \leftarrow p_u + \eta\,(e_{ui}\,q_i - \lambda\,p_u), \qquad q_i \leftarrow q_i + \eta\,(e_{ui}\,p_u - \lambda\,q_i)$$

How It Works Internally
▼

The Objective

Let \(\mathcal{K}\) be the set of observed \((u,i)\) pairs. We minimize a regularized sum of squared errors over only those entries — the missing entries never enter the loss, which is exactly what makes MF work on 90%+ sparse matrices:

$$J(P,Q) = \sum_{(u,i)\in\mathcal{K}} \big(r_{ui} - p_u\cdot q_i\big)^2 \;+\; \lambda\Big(\sum_u \lVert p_u\rVert^2 + \sum_i \lVert q_i\rVert^2\Big)$$

Why SGD and not solve it exactly?

\(J\) is not jointly convex in \((P,Q)\) — fixing \(Q\) makes it convex in \(P\) and vice versa, but not both together. Instead of an expensive alternating batch solve, SGD walks through the observed ratings one at a time, in random order, and takes a small gradient step after each one. This makes each update \(O(k)\) — cheap enough to fit datasets with billions of ratings, streamed from disk, updated online as new ratings arrive.

Design Decision: why regularize both factors?

Without \(\lambda\), the optimizer can drive \(\lVert p_u\rVert\) and \(\lVert q_i\rVert\) to huge, precisely-cancelling values that perfectly memorize the few ratings a sparse user/item has — classic overfitting. The L2 penalty is a weight-decay force that competes with the error-correction force on every single step: fit the data, but stay small. This is ridge regression, applied jointly to two coupled factor matrices instead of one coefficient vector.

🔑 Key Property

Regularized MF-SGD is exactly the algorithm Simon Funk popularized during the Netflix Prize ("FunkSVD"). It scales to massive sparse matrices because cost per update depends only on \(k\), never on the total number of users or items.

Why It Works — The Math
▼

Deriving the Gradient

For a single observed rating \((u,i,r_{ui})\), define the per-example loss \(\ell_{ui} = e_{ui}^2 + \lambda(\lVert p_u\rVert^2 + \lVert q_i\rVert^2)\) where \(e_{ui} = r_{ui} - p_u\cdot q_i\). Differentiate with respect to \(p_u\) (treating \(q_i\) as constant for this step):

$$\frac{\partial \ell_{ui}}{\partial p_u} = -2\,e_{ui}\,q_i + 2\lambda\,p_u$$

Taking a gradient-descent step with learning rate \(\eta\) (absorbing the factor of 2 into \(\eta\)) gives the update rule used every step of the walkthrough below:

$$p_u \leftarrow p_u - \eta\frac{\partial \ell_{ui}}{\partial p_u} = p_u + \eta\,(e_{ui}\,q_i - \lambda\,p_u)$$

By symmetry, differentiating with respect to \(q_i\) (holding \(p_u\) fixed) yields the mirrored update \(q_i \leftarrow q_i + \eta(e_{ui}\,p_u - \lambda\,q_i)\). Because both updates use the same error \(e_{ui}\) but scaled by the other vector, the two factors co-adapt: as \(p_u\) moves toward \(q_i\)'s direction, \(q_i\) simultaneously moves toward \(p_u\)'s — they chase each other toward agreement on \(r_{ui}\), while the \(-\lambda\,p_u\) and \(-\lambda\,q_i\) terms constantly pull both back toward the origin.

Geometric View of Regularization

Each update is a sum of two forces: an error-correction vector \(\eta\,e_{ui}\,q_i\) that reduces \(e_{ui}^2\), and a shrinkage vector \(-\eta\lambda\,p_u\) that scales \(p_u\) by a factor of \((1-\eta\lambda)\) regardless of the data. In the fixed point of training, a vector's norm settles where these two forces balance — observed, "popular" users/items with many ratings can support larger norms; sparse ones get shrunk hard toward zero, which is precisely the desired behaviour (don't trust noisy, low-evidence estimates).

⚠️ Common Pitfall

Too large a \(\lambda\) shrinks every \(p_u,q_i\) toward zero, collapsing all predictions to roughly the global average rating (underfitting). Too small a \(\lambda\) (or \(\lambda=0\)) lets vectors grow to memorize the sparse training ratings, generalizing poorly to new user-item pairs. \(\lambda\) must be tuned on held-out ratings, exactly like ridge regression's penalty.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use regularized MF-SGD:

  • Large, sparse explicit-feedback matrices (ratings, purchases) — classic collaborative filtering
  • Streaming settings where new ratings arrive continuously and the model should update online
  • You need a compact, interpretable latent representation of users and items for downstream use (search, clustering, cold-start blending)

When NOT to use / watch out for:

  • Cold start: a brand-new user/item with zero ratings has no gradient signal at all — its vector never moves from initialization. Needs content features or hybrid models.
  • Implicit feedback (clicks, views) needs a different loss (e.g. weighted/BPR) since "no rating" isn't a negative signal here.
  • If side information (genres, demographics) is rich, a hybrid or neural collaborative-filtering model usually beats plain MF.

Hyperparameters that matter most: latent dimension \(k\) (capacity vs. overfitting), learning rate \(\eta\) (too high diverges, too low is slow), regularization \(\lambda\) (bias/variance trade-off), and number of epochs (with early stopping on a held-out RMSE).

💡 Lineage

MF-SGD (this page) → add per-user/item bias terms \(b_u, b_i\) → ALS (alternate exact ridge solves instead of per-example steps, easier to parallelize) → Neural CF / two-tower models (replace the dot product with a learned similarity function). Every later model still fights the same error-vs-regularization tension you see in the walkthrough above.

import numpy as np
# Sparse rating matrix (NaN = missing)
R = np.array([
[5, 3, np.nan, 1],
[4, np.nan, np.nan, 1],
[1, 1, np.nan, 5],
[np.nan, np.nan, 5, 4],
])
n_users, n_items = R.shape
k = 2 # latent dimensions
lr, lam = 0.08, 0.06 # learning rate, regularization
# Random init of latent factor matrices
P = 0.4 * (np.random.rand(n_users, k) - 0.5)
Q = 0.4 * (np.random.rand(n_items, k) - 0.5)
observed = [(u, i, R[u, i]) for u in range(n_users)
for i in range(n_items) if not np.isnan(R[u, i])]
for epoch in range(epochs):
for u, i, r in observed:
pred = P[u] @ Q[i] # prediction = dot product
err = r - pred # residual e_ui
# SGD step with L2 (ridge) regularization
P[u] += lr * (err * Q[i] - lam * P[u])
Q[i] += lr * (err * P[u] - lam * Q[i])
loss = sum((r - P[u] @ Q[i])**2 for u, i, r in observed)
loss += lam * (np.sum(P**2) + np.sum(Q**2))
R_hat = P @ Q.T # full reconstructed matrix
