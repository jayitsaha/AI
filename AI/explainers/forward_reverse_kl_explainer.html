Forward KL vs Reverse KL

Mode-covering vs mode-seeking behaviour — and why it matters for VI and MLE
Intermediate Information Theory Variational Inference KL Divergence
1
Understanding Forward vs Reverse KL
▼

What is it? KL divergence measures how different one probability distribution is from another. But KL(p||q) and KL(q||p) are not symmetric — they penalise different types of mistakes. This asymmetry has dramatic consequences when you try to fit a simple distribution q to a complex one p.

Analogy: Imagine fitting a single bell-shaped hat over two separate mountains. Forward KL forces the hat to cover both mountains — it stretches wide and sits in the valley between them, touching everywhere p has mass. Reverse KL lets you choose which mountain to put the hat on — it locks onto one peak, ignoring the other entirely.

If you remember one thing

Forward KL(p||q) is zero-avoiding — q must be positive everywhere p is positive, forcing mean-covering behaviour. Reverse KL(q||p) is zero-forcing — q can be zero where p is large, enabling mode-seeking behaviour. This single asymmetry divides MLE (forward KL) from Variational Inference (reverse KL).

Why does the direction matter?

The formula \(\text{KL}(p \| q) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\!\left[\log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right]\) averages over draws from p. If q(x) = 0 somewhere that p(x) > 0, the log ratio blows up to +∞. So forward KL forces q to be nonzero everywhere p is. The reverse \(\text{KL}(q \| p) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim q}\!\left[\log \frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\right]\) averages over q — q pays no penalty for regions where p is large but q = 0.

2
Mode-Covering vs Mode-Seeking — Internally
▼

Forward KL — zero-avoiding (mode-covering)

Minimising \(\text{KL}(p \| q)\) over a Gaussian q fit to a bimodal p yields the moment-matching solution: q picks up the mean and variance of p. For p = 0.5 N(−2, 0.7²) + 0.5 N(2, 0.7²) (symmetric bimodal), the optimal q is:

$$q^*_{\text{fwd}} = \mathcal{N}(0,\; 2.119^2)$$

This q straddles both modes — its mean sits in the empty valley between them, and its standard deviation 2.119 is large enough to give appreciable mass to both peaks. Forward KL prefers hallucinating mass in the valley over leaving any mode uncovered.

Reverse KL — zero-forcing (mode-seeking)

Minimising \(\text{KL}(q \| p)\) over a Gaussian q yields a solution locked onto one mode:

$$q^*_{\text{rev}} = \mathcal{N}(\pm1.982,\; 0.726^2)$$

The optimal q has virtually the same width as each individual mode of p (true width 0.7), and its mean sits almost exactly on one peak. Reverse KL prefers ignoring the other mode entirely over spreading mass into the low-p valley between them.

Mechanistic reason

The gradient of \(\text{KL}(q\|p)\) w.r.t. q's parameters has terms of the form \(\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\). Where p(x) ≈ 0 between modes, q(x)/p(x) is huge, exerting a strong repulsive force pushing q away from the valley. The objective strongly penalises q placing mass where p has little mass.

Connection to MLE and Variational Inference

MLE minimises forward KL: Maximising \(\frac{1}{N}\sum_i \log p_\theta(x_i)\) is equivalent to minimising \(\text{KL}(\hat{p}_{\text{data}} \| p_\theta)\). With enough capacity, the model must cover all modes in the data — mode-dropping is penalised. This is why GANs (trained with reverse-like objectives) can drop modes while MLE-trained models tend to be more mean-covering but blurry.

VI minimises reverse KL: In Variational Inference, we minimise \(\text{KL}(q_\phi \| p(\cdot | x))\) (or equivalently maximise the ELBO). The approximate posterior q tends to collapse to one mode of the true posterior — underestimating uncertainty but making sharp predictions.

3
The Mathematics — Why Each Objective Behaves Differently
▼

Forward KL minimisation — moment matching

For a Gaussian family \(q = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\):

$$\text{KL}(p \| q) = \mathbb{E}_p[\log p] - \mathbb{E}_p[\log q] = \text{const} + \frac{(\mu - \mathbb{E}_p[x])^2 + \mathbb{E}_p[x^2] - \mathbb{E}_p[x]^2}{2\sigma^2} + \log \sigma$$

Setting \(\partial / \partial \mu = 0\) and \(\partial / \partial \sigma = 0\) gives the closed-form solution:

$$\mu^* = \mathbb{E}_p[x], \quad (\sigma^*)^2 = \mathbb{E}_p[x^2] - (\mathbb{E}_p[x])^2 = \operatorname{Var}_p(x)$$

For our bimodal p: \(\mathbb{E}_p[x] = 0\), \(\operatorname{Var}_p(x) = 0.5(4+0.49)+0.5(4+0.49) = 4.49\), so \(\sigma^* = \sqrt{4.49} \approx 2.119\). The optimal q must match all mass — it cannot mode-seek.

Reverse KL minimisation — variational fixed point

There is no closed form for general p. The gradient is:

$$\nabla_\phi \,\text{KL}(q_\phi \| p) = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}\!\left[\nabla_\phi \log q_\phi(x)\cdot\left(\log q_\phi(x) - \log p(x)\right)\right]$$

At a local minimum, the score equation \(\nabla_\phi \log q_\phi(x) = 0\) must be balanced against \(\log q/p\). Between the two modes of p, \(\log p(x)\) is very small (p near zero), so \(\log q(x) - \log p(x)\) is very large and positive — any gradient step away from the valley is strongly rewarded. The optimiser drives q into one mode.

Common misconception

"Reverse KL is always mode-seeking." — Not exactly. If q has enough capacity to represent all modes simultaneously (e.g., q is itself a mixture), reverse KL can recover the full distribution. The mode-seeking arises specifically when q is unimodal and p is multimodal. The culprit is restricted q-family, not the KL direction alone.

Numerical summary

  • Forward KL at optimal q_fwd: KL(p||q_fwd) = 0.421 nats
  • Reverse KL at optimal q_rev: KL(q_rev||p) = 0.686 nats
  • Forward KL at q_rev (mode-seeking solution): KL(p||q_rev) = 6.839 nats — very high, p is not covered
  • Reverse KL at q_fwd (mean-covering solution): KL(q_fwd||p) = 0.810 nats — higher than optimal, q puts mass in low-p valley

Key Takeaways
▼

Forward KL (p||q) — use when you need coverage

  • MLE training of generative models (language models, density estimators)
  • You want q to assign mass wherever p does — hallucination over omission
  • Result: mean-covering, high entropy, possibly diffuse/blurry

Reverse KL (q||p) — use when you need precision

  • Variational Inference (ELBO = negative reverse KL + log evidence)
  • You want sharp, confident approximations — even at the cost of missing modes
  • Result: mode-seeking, low entropy, overconfident on chosen mode
Practical rule of thumb

If you train with forward KL: your model will be diverse but may hallucinate (put mass in unlikely places). If you train with reverse KL: your model will be sharp but may miss modes — a risk in multimodal posteriors.

import numpy as np; from scipy.optimize import minimize
# Bimodal target: mixture of two Gaussians
def p(x): return 0.5*gauss(x,-2,0.7) + 0.5*gauss(x,2,0.7)
def gauss(x,mu,s): return np.exp(-0.5*((x-mu)/s)**2)/(s*np.sqrt(2*np.pi))
# Forward KL(p||q): optimal Gaussian = moment match
mu_fwd = 0.0 # E_p[x] = 0 by symmetry
sig_fwd = sqrt(Var_p[x]) # sqrt(0.5*(4+0.49)+0.5*(4+0.49)) = 2.119
q_fwd = gauss(x, 0.0, 2.119) # covers BOTH modes
# Reverse KL(q||p): no closed form — gradient descent
def rev_kl(params):
mu, sig = params
x = np.linspace(mu-5*sig, mu+5*sig, 3000)
q = gauss(x, mu, sig)
return np.trapezoid(q * (log(q) - log(p(x))), x)
res = minimize(rev_kl, [2.0, 0.7]) # converges to one mode
mu_rev, sig_rev = 1.982, 0.726 # locks onto +2 peak
q_rev = gauss(x, 1.982, 0.726) # ignores -2 mode entirely
