Task Weighting Strategies in Multi-Task Learning

Balancing multiple objectives: Fixed, Uncertainty, GradNorm, Dynamic Weight Averaging
Intermediate Deep Learning Multi-Task Learning O(N_tasks) to O(N_tasks * N_params)
1
Understanding Task Weighting
▼

What is it? Multi-task learning (MTL) trains a single model to perform several related tasks simultaneously. This often improves generalization and data efficiency. However, tasks can have vastly different loss magnitudes, convergence speeds, or inherent difficulties. Simply summing their individual losses can lead to one task dominating the training, hindering progress on others. Task weighting strategies are techniques to dynamically or statically adjust the contribution of each task's loss to the total training objective, ensuring a more balanced and effective learning process.

Analogy: Imagine a student studying for three different exams: Math, History, and Art. If the Math exam has very hard problems (high loss values) and the Art exam has easy ones (low loss values), and the student just sums up their "mistake points" from practice, they might spend all their time on Math and neglect Art, even if they're already good at Math. A smart student (or a good weighting strategy) would prioritize based on how much they *need* to improve in each subject, or how much each subject contributes to their overall grade, not just raw mistake count.

💡 If you remember one thing

In Multi-Task Learning, simply summing task losses (\(L_{total} = \sum L_i\)) is often suboptimal. Task weighting introduces coefficients \(w_i\) such that \(L_{total} = \sum w_i L_i\), allowing us to balance the influence of each task. The challenge is choosing these \(w_i\) effectively, either manually or adaptively.

2
How Weighting Strategies Work Internally
▼

The Naive Approach: Unweighted Sum

The simplest approach is to sum all task losses equally: \(L_{total} = \sum_{k=1}^K L_k\). This works if tasks have similar loss scales and learning dynamics. However, if one task's loss is inherently much larger (e.g., a large regression loss vs. a small classification cross-entropy), its gradients will dominate the shared layers, effectively sidelining other tasks. Similarly, if one task converges much faster, its loss will quickly become small, reducing its influence prematurely.

Fixed Weighting: Manual Prioritization

The most straightforward solution is to assign fixed, hand-tuned weights: \(L_{total} = \sum_{k=1}^K w_k L_k\). These weights are constants determined before training and typically require extensive hyperparameter search or domain expertise. While simple, it's often effective for well-understood task relationships or when specific tasks must be prioritized. The major drawback is the lack of adaptability during training.

Adaptive Weighting: Dynamic Balance

Modern strategies dynamically adjust weights during training, often based on observed loss values, gradient magnitudes, or task uncertainties. This allows the model to respond to changing learning dynamics. Key approaches include:

  • Uncertainty Weighting (Kendall et al., 2018): Models the homoscedastic uncertainty (noise) of each task. Tasks with higher predicted noise (uncertainty) are down-weighted, and vice-versa. The model learns these uncertainties alongside task parameters.
  • Gradient Normalization (GradNorm; Chen et al., 2018): Aims to balance the magnitudes of gradients contributed by each task to shared layers. It dynamically adjusts weights to ensure all tasks learn at comparable rates, preventing one task's gradients from overwhelming others.
  • Dynamic Weight Averaging (DWA; Liu et al., 2019): Adjusts weights based on how fast each task's loss is decreasing relative to other tasks. Tasks whose losses are decreasing slowly (i.e., "struggling" or converging slowly) get higher weights, encouraging the model to focus on them.

Design Decision: When to Choose Which?

Fixed weighting is a good baseline if you have strong priors about task importance or if tasks are very similar. Uncertainty Weighting is great when tasks have different inherent noise levels. GradNorm is ideal when you want to ensure all tasks contribute "fairly" to the overall gradient magnitude. DWA is effective for dynamic scenarios where tasks might converge at different speeds, allowing the model to adapt its focus.

🔑 Key Property

All adaptive weighting strategies aim to find an optimal balance between tasks, but they define "balance" differently: Uncertainty focuses on task noise, GradNorm on gradient magnitudes, and DWA on relative training speeds (loss reduction rates).

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

1. Naive and Fixed Weighting

The simplest forms are direct sums. For \(K\) tasks with individual losses \(L_k\):

$$L_{total} = \sum_{k=1}^K w_k L_k$$

For naive weighting, all \(w_k = 1\). For fixed weighting, \(w_k\) are constants (e.g., \(w_k \in [0,1]\) that sum to 1, or arbitrary positive constants).

2. Uncertainty Weighting (UW)

UW frames multi-task learning as maximizing the Gaussian likelihood of a model's outputs, where each task has a learned observation noise parameter \(\sigma_k\). The total loss becomes:

$$L_{UW} = \sum_{k=1}^K \left( \frac{1}{2\sigma_k^2} L_k + \log \sigma_k \right)$$

Here, \(\sigma_k^2\) are task-dependent scalar parameters (often learned as \(\log \sigma_k^2\) to ensure positivity) that act as inverse weights. If a task is noisy (large \(\sigma_k\)), its loss \(L_k\) is down-weighted (small \(1/(2\sigma_k^2)\)). The \(\log \sigma_k\) term acts as a regularizer, preventing \(\sigma_k\) from becoming arbitrarily small to minimize the first term. The weights are effectively \(w_k = \frac{1}{2\sigma_k^2}\).

3. Dynamic Weight Averaging (DWA)

DWA uses the relative rates of loss decrease to determine weights. For each task \(k\), it calculates the "training speed" \(r_k(t-1)\) at the previous epoch \(t-1\):

$$r_k(t-1) = L_k(t-1) / L_k(t-2)$$

Then, the weights for the current epoch \(t\) are computed using a softmax-like function, with a temperature parameter \(T\) to control the sharpness of the weighting:

$$w_k(t) = \frac{K \exp(r_k(t-1) / T)}{\sum_{j=1}^K \exp(r_j(t-1) / T)}$$

Tasks with a higher \(r_k\) (meaning their loss is decreasing slower, i.e., \(L_k(t-1)\) is relatively large compared to \(L_k(t-2)\)) receive a larger weight, pushing the model to focus on tasks that are "struggling" more or converging slower.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

Many adaptive weighting methods (like GradNorm) rely on gradient magnitudes. If tasks have very different model architectures or shared layer depths, direct gradient magnitude comparison can be misleading. Always ensure the "unit" of comparison (e.g., gradients w.r.t. the last shared layer) is appropriate for your model's structure.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use which strategy:

  • Naive Sum: Only for very similar tasks with comparable loss scales and convergence rates. Often a bad default.
  • Fixed Weighting: When you have strong domain knowledge about task importance, or if you've exhaustively tuned weights and found a stable configuration. Simple to implement.
  • Uncertainty Weighting: Excellent when tasks have different inherent noise characteristics (e.g., regressing a noisy value vs. classifying a clean image). It learns to trust less noisy tasks more.
  • GradNorm: When you want to ensure all tasks contribute "equally" or "fairly" to the overall gradient updates, preventing one task's gradients from dominating. Good for balancing learning speeds.
  • Dynamic Weight Averaging (DWA): Effective for tasks with varying convergence speeds. It dynamically shifts focus to tasks that are learning slower, ensuring no task is prematurely abandoned.

General considerations:

  • Hyperparameter Tuning: Even adaptive methods often have hyperparameters (e.g., temperature \(T\) in DWA, \(\alpha\) in GradNorm) that need tuning.
  • Computational Cost: Dynamic methods add a small overhead per training step, usually negligible compared to the forward/backward pass.
  • Interactions: Task weighting can interact with other regularization techniques or optimizer settings.
💡 Big Picture

Task weighting is a crucial component of successful multi-task learning. By intelligently balancing the influence of different tasks, these strategies allow models to learn more robust, generalized representations and avoid being derailed by imbalances in task difficulty or loss scales.

for i, task_name in enumerate(task_names):
weights[task_name] = dwa_weights[i].item()
return weights
