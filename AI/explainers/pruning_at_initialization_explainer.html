Understanding Pruning at Initialization
▼

What is it? Neural network pruning aims to remove redundant connections (weights) to create smaller, faster, and more memory-efficient models. Traditionally, pruning happens *after* training. "Pruning at initialization" (also called "zero-shot pruning") takes a different approach: it identifies and removes unimportant connections from a randomly initialized network, *before* any training updates. The remaining sparse subnetwork is then trained from scratch.

Analogy: Imagine a sculptor looking at a block of marble. Traditional pruning is like carving the whole statue and then chipping away tiny bits to refine it. Pruning at initialization is like the sculptor, before even starting to carve, immediately identifying the "veins" or "fault lines" in the marble that will define the statue, and only working with those. It's about finding the essential structure from the very beginning.

💡 If you remember one thing

These methods identify critical connections based on initial weights and a single forward/backward pass (or two) on a small subset of data, *without* any actual weight updates. This is related to the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis, which posits that dense, randomly-initialized networks contain sparse subnetworks (winning tickets) that, when trained in isolation, can achieve comparable accuracy to the original dense network.

2
How SNIP & GraSP Work
▼

The Core Idea: Importance Scores from Initial State

Both SNIP and GraSP aim to assign an "importance score" to each weight \(w_{ij}\) in the network based on its initial random state and how it affects the loss for a small batch of data. Connections with low scores are deemed less critical and are pruned away.

SNIP (Single-shot Network Pruning)

SNIP (Lee et al., 2019) prunes connections that have a small *magnitude of sensitivity* of the loss to the connection. Essentially, it asks: "If I were to slightly change this weight, how much would the loss change?" If the loss changes very little, that weight is not very important for the initial forward pass. The score for a weight \(w_{ij}\) is given by:

$$S_{SNIP}(w_{ij}) = \left| \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_{ij}} \cdot w_{ij} \right|$$

This score combines the gradient (how much loss changes with weight) and the weight's initial magnitude. Connections with lower \(S_{SNIP}\) are pruned. It requires just one forward and one backward pass on a minibatch of data.

GraSP (Gradient Signal Preservation)

GraSP (Wang et al., 2020) takes a more sophisticated approach. It aims to prune connections such that the *gradient flow* through the network is maximally preserved. Instead of just looking at how a weight affects the current loss, GraSP considers how pruning a weight would affect the gradients of *other* weights, or the subsequent updates. It identifies weights whose removal least perturbs the trajectory of future gradient updates.

A common form of the GraSP score for a weight \(w_k\) involves its first and second derivatives with respect to the loss:

$$S_{GraSP}(w_k) = - \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_k} \cdot \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial w_k \partial (\text{some other weight or term})}$$

The original formulation is more complex, involving a sum over data points and Hessian-vector products. For practical implementation and intuition, it often simplifies to a term related to \(-\frac{\partial L}{\partial w_k} \cdot \nabla_{w_k} \left( \sum_{p \in \mathcal{P}} \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_p} \right)\) or similar approximations that capture how pruning affects the sum of gradients. This typically requires two backward passes.

🔑 Key Property

Both methods are "zero-shot" or "single-shot" because they determine the sparse subnetwork *without any training updates*. They leverage the initial random state of the weights and the local geometry of the loss function (gradients, or second-order information) to identify potentially critical connections.

GraSP: Preserving Gradient Flow

GraSP's objective is to maintain the "gradient flow" or the sum of gradients over all parameters, \(\sum_k \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_k}\), after pruning. If a weight \(w_j\) is pruned, the gradients of other weights \(w_i\) might change. GraSP seeks to minimize the total change in gradients, which can be approximated using a Taylor expansion of the gradients themselves. Let \(g_k = \frac{\partial L}{\partial w_k}\). The change in \(g_k\) due to pruning \(w_j\) is \(\Delta g_k \approx \frac{\partial g_k}{\partial w_j} (-w_j)\). GraSP wants to minimize \(\sum_k g_k \Delta g_k\).

Weights that, if removed, would cause a large change in the total gradient signal are preserved. It essentially prioritizes weights that are "active" and "influential" for maintaining the overall gradient dynamics.

⚠️ Computational Cost

While "zero-shot," both SNIP and especially GraSP are computationally more expensive than simple magnitude pruning. SNIP requires one backward pass. GraSP, due to its second-order nature, typically requires two backward passes (or one backward pass to compute gradients, and another to compute Hessian-vector products), making it more demanding for very large models.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use pruning at initialization:

  • When you need to train a sparse model from scratch for efficiency (memory, speed) but want a principled way to select the initial topology.
  • To explore the "Lottery Ticket Hypothesis" and find effective subnetworks early in the training pipeline.
  • For very large models where training a dense model to convergence for traditional pruning is too expensive.
  • When you want to avoid the "retraining from scratch" phase of LTH, as these methods can sometimes find tickets that perform well even without it (though LTH typically involves resetting to original initial weights).

When NOT to use:

  • If computational overhead for score calculation is a concern (especially GraSP) compared to simpler magnitude pruning (which is post-training).
  • If the model is already small or underfitting, as pruning will likely degrade performance.
  • If the chosen sparsity level is too high, leading to a "dead" subnetwork that cannot learn effectively.
  • If the initial random weights are pathological, as these methods rely on the initial gradient landscape.
💡 Big Picture

Pruning at initialization methods offer a powerful way to discover efficient network architectures before committing significant compute to training. They provide a theoretical and empirical bridge between the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis and practical sparse training, enabling the creation of "born sparse" networks that can be highly performant.

Code Trace (PyTorch-like Pseudocode)
▼
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F
# Assume 'model' is a randomly initialized nn.Module
# 'dataloader' provides a small batch of data (X_batch, y_batch)
# 'sparsity_ratio' is the fraction of weights to prune (e.g., 0.5 for 50%)
def calculate_snip_scores(model, X_batch, y_batch):
model.zero_grad()
output = model(X_batch)
loss = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(output, y_batch.float())
loss.backward()
snip_scores = []
for name, param in model.named_parameters():
if 'weight' in name: # Only prune weights, not biases
score = torch.abs(param.grad * param) # |dL/dw * w|
snip_scores.append(score.flatten())
return torch.cat(snip_scores)
def calculate_grasp_scores(model, X_batch, y_batch):
# Step 1: Compute initial gradients and store them
model.zero_grad()
output = model(X_batch)
loss = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(output, y_batch.float())
loss.backward(create_graph=True) # Important: retain graph for second backward
initial_grads = {name: param.grad.clone() for name, param in model.named_parameters() if 'weight' in name and param.grad is not None}
# Step 2: Compute second-order gradients (sum of all gradients)
# This is a proxy for the Hessian-vector product H * 1
# Sum of all first-order gradients w.r.t. loss
sum_of_grads = sum(initial_grads[name].sum() for name in initial_grads)
model.zero_grad() # Clear previous gradients for second backward pass
sum_of_grads.backward()
grasp_scores = []
for name, param in model.named_parameters():
if 'weight' in name and param.grad is not None:
# score = - (initial_grad * second_grad)
score = - initial_grads[name] * param.grad # param.grad now holds d(sum_grads)/dw
grasp_scores.append(score.flatten())
return torch.cat(grasp_scores)
def prune_weights(model, scores, sparsity_ratio):
all_scores = scores.abs()
num_weights = all_scores.numel()
num_prune = int(num_weights * sparsity_ratio)
threshold = torch.topk(all_scores, num_prune, largest=False).values[-1]
mask = {}
current_idx = 0
for name, param in model.named_parameters():
if 'weight' in name:
layer_scores = all_scores[current_idx : current_idx + param.numel()].reshape(param.shape)
mask[name] = (layer_scores >= threshold).float()
current_idx += param.numel()
return mask
