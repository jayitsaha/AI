Understanding LDA
▼

What is it? Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) finds the best straight-line direction to project high-dimensional data so that classes become as separated as possible on that 1-D number line. It simultaneously does two things: classification and dimensionality reduction, in a single mathematically principled step.

Analogy: Imagine two overlapping clouds of blue and red points on a table. You need to cast a shadow of them onto a single ruler on the wall. Most angles of the ruler will make the shadows overlap heavily and you can't tell which shadow belongs to which cloud. LDA finds the perfect ruler angle where the two shadows are furthest apart relative to how spread out each shadow is individually. The ratio "separation between clouds / spread within each cloud" is exactly Fisher's criterion.

Key assumption: Both classes share the same covariance matrix \(\Sigma\). This forces a linear decision boundary (hyperplane). Relax that assumption and you get QDA (Quadratic Discriminant Analysis).

💡 If you remember one thing

The LDA projection direction is exactly \(\mathbf{w} \propto S_W^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)\) — the inverse within-class scatter times the mean difference. This is simultaneously the Fisher discriminant, the maximum likelihood direction under equal Gaussians, and the direction that maximises $$J(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{\mathbf{w}^\top S_B \mathbf{w}}{\mathbf{w}^\top S_W \mathbf{w}}$$

2
How LDA Works Internally
▼

Step 1 — Compute class statistics

Given \(n_k\) samples per class \(k\), compute the class means \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k = \frac{1}{n_k}\sum_{i \in C_k} \mathbf{x}_i\) and the within-class scatter matrix:

$$S_W = \sum_k \sum_{i \in C_k} (\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k)(\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k)^\top$$

And the between-class scatter matrix (two-class case):

$$S_B = (\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)^\top$$

Step 2 — Maximise Fisher's criterion

We want direction \(\mathbf{w}\) such that the projected class means are far apart (large \(\mathbf{w}^\top S_B \mathbf{w}\)) while projected within-class spread is small (small \(\mathbf{w}^\top S_W \mathbf{w}\)). This is the generalised Rayleigh quotient:

$$J(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{\mathbf{w}^\top S_B \mathbf{w}}{\mathbf{w}^\top S_W \mathbf{w}}$$

Step 3 — Closed-form solution

Setting \(\nabla_\mathbf{w} J = 0\) gives the generalised eigenvalue problem \(S_B \mathbf{w} = \lambda S_W \mathbf{w}\). For two classes, \(S_B\mathbf{w}\) always points in the direction \((\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)\), so the solution simplifies to:

$$\mathbf{w}^* = S_W^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)$$

Step 4 — Classify

Project a new point: \(z = \mathbf{w}^{*\top}\mathbf{x}\). Compare \(z\) against threshold \(\theta = \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{w}^{*\top}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_0 + \boldsymbol{\mu}_1)\). Assign class 1 if \(z > \theta\), class 0 otherwise.

🔑 Key Property — Shared Covariance → Linear Boundary

Under the generative model \(\mathbf{x}|C_k \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \Sigma)\) with shared \(\Sigma\), the log-posterior ratio \(\log \frac{P(C_1|\mathbf{x})}{P(C_0|\mathbf{x})}\) is a linear function of \(\mathbf{x} — exactly the LDA projection. If instead each class has its own \(\Sigma_k\), the log-ratio becomes quadratic, giving QDA and a curved boundary.

⚠️ LDA Assumptions

1. Gaussian classes: Each class is multivariate Gaussian.
2. Equal covariance: All classes share the same \(\Sigma\) (pooled within-class scatter).
3. Full-rank \(S_W\): Requires \(n > d\) (more samples than features); otherwise use regularised LDA.
Violating assumption 1 or 2 means QDA or a kernel method will outperform LDA.

3
Why It Works — Full Derivation
▼

Derivation of the Optimal Direction

We maximise \(J(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{\mathbf{w}^\top S_B \mathbf{w}}{\mathbf{w}^\top S_W \mathbf{w}}\). Take the gradient and set to zero:

$$\nabla_\mathbf{w} J = \frac{2 S_B \mathbf{w} \cdot (\mathbf{w}^\top S_W \mathbf{w}) - 2 S_W \mathbf{w} \cdot (\mathbf{w}^\top S_B \mathbf{w})}{(\mathbf{w}^\top S_W \mathbf{w})^2} = 0$$

Numerator zero: \(S_B \mathbf{w} (\mathbf{w}^\top S_W \mathbf{w}) = S_W \mathbf{w} (\mathbf{w}^\top S_B \mathbf{w})\). Let \(\lambda = J(\mathbf{w})\):

$$S_B \mathbf{w} = \lambda S_W \mathbf{w} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad S_W^{-1} S_B \mathbf{w} = \lambda \mathbf{w}$$

For two classes, \(S_B = (\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)^\top\), so \(S_B \mathbf{w} = (\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)[(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)^\top \mathbf{w}]\). The scalar bracket doesn't affect direction:

$$\mathbf{w}^* \propto S_W^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)$$

Probabilistic Derivation (Bayes Optimal)

Under the generative model \(P(\mathbf{x}|C_k) = \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \Sigma)\) with equal priors, Bayes' rule gives:

$$\log \frac{P(C_1|\mathbf{x})}{P(C_0|\mathbf{x})} = (\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)^\top \Sigma^{-1} \mathbf{x} - \frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1^\top \Sigma^{-1} \boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0^\top \Sigma^{-1} \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)$$

Classify as class 1 if log-ratio > 0. The weight vector is \(\Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)\) — identical to the Fisher solution (with \(S_W / (n_0 + n_1 - 2)\) estimating \(\Sigma\)). LDA is Bayes-optimal under its assumptions.

Numerical Trace (our example)

With \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_0 = [1.28, 0.95]^\top\), \(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 = [4.13, 3.18]^\top\), pooled \(S_W = \begin{bmatrix}37.68 & 8.09 \\ 8.09 & 27.50\end{bmatrix}\):

$$S_W^{-1} = \frac{1}{37.68\times27.50 - 8.09^2}\begin{bmatrix}27.50 & -8.09 \\ -8.09 & 37.68\end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix}0.0289 & -0.0085\\ -0.0085 & 0.0396\end{bmatrix}$$

\(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0 = [2.85, 2.23]^\top\). Then:

$$\mathbf{w}^* = S_W^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0) \approx \begin{bmatrix}0.0289\times2.85 - 0.0085\times2.23 \\ -0.0085\times2.85 + 0.0396\times2.23\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0.0632 \\ 0.0640\end{bmatrix}$$

Normalised: \(\hat{\mathbf{w}} \approx [0.702, 0.712]^\top\). Fisher J(w*) = 0.317, vs J(w_bad) = 0.009 — a 37× improvement in class separation.

📐 Key Formulas

Fisher criterion (maximise): \(J(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{\mathbf{w}^\top S_B \mathbf{w}}{\mathbf{w}^\top S_W \mathbf{w}}\)

Optimal direction: \(\mathbf{w}^* = S_W^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)\)

Decision threshold: \(\theta = \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{w}^{*\top}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_0 + \boldsymbol{\mu}_1)\)

🎯 Decision Guide

Use LDA when: classes are approximately Gaussian, covariances are similar, you also want dimensionality reduction (use as preprocessing before other classifiers), or interpretability matters.
Use QDA when: classes clearly have different covariance shapes.
Use Logistic Regression when: the Gaussian assumption is questionable but you still want a linear boundary.
Use SVM when: margin maximisation is the priority or you need a kernel for non-linear boundaries.

6
Key Takeaways & Interview Fast-Facts
▼

What to remember

LDA maximises Fisher's criterion \(J(\mathbf{w})=\frac{\mathbf{w}^\top S_B \mathbf{w}}{\mathbf{w}^\top S_W \mathbf{w}}\). The closed-form solution is \(\mathbf{w}^* = S_W^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)\). With \(K\) classes there are \(\min(K-1, d)\) discriminant directions — LDA is a dimensionality reduction technique, not just a classifier.

Common interview traps

Q: How does LDA differ from PCA? — PCA maximises variance (unsupervised), LDA maximises class separation (supervised). PCA ignores class labels; LDA uses them.

Q: Why is the decision boundary linear? — Because the log-posterior ratio \(\log P(C_1|\mathbf{x})/P(C_0|\mathbf{x})\) is linear in \(\mathbf{x}\) when both classes share the same covariance \(\Sigma\). The quadratic terms \(\mathbf{x}^\top\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{x}\) cancel.

Q: What happens when \(n < d\)? — \(S_W\) becomes singular (rank deficient). Fix: PCA first, then LDA on reduced space; or use Regularised LDA: \(S_W \leftarrow (1-\alpha)S_W + \alpha I\).

🚫 LDA Anti-Patterns

Do NOT use LDA when: classes are clearly non-Gaussian (heavy tails, multimodal), covariance matrices differ wildly between classes, you have far more features than samples without regularisation, or your data has severe label imbalance (class priors matter — unequal priors shift the threshold).
