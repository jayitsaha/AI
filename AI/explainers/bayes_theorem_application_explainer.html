Bayes' Theorem Application

How to rationally update beliefs in light of new evidence — with real numbers
Intermediate Classical ML · Bayesian Must Know
1
Understanding the Concept
▾

Bayes' theorem is the mathematical rule for updating a belief when you see evidence. It answers one precise question: Given that I observed X, how should I revise my probability that Y is true?

Imagine you test positive for a rare disease. Your first instinct might be to panic — "the test is 99% accurate!" But Bayes' theorem reveals something counterintuitive: if the disease is rare (say 1-in-1000), even a highly accurate test will produce mostly false positives. The theorem forces you to account for the base rate — how common the disease is in the first place.

The Core Formula

$$P(\text{Cause} \mid \text{Evidence}) = \frac{P(\text{Evidence} \mid \text{Cause}) \cdot P(\text{Cause})}{P(\text{Evidence})}$$

Or more compactly: Posterior ∝ Likelihood × Prior. The normalizing denominator \(P(\text{Evidence})\) ensures everything sums to 1.

The three components have names: Prior — your belief before seeing evidence. Likelihood — how probable is the evidence if your hypothesis is true? Posterior — your updated belief after combining both.

If You Remember One Thing

Ignoring the base rate (prior) is called the base-rate fallacy. Bayes' theorem prevents it by forcing the prior into every calculation. A 99%-accurate test on a 0.1% prevalence disease still gives ~9% true-positive rate after a positive result.

2
The Full Derivation & Components
▾

Bayes' theorem follows directly from the definition of conditional probability. If \(A\) and \(B\) are events:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \quad \Rightarrow \quad P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B) \cdot P(B)$$

By symmetry, \(P(A \cap B) = P(B \mid A) \cdot P(A)\). Equating the two expressions and dividing by \(P(B)\):

$$\boxed{P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A) \cdot P(A)}{P(B)}}$$

The denominator \(P(B)\) is computed via the law of total probability by summing over all mutually exclusive hypotheses \(H_i\):

$$P(B) = \sum_{i} P(B \mid H_i) \cdot P(H_i)$$

Medical test example (concrete numbers): Disease prevalence \(P(D)=0.001\), test sensitivity \(P(+|D)=0.99\), test specificity \(P(-|\neg D)=0.99\) so false-positive rate \(P(+|\neg D)=0.01\).

$$P(D \mid +) = \frac{0.99 \times 0.001}{0.99 \times 0.001 + 0.01 \times 0.999} = \frac{0.00099}{0.00099 + 0.00999} \approx 0.0901$$

Despite 99% accuracy, a positive result only means ~9% chance of actually having the disease. This is the base-rate effect in action.

Common Mistake

Confusing \(P(+|D)\) (sensitivity) with \(P(D|+)\) (the posterior you actually want). These are fundamentally different quantities — this confusion is called the inverse probability fallacy.

3
Spam Filter: Naïve Bayes in Practice
▾

Bayesian spam filtering applies the theorem to classify emails. Each word acts as evidence. The Naïve Bayes simplification assumes words are conditionally independent given the class:

$$P(\text{Spam} \mid w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_n) \propto P(\text{Spam}) \cdot \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(w_i \mid \text{Spam})$$

Worked example: Suppose our training corpus shows:

  • 40% of emails are spam: \(P(S)=0.4\), \(P(\neg S)=0.6\)
  • Word "free" appears in 80% of spam, 5% of ham: \(P(\text{free}|S)=0.8\), \(P(\text{free}|\neg S)=0.05\)
  • Word "meeting" appears in 10% of spam, 60% of ham

For email containing both "free" and "meeting":

$$P(S|\text{free, meeting}) \propto 0.4 \times 0.8 \times 0.1 = 0.032$$
$$P(\neg S|\text{free, meeting}) \propto 0.6 \times 0.05 \times 0.6 = 0.018$$
$$P(S|\text{free, meeting}) = \frac{0.032}{0.032 + 0.018} = \frac{0.032}{0.050} = 0.64$$

64% posterior probability of spam — the "meeting" word pulled the score down from what "free" alone would give (which would be ~91% spam). Each word updates the belief sequentially.

Sequential Updating

Bayes is iterative: today's posterior becomes tomorrow's prior. \(P_{\text{new}}(\theta) = \frac{P(\text{data}|\theta) \cdot P_{\text{old}}(\theta)}{P(\text{data})}\). This is the foundation of Bayesian updating in online learning.

Key Takeaways & When to Apply
▾
MethodWhen to UseKey Property
Bayes' Theorem (exact)Small hypothesis space, discrete eventsExact posterior; needs prior
Naïve Bayes classifierText classification, spam filteringFast; independence assumption
MCMC / Variational BayesContinuous parameters, complex modelsApproximate; scales to high-D
Frequentist hypothesis testNo prior available; regulatory contextsNo prior needed; binary decision
  • Use Bayes when you have a meaningful prior (domain knowledge, previous studies) and want a full probability, not just yes/no.
  • Avoid when prior is unknown and you'd just use a uniform prior — frequentist methods may be more honest about assumptions.
  • The base-rate effect is the #1 practical insight: rare events stay improbable even after positive tests unless the test is extraordinarily specific.
  • Related topics: Maximum Likelihood Estimation, Naïve Bayes Classifier, Prior/Posterior Distributions, Bayesian Networks.
Interview Gold

Interviewers love the medical-test example. Know that posterior ∝ likelihood × prior, what the normalizing constant does, and why a 99%-accurate test on a 0.1%-prevalence disease gives only ~9% true-positive posterior. This demonstrates you understand base rates — a common trap.
