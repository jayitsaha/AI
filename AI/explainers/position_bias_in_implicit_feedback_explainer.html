Position Bias in Implicit Feedback

Why a click means "seen AND liked" — not just "liked" — and how to un-confound the two
Intermediate Recommender Systems Causal / Counterfactual ML Examination Hypothesis
1
Understanding Position Bias
▼

What is it? Implicit feedback — clicks, watches, add-to-carts — is the fuel of modern recommenders because it's abundant and free to collect. But a click is not a vote of relevance. Users scan ranked lists top-to-bottom and rarely look past the first few results, so an item ranked #1 gets clicked far more often than an equally good item ranked #8 — simply because it was seen more. This is position bias: the confounding of "the user liked it" with "the user saw it."

Analogy: Imagine a talent show where judges only watch the first three contestants closely and skim the rest. If contestant #1 gets applause, is it because they were the best, or because they got full attention while contestant #7 was barely watched? Judging "quality" purely by applause volume would systematically favor whoever went first — regardless of actual talent.

One-sentence summary: Observed click-through rate is a product of how relevant an item is and how likely it was to be examined at its position, so raw CTR is a biased proxy for relevance unless you divide the position effect back out.

💡 If you remember one thing

The examination hypothesis factorizes every click into two independent events: $$P(\text{click}=1 \mid i, k) = P(\text{examine} \mid k)\cdot P(\text{relevant} \mid i)$$ where \(i\) is the item and \(k\) is its rank position. Only the product is observed — relevance and position are entangled until you deliberately separate them.

2
How the Examination Hypothesis Works
▼

The Generative Story of a Click

Click models such as the Position-Based Model (PBM) assume a two-stage process for every impression: first, the user's eye either lands on the slot at rank \(k\) (examination, governed purely by position) or it doesn't; second, conditional on examining it, the user clicks if and only if the item is relevant to them. Crucially, examination is assumed to depend only on rank, not on which item occupies that rank — this is the independence assumption that makes the whole framework tractable.

Modeling Examination Probability

Empirically, examination probability decays sharply with rank — eye-tracking studies (Joachims et al., 2005) and click-log analyses (Craswell et al., WSDM 2008, "An Experimental Comparison of Click Position-Bias Models") show a heavy top-heavy curve. A common parametric form is a power law: \(\theta_k = 1/k^{\eta}\), with \(\eta \approx 1\) fit from data. Position 1 might be examined 90% of the time; position 8 might be examined 10% of the time — an 9x gap that has nothing to do with quality.

Design Decision: Why Multiply, Not Add?

Clicking requires both conditions to hold — the eye must land on the slot AND the item must be judged worth clicking. That's a logical AND, which under independence becomes a product of probabilities, not a sum. This also explains why position bias is multiplicative and hence correctable by division — the core trick behind Inverse Propensity Scoring (IPS), explored below.

🔑 Key Property

Examination probability \(\theta_k\) is a propensity — exactly analogous to a treatment-assignment probability in causal inference. Recovering true relevance from confounded clicks is structurally the same problem as removing selection bias in an observational study.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Inverse Propensity Scoring (IPS)

Let \(c_i \in \{0,1\}\) be the observed click on item \(i\) shown at rank \(k(i)\), with true (latent) relevance \(r_i \in [0,1]\) and known examination propensity \(\theta_{k(i)}\). Define the IPS-corrected estimator:

$$\hat{r}_i = \frac{c_i}{\theta_{k(i)}}$$

Taking the expectation over repeated impressions of item \(i\) at rank \(k(i)\):

$$\mathbb{E}[\hat{r}_i] = \frac{\mathbb{E}[c_i]}{\theta_{k(i)}} = \frac{\theta_{k(i)} \cdot r_i}{\theta_{k(i)}} = r_i$$

The estimator is unbiased — the position effect cancels exactly, regardless of which rank the item happened to occupy. This is the same mathematical trick used in importance sampling and in causal effect estimation via inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW).

The Variance Cost

Unbiasedness isn't free. The variance of the IPS estimator is:

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat{r}_i) = \frac{r_i(1-\theta_{k(i)} r_i)}{\theta_{k(i)}^2}\theta_{k(i)} \approx \frac{r_i}{\theta_{k(i)}}$$

As \(\theta_{k(i)} \to 0\) (deep ranks, rarely examined), the estimator's variance explodes — a single lucky click on a barely-seen item gets divided by a tiny number and produces a wildly inflated relevance estimate. This is why real systems clip propensities, \(\theta_{k} \leftarrow \max(\theta_k, \tau)\), trading a small amount of bias for a large reduction in variance.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

IPS requires known propensities \(\theta_k\). In production, they're estimated — typically via randomized result swapping ("intervention harvesting") or regression-based EM over click logs. If the propensity model is wrong, IPS silently reintroduces bias while looking unbiased on paper. Always validate propensities against a small randomized-exposure experiment.

Key Takeaways
▼

When the examination hypothesis holds well:

  • Single-item scanning behavior (search results, feeds) with roughly top-down reading order
  • Stable UI layout across sessions, so position → attention mapping is stationary
  • No strong "trust bias" (users don't over-click position 1 purely because they assume the ranker is good)

When it breaks down:

  • Grid/carousel UIs where eye movement isn't strictly sequential
  • Multi-click sessions where the cascade/dependent-click assumption is more appropriate than independent PBM
  • Trust bias — users click top results more even holding true relevance fixed, which IPS with a pure examination model won't capture

How propensities are estimated in practice: randomized result swapping / intervention harvesting (swap two items across a small traffic slice and let relevance cancel out), regression-based EM over historical logs (Wang et al., 2018), or dedicated randomized-exposure experiments where a small percentage of traffic sees a randomly shuffled ranking.

💡 Where this shows up

Unbiased Learning-to-Rank (ULTR), counterfactual LTR, off-policy bandit evaluation, and any A/B test that uses click-through rate as its north-star metric all inherit this exact confound. If you rank by naive CTR, you are training a feedback loop that entrenches whatever was ranked first — regardless of whether it deserved to be there.
