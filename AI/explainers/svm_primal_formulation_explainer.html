Hard-Margin SVM: Primal Formulation

The geometric heart of support vector machines — finding the widest street between two classes
Intermediate Classical ML · SVM Quadratic Program
1
Understanding the Concept
▼

A Support Vector Machine classifier finds the hyperplane that separates two classes with the largest possible margin — the widest empty "street" between positive and negative examples. The hard-margin variant assumes the training data is perfectly linearly separable: no point lies inside the margin or on the wrong side.

Think of it like this: imagine two teams of red and blue marbles on a table, and you want to place a ruler between them. You don't just want any ruler position — you want the one that keeps the closest marbles as far apart as possible, so a new marble rolling in has the best chance of landing in the right region. The SVM finds exactly that ruler, and the closest marbles that "touch" each gutter are called support vectors.

Key Insight

Only the support vectors — the points that lie exactly on the \(\pm 1\) gutters — determine the decision boundary. All other training points are irrelevant once training is done. This gives SVMs remarkable robustness to far-away outliers.

The primal form encodes this geometrically: we choose weight vector \(\mathbf{w}\) and bias \(b\) to minimise \(\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2\), which is equivalent to maximising the margin \(\frac{2}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}\), while enforcing that every training point satisfies \(y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b) \geq 1\).

2
How It Works — The Geometry
▼

The decision hyperplane is the set of points satisfying \(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b = 0\). Two parallel "gutters" run at \(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b = +1\) and \(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b = -1\). The perpendicular distance from the origin to the positive gutter is \(\frac{|1|}{\|\mathbf{w}\|} = \frac{1}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}\), and similarly for the negative side. The full margin width is therefore \(\frac{2}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}\).

$$\text{margin} = \frac{2}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}$$

Maximising the margin is equivalent to minimising \(\|\mathbf{w}\|\), or equivalently \(\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2\) (the square makes the objective smooth and strictly convex, simplifying optimisation). The full primal program is:

$$\min_{\mathbf{w},\,b} \;\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b) \geq 1, \quad i=1,\ldots,n$$

Each constraint says: positive-class points (\(y_i=+1\)) must satisfy \(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i+b\geq 1\); negative-class points (\(y_i=-1\)) must satisfy \(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i+b\leq -1\). Geometrically this means all training points lie outside the margin gutters or exactly on them.

Feasibility Requirement

Hard-margin SVM has no solution if the classes are not linearly separable — the constraint set is empty. Soft-margin SVM (slack variables) resolves this; the kernel trick allows non-linear boundaries.

This is a convex quadratic program (convex objective, linear constraints). It has a unique global minimum. The Lagrangian dual (not shown here) is also a QP in \(n\) dual variables \(\alpha_i \geq 0\), one per training point; most \(\alpha_i\) are zero at the solution, leaving only support vectors with \(\alpha_i > 0\).

3
The Math — Derivation & KKT Conditions
▼

Form the Lagrangian by attaching multipliers \(\alpha_i \geq 0\) to each constraint:

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}, b, \boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 - \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i \bigl[y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i + b) - 1\bigr]$$

Setting partial derivatives to zero gives the stationarity conditions:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{w}} = \mathbf{w} - \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf{w}^* = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i$$
$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial b} = -\sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i y_i = 0$$

The complementary slackness (KKT) conditions state:

$$\alpha_i \bigl[y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i + b) - 1\bigr] = 0, \quad \forall\, i$$

This means either \(\alpha_i = 0\) (point is outside the margin, irrelevant) or \(y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i + b) = 1\) (point lies on the gutter — a support vector). The optimal \(\mathbf{w}^*\) is a linear combination of support vectors only.

Bias Recovery

For any support vector \((\mathbf{x}_s, y_s)\) on the gutter: \(b^* = y_s - \mathbf{w}^{*\top}\mathbf{x}_s\). In practice, average over all support vectors for numerical stability.

Substituting \(\mathbf{w}^*\) back into the Lagrangian yields the dual program:

$$\max_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i - \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_i \alpha_j y_i y_j \mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_j \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \alpha_i \geq 0,\; \sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0$$

Only inner products \(\mathbf{x}_i^\top\mathbf{x}_j\) appear — the entry point for the kernel trick (\(\mathbf{x}_i^\top\mathbf{x}_j \to k(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j)\)) to handle non-linear separability.

Key Takeaways & When to Use
▼
  • Primal objective: \(\min \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2\) — a strictly convex QP with a unique global solution.
  • Constraints: \(y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i+b)\geq 1\) place all points outside or on the margin gutters.
  • Margin width: \(\frac{2}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}\) — maximising margin is equivalent to minimising \(\|\mathbf{w}\|\).
  • Support vectors are the only points that matter; all others have zero dual weight \(\alpha_i=0\).
  • Extends naturally to soft-margin (slack \(\xi_i\)), kernels (via dual), and multi-class (OvO/OvR).
When to Use Hard-Margin SVM

Use when data is known to be perfectly linearly separable and noise-free. Otherwise prefer soft-margin SVM (\(\ell_1\) slack with hyperparameter \(C\)). For non-linear boundaries: kernel SVM with RBF or polynomial kernel.
