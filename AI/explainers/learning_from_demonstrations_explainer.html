Understanding Learning from Demonstrations
▼

What is it? Learning from Demonstrations (LfD), also called Imitation Learning or Programming by Demonstration, is a paradigm where a robot acquires a skill by watching or being guided through examples of that skill, instead of being hand-coded rule by rule or left to discover the skill through pure trial-and-error reinforcement learning (RL).

Analogy: Think of an apprentice learning a craft. A master doesn't hand the apprentice a reward function and say "figure it out" — they show the motion a few times. The apprentice imitates, and only refines through limited additional practice. LfD gives a robot that same head start: a small set of demonstrated trajectories \(\mathcal{D} = \{\tau_i\}\) replaces millions of random, possibly unsafe, trial-and-error attempts.

💡 If you remember one thing

LfD trades exploration for demonstration. Instead of a policy \(\pi_\theta\) discovering good behavior from a sparse reward signal via random search, it is seeded — or entirely constructed — from a dataset \(\mathcal{D}=\{(s_t,a_t)\}\) of expert state-action pairs: $$\pi_\theta \;\longleftarrow\; \text{learn from } \mathcal{D} \quad \text{instead of} \quad \pi_\theta \;\longleftarrow\; \text{discover via } \max_\theta \mathbb{E}_\pi[\textstyle\sum_t r_t]$$

How LfD Works Internally
▼

Where Demonstrations Come From

Teleoperation — a human drives the robot remotely with a joystick, VR controller, or a master arm. The recorded actions already live in the robot's own actuation space, so there is no embodiment gap, but it needs special hardware and dedicated operator time.

Kinesthetic teaching — a human physically grasps the robot's end-effector or limbs and guides them through the task while the robot is in gravity-compensated (backdrivable) mode. This captures the robot's own joint trajectories directly and feels natural, but is limited to what a human can physically maneuver — you can't kinesthetically teach a bimanual task solo.

Video / observation — the robot watches a human (or another robot) perform the task in raw video, the cheapest and most scalable source by far. The catch: you only observe the demonstrator's own body, not the robot's actions — a severe version of the correspondence problem discussed below.

The LfD Taxonomy — Three Families

1. Behavioral Cloning (BC) frames imitation as supervised learning: fit a policy \(\pi_\theta(a|s)\) directly to the recorded \((s,a)\) pairs by regression or classification. Simple, fast, but brittle — it never sees the states its own errors create.

2. Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) does not clone actions; it infers the reward function under which the demonstrations look (near-)optimal, then runs RL or planning against that recovered reward. Slower, but it explains why the expert acted and generalizes to states the expert never visited.

3. Trajectory / movement-primitive methods such as Dynamic Movement Primitives (DMPs) encode the demonstrated motion itself as parameters of a stable dynamical system — a reusable "motion primitive" that can be replayed, sped up, or re-aimed at a new goal with no reward recovery and no RL loop at all.

🔑 Key Distinction

Behavioral cloning replicates actions. Inverse RL replicates intent (the reward). Movement primitives replicate the shape of the motion. All three reduce the exploration burden compared with reward-only RL, but they fail in different ways when demonstrations are scarce or embodiments mismatch.

Why It Works — The Math
▼

Behavioral Cloning as Supervised Regression

$$\theta^\star = \arg\min_\theta \; \mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[\, \mathcal{L}\big(\pi_\theta(s), a\big) \,\big]$$

This is ordinary supervised learning, and it inherits its central weakness from a mismatch between training and test distributions: the policy is trained on states the expert visits, but executed on states itself visits. Small per-step errors compound because each mistake nudges the robot slightly off the expert's manifold, into a state the policy never trained on:

$$J(\pi_\theta) \;\le\; J(\pi^\star) + O(\epsilon T^2)$$

where \(\epsilon\) is the per-step error and \(T\) is the horizon — the quadratic-in-horizon covariate-shift bound from Ross & Bagnell's analysis of imitation learning.

Inverse RL — Recovering Intent

Maximum-entropy IRL models expert trajectories as approximately reward-optimal under a Boltzmann distribution over trajectory return:

$$P(\tau \mid R) = \frac{1}{Z}\exp\Big(\sum_{t} R(s_t,a_t)\Big), \qquad \pi^\star = \arg\max_\pi \; \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi}\big[R(\tau)\big]$$

\(R\) is fit so that the expert's own feature expectations are matched by the resulting optimal policy — then that same \(R\) can guide RL into states the demonstrations never covered.

Dynamic Movement Primitives — Encoding Motion Shape

$$\tau^2\,\ddot{x} = K(g-x) - D\tau\dot{x} + (g-x_0)\,f(s), \qquad \tau\dot{s} = -\alpha_s s$$

A critically-damped spring-damper pulls \(x\) toward goal \(g\); the nonlinear forcing term \(f(s) = \frac{\sum_i \psi_i(s)w_i}{\sum_i \psi_i(s)}\,s\) — a weighted sum of Gaussian basis functions \(\psi_i\) — is fit once from a single demonstration and reproduces its exact shape. Because \(f\) is scaled by \((g-x_0)\), changing the goal automatically re-scales the learned shape to a new start/goal pair — this is what makes DMPs generalize from one demonstration.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

None of these methods can exceed a fundamentally flawed or inconsistent demonstrator. BC additionally suffers if the demo set is too small to cover the states the policy will actually encounter — the classic fix is to interleave expert corrections (DAgger) or to keep learning with RL after the imitation warm start.

Key Takeaways
▼

When demonstrations help most:

  • High-dimensional continuous control (manipulation, locomotion) where random exploration almost never finds the goal
  • Real hardware, where unconstrained exploration is slow, unsafe, or wears out the robot
  • Sparse-reward tasks — a handful of demos can bootstrap behavior that pure RL might never stumble into

Where LfD alone falls short:

  • Demonstrations can be suboptimal, noisy, or inconsistent across episodes
  • Small demo sets generalize poorly to unseen states (few-shot regime) — BC especially overfits
  • Embodiment mismatch (the correspondence problem) can make literal imitation infeasible
  • BC's performance ceiling is bounded by the demonstrator's own skill unless combined with RL fine-tuning

Combining demos with RL: warm-start a policy or critic with BC/offline RL and then continue online RL fine-tuning; seed the replay buffer with demonstrations for off-policy methods (e.g. DDPGfD, DQfD); shape exploration noise to stay near the demonstrated trajectory rather than searching uniformly at random; or use the demos purely to define a reward via IRL and let RL do the rest.

💡 Hierarchy

Pure imitation (BC/DMP, zero environment interaction) ⊂ demo-guided RL (demos + limited exploration) ⊂ pure RL (reward-only, unlimited exploration). Moving right relaxes the assumption that demonstrations exist but pays for it in exploration cost; moving left pays in generalization risk when demos are scarce.
