Similarity Metrics: Cosine, Pearson, Jaccard

Three different ways to ask "how alike are these two things?" — and why the answer depends on which question you ask
Foundational Recommender Systems Vector Geometry O(n)
1
Understanding Similarity Metrics
▼

What is it? A similarity metric turns two objects — two vectors of numbers, or two sets of items — into a single score describing how alike they are. Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors, ignoring their length. Pearson correlation measures the angle between two mean-centered vectors, so it ignores each vector's overall baseline too. Jaccard similarity throws away numbers entirely and asks: of everything either side touched, what fraction did both touch?

Analogy: Imagine two movie critics, Alice and Bob. Alice hands out big enthusiastic scores, Bob is a harsher grader who scores everything a few points lower. If you compare their raw scores as vectors pointing out from zero, Bob's "harsh but consistent" vector points in a noticeably different direction than Alice's — cosine similarity dips even though they agree on which movies are better than which. Pearson fixes this by first asking each critic "what's YOUR average score?" and comparing deviations from that personal average instead. Jaccard is a different animal altogether — instead of asking "did you like the same movies," it asks "did you watch the same movies," using only membership in a set.

💡 If you remember one thing

All three metrics are really about direction, not magnitude. Cosine compares raw directions. Pearson compares directions after removing each vector's own mean — which is exactly why $$r_{A,B} = \cos(A-\bar A,\; B-\bar B)$$ Jaccard skips vectors altogether and compares directions of a different kind: which coordinates are "on" at all.

2
How Each Metric Works Internally
▼

Cosine Similarity

Treat each user's ratings as a vector in item-space. Cosine similarity is the cosine of the angle \(\theta\) between the two vectors:

$$\cos(A,B) = \frac{A \cdot B}{\|A\|\,\|B\|} = \frac{\sum_i A_iB_i}{\sqrt{\sum_i A_i^2}\sqrt{\sum_i B_i^2}}$$

It ranges from \(-1\) (opposite directions) to \(1\) (same direction), and is completely blind to how long the vectors are — doubling every rating doesn't change it at all. It is not blind to a constant shift, though: adding the same number to every component of a vector moves its direction relative to the origin.

Pearson Correlation

Pearson first subtracts each vector's own mean, then takes cosine similarity of what's left:

$$r_{A,B} = \frac{\sum_i (A_i-\bar A)(B_i-\bar B)}{\sqrt{\sum_i (A_i-\bar A)^2}\sqrt{\sum_i (B_i-\bar B)^2}} = \cos\big(A-\bar A,\,B-\bar B\big)$$

Because it operates on deviations from each user's own average, Pearson is immune to any additive rating bias — a "generous" rater and a "harsh" rater with the same taste get \(r=1\), even though their raw cosine similarity would be lower.

Jaccard Similarity

For two sets \(S_A, S_B\) (e.g. the movies each user has watched, clicked, or purchased — no rating values needed):

$$J(S_A,S_B) = \frac{|S_A \cap S_B|}{|S_A \cup S_B|}$$

Jaccard is the natural metric for implicit feedback (clicks, views, purchases) where you only know "did they interact with it," not "how much did they like it."

🔑 Key Property

Cosine is invariant to positive scaling. Pearson is invariant to positive scaling AND additive shift. Jaccard doesn't operate on numbers at all — it's invariant to everything except set membership. Each relaxation answers a different real-world question.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Cosine from the Dot Product Definition

The geometric definition of the dot product is \(A\cdot B = \|A\|\|B\|\cos\theta\). Solving for \(\cos\theta\) directly gives the cosine similarity formula — it literally is the cosine of the angle between the vectors, nothing more exotic.

Pearson = Cosine After Centering

Recall the covariance-based definition of Pearson's \(r\):

$$r_{A,B} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(A,B)}{\sigma_A\sigma_B} = \frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_i(A_i-\bar A)(B_i-\bar B)}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_i(A_i-\bar A)^2}\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_i(B_i-\bar B)^2}}$$

The \(\frac{1}{n}\) factors cancel top and bottom, leaving exactly the cosine-similarity formula applied to the centered vectors \(A' = A-\bar A\) and \(B'=B-\bar B\). This is the whole trick: Pearson correlation is nothing but cosine similarity computed in mean-centered coordinates. In recommender-systems literature this centered version of cosine even has its own name: adjusted cosine similarity, and it is defined to be identical to Pearson.

Why Jaccard Isn't "Cosine on 0/1 Vectors" (Quite)

If you encode set membership as a binary vector (\(1\) = interacted, \(0\) = didn't), cosine similarity of those binary vectors is \(\frac{|S_A\cap S_B|}{\sqrt{|S_A|\,|S_B|}}\) — close to Jaccard's \(\frac{|S_A\cap S_B|}{|S_A\cup S_B|}\) but not equal, because the denominators differ (geometric mean of sizes vs. size of the union). Jaccard penalizes a large union more directly, which is why it's the standard for measuring pure set overlap.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

Using raw cosine similarity on explicit ratings without centering silently penalizes users who simply rate on a different scale than others — even if their taste is identical. This is one of the most common recommender-systems bugs. If ratings carry a per-user bias, center first (i.e. use Pearson / adjusted cosine), not raw cosine.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use which:

  • Explicit star ratings where users have different "generosity" baselines → Pearson (or adjusted cosine)
  • Dense embeddings from a trained model (e.g. item2vec, sentence embeddings) → cosine, since magnitude is often meaningless and there's no per-user bias to remove
  • Binary implicit signals (clicked / didn't, bought / didn't) → Jaccard, since there are no magnitudes to compare in the first place
  • Very sparse, high-dimensional binary data (e.g. tag sets, market baskets) → Jaccard again — it naturally penalizes small overlaps relative to large unions
💡 The One-Sentence Version

Cosine asks "same direction?", Pearson asks "same pattern, ignoring each side's personal baseline?", and Jaccard asks "same membership, ignoring magnitude entirely?" — pick the metric that matches the question your data can actually answer.

Code Trace
▼
import numpy as np
# Alice & Bob's affinity scores for [Inception, Titanic, Matrix]
A = np.array([9, 4, 8])
B = np.array([5, 0, 4]) # Bob: harsher grader, same taste
# --- Cosine similarity (raw) ---
cos_raw = A.dot(B) / (np.linalg.norm(A) * np.linalg.norm(B))
# --- Pearson correlation = cosine of centered vectors ---
A_c = A - A.mean()
B_c = B - B.mean()
pearson = A_c.dot(B_c) / (np.linalg.norm(A_c) * np.linalg.norm(B_c))
# identical to: np.corrcoef(A, B)[0, 1]
# --- Jaccard similarity on watched-movie sets ---
alice_watched = {"Inception","Titanic","Matrix","Interstellar"}
bob_watched = {"Inception","Matrix","Interstellar","Joker","Dune"}
jaccard = len(alice_watched & bob_watched) / len(alice_watched | bob_watched)
print(f"cosine = {cos_raw:.4f}") # 0.9478 — penalized by Bob's bias
print(f"pearson = {pearson:.4f}") # 1.0000 — bias removed
print(f"jaccard = {jaccard:.4f}") # 0.5000 — pure set overlap
