From Two-Stream Networks to Vision Transformers for Temporal Dynamics

Understanding Action Recognition
▼

What is it? Action recognition is the task of identifying and classifying human activities or events within video sequences. This goes beyond simple object detection (what is in the frame?) to understanding dynamic processes (what is happening?). It's a fundamental problem in computer vision with applications in surveillance, human-computer interaction, sports analytics, and autonomous systems.

Why is it hard? Unlike static images, videos introduce a crucial third dimension: time. This means models must learn not only spatial features (e.g., body posture, objects present) but also temporal dynamics (e.g., movement patterns, sequence of events). Challenges include:

  • Spatiotemporal context: Actions are defined by how objects and people move and interact over time.
  • Long-range dependencies: Crucial cues might appear far apart in time (e.g., preparing for a jump vs. landing).
  • Appearance vs. Motion: Distinguishing between a static pose and a dynamic action requires careful integration of visual cues.
  • Viewpoint, scale, and occlusion variations: People perform actions differently, and cameras capture them from various angles.

Analogy: Imagine you're trying to understand a story. If you only see individual pictures (frames), you might identify characters and objects, but you won't grasp the plot. To understand the story, you need to see the sequence of pictures, how they change, and the relationships between them over time. Action recognition is like watching the movie, not just flipping through a photo album.

💡 If you remember one thing

The core challenge in action recognition is effectively modeling both spatial information (what is where in each frame) and temporal information (how things change across frames) to capture the full spatiotemporal dynamics of an action.

2
How Action Recognition Models Evolved
▼

Early approaches often relied on hand-crafted features or extended 2D image CNNs by processing frames independently and then aggregating features. However, these struggled to capture true temporal dynamics. The field has evolved through several key architectural innovations:

1. Two-Stream Networks (2014)

Pioneered by Simonyan and Zisserman, this architecture uses two parallel convolutional neural networks: one for processing individual RGB frames (capturing appearance) and another for processing optical flow fields (capturing motion). The outputs of these two streams are then fused (e.g., by averaging softmax scores) to make a final prediction. This explicitly separates and models appearance and motion cues.

  • Pros: Effectively leverages both static visual content and dynamic motion information.
  • Cons: Requires computationally expensive pre-computation of optical flow.

2. 3D Convolutional Networks (C3D, I3D, 2015-2017)

Instead of separate streams, 3D CNNs extend 2D convolutional filters into the temporal dimension, allowing them to learn spatiotemporal features directly from raw video clips. A 3D kernel slides across height, width, and time. I3D (Inflated 3D ConvNet) specifically inflates 2D ImageNet pre-trained models into 3D, leveraging strong image features.

  • Pros: End-to-end learning from raw video, no need for optical flow.
  • Cons: High computational cost and memory requirements due to 3D operations.

3. SlowFast Networks (2019)

Introduced by Feichtenhofer et al., SlowFast networks are inspired by the distinct processing pathways in the primate visual cortex. They consist of two pathways operating at different frame rates and spatial resolutions:

  • Slow Pathway: Operates at a low frame rate (e.g., 1 frame/sec) and high spatial resolution to capture appearance and semantic information.
  • Fast Pathway: Operates at a high frame rate (e.g., 10 frames/sec) and low spatial resolution to capture rapid motion.

Information is exchanged between the pathways, allowing for efficient and effective modeling of both slow-changing semantic content and fast-changing motion.

  • Pros: Achieves state-of-the-art performance efficiently by optimizing resource allocation for different types of temporal information.
  • Cons: Still relies on complex 3D CNNs, can be challenging to implement.

4. Vision Transformers for Video (TimeSformer, VideoMAE, 2021-2022)

Inspired by the success of Transformers in NLP and Vision Transformers (ViT) in image classification, these models adapt the self-attention mechanism to video data. TimeSformer (Time-Space Transformer) employs factorized attention (spatial attention then temporal attention, or vice-versa) over video patches. VideoMAE (Masked Autoencoder for Video) uses a masked autoencoding strategy for self-supervised pre-training on large video datasets, similar to BERT in NLP, making it highly data-efficient for pre-training.

  • Pros: Captures global spatiotemporal dependencies, highly scalable with large datasets, achieves new SOTA.
  • Cons: High computational cost of attention for long videos, requires vast amounts of data for optimal performance (though VideoMAE mitigates this for pre-training).
🔑 Key Trend

The evolution of action recognition models reflects a continuous effort to better integrate and balance spatial and temporal information, moving from explicit separation (Two-Stream) to direct 3D learning (I3D), efficient multi-rate processing (SlowFast), and finally global, flexible attention mechanisms (TimeSformer, VideoMAE).

Why It Works — The Math Behind the Models
▼

Two-Stream Fusion

The core idea of Two-Stream Networks is to train two separate CNNs and combine their predictions. If \(f_{RGB}\) and \(f_{Flow}\) are the feature extractors for RGB frames and optical flow, respectively, and \(P(C \mid X)\) is the softmax probability for class \(C\) given input \(X\), then the final prediction is a weighted sum:

$$P(C \mid V) = \alpha \cdot P(C \mid \text{RGB Stream}) + (1-\alpha) \cdot P(C \mid \text{Flow Stream})$$

where \(\alpha\) is a learned or empirically set fusion weight. This allows the network to explicitly leverage both appearance and motion cues, which are often complementary.

3D Convolution (I3D)

A 3D convolutional operation extends the 2D convolution by adding a temporal dimension. For an input feature map \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times H \times W \times C_{in}}\) and a 3D kernel \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{k_t \times k_h \times k_w \times C_{in} \times C_{out}}\), the output \((W * X)\) at position \((d, h, w)\) is:

$$(W * X)(d,h,w) = \sum_{i=0}^{k_t-1} \sum_{j=0}^{k_h-1} \sum_{k=0}^{k_w-1} W(i,j,k) \cdot X(d-i, h-j, w-k)$$

I3D uses "inflated" 3D kernels. A 2D kernel \(W_{2D} \in \mathbb{R}^{k_h \times k_w \times C_{in} \times C_{out}}\) (pre-trained on ImageNet) is inflated into a 3D kernel \(W_{3D} \in \mathbb{R}^{k_t \times k_h \times k_w \times C_{in} \times C_{out}}\) by replicating its weights along the temporal dimension and normalizing, e.g., \(W_{3D}(t,h,w) = W_{2D}(h,w) / k_t\) for all \(t \in [0, k_t-1]\). This initializes 3D filters with strong spatial feature detectors.

Transformer Attention (TimeSformer)

Video Transformers, like TimeSformer, operate on sequences of spatiotemporal patches. Each patch is flattened and projected into an embedding space. The core mechanism is multi-head self-attention. For a query \(Q\), key \(K\), and value \(V\) matrices derived from patch embeddings, the attention output is:

$$\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V$$

TimeSformer can use factorized attention (e.g., spatial attention on all patches within a frame, then temporal attention on corresponding patches across frames) or joint spatiotemporal attention, allowing flexible modeling of dependencies across both dimensions.

⚠️ Computational Cost

While powerful, 3D convolutions and especially self-attention over many video patches are computationally intensive. This often necessitates downsampling frames, reducing spatial resolution, or employing strategies like factorized attention (TimeSformer) or sparse sampling (SlowFast) to manage resources effectively.

Key Takeaways & Best Practices
▼

When to use which model type:

  • Two-Stream Networks: Excellent baseline if you need explicit motion modeling and can afford optical flow pre-computation. Good for datasets where motion is a strong differentiator.
  • I3D (3D CNNs): A solid choice for end-to-end learning from raw video. Perform well for actions with complex spatiotemporal patterns. Consider if you have sufficient computational resources and data.
  • SlowFast Networks: Ideal for scenarios requiring high accuracy and efficient handling of diverse actions (both slow semantic changes and rapid movements). Balances computational cost with performance effectively.
  • TimeSformer / VideoMAE (Transformers): State-of-the-art performance, especially with large datasets. TimeSformer excels at capturing long-range dependencies. VideoMAE is particularly strong for self-supervised pre-training on unlabeled video, making it robust and data-efficient for downstream tasks. Prefer these for cutting-edge performance and scalability.

When NOT to use:

  • Two-Stream: If real-time inference is critical and optical flow computation is a bottleneck.
  • I3D: On very limited datasets, as 3D CNNs have many parameters and can overfit. Also, if computational resources are severely constrained.
  • Transformers: If your dataset is very small and you cannot leverage large-scale pre-training. Transformers generally require more data than CNNs to learn robust representations from scratch.
💡 General Recommendation

For new projects, start with a pre-trained SlowFast or a Transformer-based model (like VideoMAE for pre-training, then fine-tuning). These models generally offer the best balance of performance, flexibility, and scalability for modern action recognition tasks.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
# Example: Two-Stream Network (conceptual)
class TwoStreamNet(nn.Module):
def __init__(self, num_classes=10):
super().__init__()
self.rgb_stream = nn.Sequential(nn.Conv2d(3, 64, 3), nn.ReLU(), nn.MaxPool2d(2), nn.AdaptiveAvgPool2d((1,1)))
self.flow_stream = nn.Sequential(nn.Conv2d(2, 64, 3), nn.ReLU(), nn.MaxPool2d(2), nn.AdaptiveAvgPool2d((1,1))) # 2 channels for optical flow (dx, dy)
self.classifier = nn.Linear(128, num_classes)
def forward(self, rgb_frames, flow_frames):
# rgb_frames: (B, T, C, H, W), flow_frames: (B, T, 2, H, W)
# Simplified: average features over time, then concatenate
rgb_features = self.rgb_stream(rgb_frames.mean(dim=1)).squeeze()
flow_features = self.flow_stream(flow_frames.mean(dim=1)).squeeze()
fused_features = torch.cat((rgb_features, flow_features), dim=1)
return self.classifier(fused_features)
# Example: I3D (conceptual 3D Conv layer)
class I3DBlock(nn.Module):
def __init__(self, in_channels, out_channels):
super().__init__()
# A 3D convolution kernel processes (D, H, W) simultaneously
self.conv3d = nn.Conv3d(in_channels, out_channels, kernel_size=(3,3,3), padding=(1,1,1))
self.relu = nn.ReLU()
def forward(self, x):
# x: (B, C, D, H, W) - Batch, Channels, Depth (Time), Height, Width
return self.relu(self.conv3d(x))
# Example: TimeSformer (conceptual patch embedding + attention)
class TimeSformerBlock(nn.Module):
def __init__(self, embed_dim, num_heads):
super().__init__()
self.norm = nn.LayerNorm(embed_dim)
# Factorized attention: spatial then temporal
self.spatial_attn = nn.MultiheadAttention(embed_dim, num_heads)
self.temporal_attn = nn.MultiheadAttention(embed_dim, num_heads)
self.mlp = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(embed_dim, embed_dim*4), nn.GELU(), nn.Linear(embed_dim*4, embed_dim))
def forward(self, x_patches):
# x_patches: (T*S, B, E) - Time*Spatial_patches, Batch, Embedding_dim
# Assume T=num_frames, S=num_spatial_patches_per_frame
T_GLOBAL_FOR_DEMO = 5 # Placeholder for actual T
S = x_patches.shape[0] // T_GLOBAL_FOR_DEMO
x_spatial = x_patches.view(T_GLOBAL_FOR_DEMO, S, -1, x_patches.shape[-1]).permute(1,0,2,3).reshape(S, -1, x_patches.shape[-1])
attn_spatial, _ = self.spatial_attn(x_spatial, x_spatial, x_spatial)
# Reshape back and then for temporal attention: (T, B*S, E)
x_temporal = attn_spatial.view(S, T_GLOBAL_FOR_DEMO, -1, x_patches.shape[-1]).permute(1,0,2,3).reshape(T_GLOBAL_FOR_DEMO, -1, x_patches.shape[-1])
attn_temporal, _ = self.temporal_attn(x_temporal, x_temporal, x_temporal)
# Final output (T*S, B, E)
out = attn_temporal.view(T_GLOBAL_FOR_DEMO, S, -1, x_patches.shape[-1]).permute(0,1,2,3).reshape(T_GLOBAL_FOR_DEMO*S, -1, x_patches.shape[-1])
return out + self.mlp(self.norm(out))
