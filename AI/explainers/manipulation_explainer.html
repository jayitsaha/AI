Understanding Manipulation
▼

What is it? Manipulation is the problem of using an end-effector (a parallel-jaw gripper, a suction cup, or a multi-fingered hand) to physically change the state of an object — pick it up, reorient it, insert it, or use it as a tool. The core sub-problem is grasping: given an object's shape (from a camera or point cloud), find a gripper pose and finger configuration that will hold the object stably against gravity and disturbance forces.

Analogy: Picking up a pencil is trivial for you, but encodes a huge amount of implicit physics: you unconsciously choose contact points that won't let the pencil rotate out of your fingers, you squeeze just hard enough to counter friction without crushing it, and if it starts to slip you feel it and correct. A robot has none of this for free — every one of those decisions (where to touch, how hard, how to react to slip) must be computed or learned explicitly.

💡 If you remember one thing

A grasp is stable if the contact forces the gripper can apply — each limited to a friction cone — can produce any wrench (force + torque) needed to resist disturbances. This property is called force closure, and it's the mathematical backbone underneath both classical grasp planners and the training labels used by learned systems like Dex-Net and GraspNet.

2
How Grasping Works Internally
▼

Grasp Representations

A grasp needs a compact numeric description before any algorithm can search over it. The simplest is the antipodal 2-finger grasp: two contact points on the object's surface whose inward surface normals point roughly toward each other, so the fingers can squeeze along a common axis without the object twisting free. The general form is the 6-DoF grasp pose \((x,y,z,\text{roll},\text{pitch},\text{yaw})\) plus a gripper opening width — the full \(SE(3)\) pose of the end-effector relative to the object, which is what modern learned grasp detectors (GraspNet, 6-DoF GraspNet) regress directly from a point cloud.

Analytic Grasp Quality

Given candidate contacts, classical (analytic) methods check force closure: treat each contact's admissible forces as a friction cone (Coulomb friction, \(|f_t| \le \mu f_n\)), convert each boundary force into a 3D wrench \(w=(f_x,f_y,\tau)\), and ask whether the origin lies strictly inside the convex hull of these wrenches. If it does, the contacts can generate a force in any direction — the grasp resists arbitrary disturbances. The margin by which the origin is enclosed (the radius of the largest ball centered at the origin that still fits inside the hull) is the Ferrari–Canny ε-metric, a single scalar grasp-quality score.

Data-Driven Grasping: Dex-Net & GraspNet

Computing force closure analytically requires an accurate 3D mesh and is slow to search exhaustively. Dex-Net flips this around: it generates millions of candidate grasps on simulated object meshes, labels each with its analytic ε-metric, and trains a CNN (GQ-CNN) to predict grasp quality directly from a depth image — replacing an expensive geometric search with a fast forward pass. GraspNet-1Billion goes further, learning to regress full 6-DoF grasp poses end-to-end from raw point clouds, trained on a huge annotated real+synthetic dataset.

🔑 Key Property

Analytic and learned grasping are not rivals — they're stages of the same pipeline. Analytic force-closure metrics generate the training labels; the learned model amortizes that expensive geometric reasoning into a millisecond-scale prediction usable in closed-loop control.

Why It Works — The Math
▼

Friction Cone

At a frictional point contact with normal force \(f_n\) and tangential force \(f_t\), Coulomb's law bounds the tangential force:

$$|f_t| \le \mu f_n \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \theta = \arctan(\mu)$$

The set of physically realizable contact-force directions forms a cone of half-angle \(\theta\) around the inward surface normal. Any force inside this cone can be applied without the contact slipping.

Contact Wrenches & Force Closure

A unit force \(d_i\) applied at contact position \(r_i\) (relative to the object's reference point) produces the spatial wrench:

$$w_i = \begin{pmatrix} d_i \\ r_i \times d_i \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^3 \; \text{(2D: force + scalar torque)}$$

Sampling the two boundary rays of each contact's friction cone gives a finite set of primitive wrenches. The grasp achieves force closure iff:

$$\mathbf{0} \in \operatorname{int}\big(\operatorname{ConvexHull}(\{w_1,\dots,w_k\})\big)$$

i.e. the origin is strictly inside the hull — the contacts can synthesize a wrench pointing in every direction, so no external disturbance can pull the object free without violating a friction cone somewhere.

The ε-Quality Metric

Force closure is binary; the Ferrari–Canny metric turns it into a continuous score — the radius of the largest ball centered at the origin that still fits inside the (normalized) wrench hull:

$$Q_\epsilon = \max \{\, r : B(\mathbf{0},r) \subseteq \operatorname{ConvexHull}(\{\hat w_i\}) \,\} = \min_{\text{faces}} \; \operatorname{dist}(\mathbf{0}, \text{face})$$
⚠️ Common Pitfall

Force closure and a high ε-metric assume a known, rigid friction coefficient and perfect geometry. Real grasps fail from perception noise, soft/deformable objects, and unmodeled compliance — this sim-to-real gap is why modern pipelines pair analytic scoring with learned perception and closed-loop tactile feedback rather than trusting one offline number.

Key Takeaways
▼

When analytic grasp planning shines:

  • Known, rigid CAD geometry (bin-picking of manufactured parts)
  • Need for certified guarantees (force closure is a provable property)
  • Generating training labels at scale in simulation (Dex-Net's whole premise)

When learned / data-driven grasping wins:

  • Novel, unseen objects with only a depth image or point cloud — no mesh available
  • Millisecond inference needed for closed-loop, reactive grasping
  • Deformable / non-rigid objects where analytic contact models break down

Dexterous manipulation raises the stakes: a multi-fingered hand (e.g. Shadow Hand, Allegro Hand) has 16–24 joints — an action space an order of magnitude larger than a 1-DoF parallel jaw, with contact points that change during the motion (rolling, sliding, re-grasping). This makes analytic planning intractable and pushes the field toward imitation learning (cloning teleoperated demonstrations), reinforcement learning in simulation (e.g. in-hand cube reorientation), and sim-to-real transfer (domain randomization, system identification) to bridge the gap between simulated contact dynamics and messy real-world friction and compliance.

💡 Hierarchy

Antipodal 2-finger grasp ⊂ 6-DoF single-grasp pose ⊂ dexterous multi-finger, multi-contact manipulation. Each level relaxes assumptions about contact count, geometry, and gripper DoF — and correspondingly shifts the solution method from closed-form geometry toward learned, data-driven control.

a1, a2 = 3.49, 0.35 # contact 1, contact 2
mu = 0.4 # Coulomb friction coefficient
theta = np.arctan(mu) # friction cone half-angle
def contact_pos(a): return np.array([np.cos(a), np.sin(a)])
def wrench(a, force_angle):
r = contact_pos(a)
d = np.array([np.cos(force_angle), np.sin(force_angle)])
tau = r[0]*d[1] - r[1]*d[0] # 2D cross product
return np.array([d[0], d[1], tau])
