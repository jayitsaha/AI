Lazy Learning & Non-Parametric Models

K-Nearest Neighbours, instance-based memory, and why deferring all computation to query time changes everything.

Intermediate Classical ML Part III · KNN O(nd) query
1
Understanding the Concept
▾

Lazy learning means the model does nothing during training — it simply memorises every training example verbatim. All the work is deferred to query time. The K-Nearest Neighbours (KNN) classifier is the canonical example: given a new point, find the k closest stored examples and let them vote on the label.

Non-parametric does not mean "no parameters" — it means the number of effective parameters is not fixed at training time. It grows with the dataset. A linear model always has exactly \(d+1\) parameters regardless of \(n\); KNN effectively uses all \(n\) training points as parameters.

Key Insight

Lazy ≠ simple. KNN makes zero modelling assumptions about the data distribution — it is the ultimate model-free learner. That power comes with a cost: \(\mathcal{O}(nd)\) query time and \(\mathcal{O}(nd)\) memory.

Real-world analogy. Imagine a doctor who never reads textbooks but has seen every patient on record. When a new patient arrives, she looks up the 5 most similar past patients and prescribes whatever worked for them. She learns nothing up-front; all reasoning is deferred until the appointment.

The One Property That Defines KNN

The decision boundary is entirely implicit — it is never stored, never computed during training, and emerges anew each time a query arrives. This makes KNN arbitrarily flexible but memory-hungry.

2
How KNN Works Internally
▾

Training phase: store every \((x_i, y_i)\) pair. No computation. Complexity \(\mathcal{O}(1)\) per example, \(\mathcal{O}(nd)\) total memory.

Query phase for a new point \(x_q\):

  1. Compute distance \(d(x_q, x_i)\) for all \(n\) training points — \(\mathcal{O}(nd)\).
  2. Sort distances, select the \(k\) smallest — \(\mathcal{O}(n\log n)\) naïve, \(\mathcal{O}(n + k\log n)\) with partial sort.
  3. Classification: majority vote among \(k\) neighbours. Regression: mean (or distance-weighted mean).

Numerical trace with k=3. Suppose \(x_q = (2, 3)\) and training set:

$$d_{\text{Euclidean}} = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{d}(x_j^{(q)} - x_j^{(i)})^2}$$

Design Decision: Choice of k

k=1 → zero training error, high variance (memorises noise). k=n → always predicts the majority class, zero variance but max bias. Optimal k is found via cross-validation, typically \(\approx\sqrt{n}\) as a starting point.

Distance metrics matter enormously. The Euclidean metric treats all features equally, so features on different scales dominate. Always standardise features (z-score or min-max) before KNN. Alternatives: Manhattan (\(L_1\)), Minkowski (\(L_p\)), cosine similarity for text/embeddings, Hamming for categorical.

Minkowski Family

$$d_p(x,z) = \left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}|x_j - z_j|^p\right)^{1/p}$$ \(p=1\): Manhattan  |  \(p=2\): Euclidean  |  \(p\to\infty\): Chebyshev \(\max_j|x_j-z_j|\)

3
Why It Works: Theory & Math
▾

The 1-NN theorem (Cover & Hart, 1967) is the theoretical backbone. As \(n\to\infty\), the 1-NN error rate \(R^*_{1NN}\) satisfies:

$$R^* \le R^*_{1NN} \le R^*\left(2 - \frac{c \cdot R^*}{c-1}\right) \le 2R^*$$

where \(R^*\) is the Bayes error rate (minimum possible error) and \(c\) is the number of classes. This is profound: 1-NN is guaranteed to achieve at most twice the Bayes error — with no model assumptions at all.

Bias-variance in KNN. KNN's squared error decomposes as:

$$\text{MSE} = \text{Bias}^2 + \text{Variance} = \left(f(\bar{x}) - f(x_q)\right)^2 + \frac{\sigma^2}{k}$$

Small \(k\) → tiny neighbourhood → low bias (neighbour labels ≈ query label) but high variance (sensitive to noise). Large \(k\) → neighbourhood spans different regions → high bias, low variance.

Curse of dimensionality. In \(d\) dimensions, to maintain a neighbourhood of fixed volume fraction \(\epsilon\), each edge of a hypercube must span \(\epsilon^{1/d}\) of the range. For \(\epsilon=0.01, d=10\): edge \(= 0.01^{0.1} \approx 0.63\) — the "neighbourhood" covers 63% of each dimension! Distances become meaningless: the ratio of farthest to nearest neighbour converges to 1 as \(d\to\infty\).

$$\frac{\text{dist}_{\max} - \text{dist}_{\min}}{\text{dist}_{\min}} \to 0 \quad \text{as } d \to \infty$$
Curse of Dimensionality

KNN degrades rapidly in high dimensions (\(d \gtrsim 20\)). All distances become approximately equal, so "nearest" neighbours are no more informative than random points. Dimensionality reduction (PCA, UMAP) is essential before applying KNN to high-dimensional data.

Parametric vs Non-parametric: formal distinction. A parametric model assumes the data comes from a fixed functional form with \(p\) parameters (e.g., logistic regression: \(\hat{y} = \sigma(w^\top x)\), always \(d+1\) weights). A non-parametric model's effective complexity scales with \(n\) — KNN uses all \(n\) training points at inference time. The model never "compresses" the data.

Lazy vs Eager: The Fundamental Trade-off

Eager learners (decision trees, neural nets) generalise eagerly — they compress training data into a fixed hypothesis. Lazy learners defer generalisation to query time — they pay zero at train time but pay full price at query time. Neither is universally better; the choice depends on query frequency vs training frequency.

When to Use KNN
  • Small-to-medium datasets (\(n \lesssim 10^5\), \(d \lesssim 30\))
  • Online learning: new data immediately useful (just append to store)
  • Anomaly detection: distance to k-th neighbour is a natural outlier score
  • Recommendation systems (user/item similarity in low-dimensional embeddings)
  • Baseline before investing in complex models
When Not to Use KNN
  • High dimensions (\(d > 50\)) without prior dimensionality reduction
  • Large datasets where \(\mathcal{O}(nd)\) query latency is unacceptable
  • Missing values: distances undefined without imputation strategy
  • Class imbalance without distance weighting or sampling

Speedup strategies: KD-Trees (\(\mathcal{O}(d\log n)\) query, works for \(d \lesssim 20\)), Ball Trees (better for high \(d\)), Approximate Nearest Neighbours (FAISS, Annoy, HNSW — sub-linear at cost of recall).

6
Key Takeaways & Related Topics
▾
  • Lazy = zero training cost, deferred to query. Non-parametric = model complexity scales with data size, not fixed.
  • KNN achieves at most twice the Bayes error asymptotically (Cover & Hart 1967) — theoretically strong.
  • k controls bias-variance: small k → high variance; large k → high bias. Use cross-validation to tune.
  • Feature scaling is non-negotiable — distance metrics are scale-sensitive.
  • The curse of dimensionality is KNN's Achilles heel: distances concentrate in high dimensions.
  • Modern ANN libraries (FAISS, ScaNN) make KNN viable at billion-scale for embeddings.

Prerequisites: Distance metrics, probability basics, bias-variance tradeoff.
