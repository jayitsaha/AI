1
Understanding LDA Inference
▼

What is this? Latent Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) is a generative model that explains why a document collection looks the way it does: each document mixes a few hidden topics, each topic is a distribution over words. The inference problem is: given only the words we can observe, figure out the hidden topic assignments and topic-word distributions. Crucially, the exact answer requires summing over an exponentially large space — it's mathematically intractable.

Analogy: Imagine a library where books are secretly assembled by mixing chapters from K master "subject encyclopedias." You see only the final books. Inference means reverse-engineering: which master encyclopedia contributed each paragraph? Two strategies exist: (1) Variational EM — guess the answer analytically by solving a simpler approximation; (2) Gibbs sampling — randomly reassign paragraphs one at a time, trusting that after many rounds the distribution of guesses converges to the right answer.

💡 If you remember one thing

The posterior \(p(\theta, \phi, z \mid w)\) is intractable because normalizing over all topic assignments couples every document together. Variational EM replaces it with a factored approximation \(q\) and maximizes the ELBO; collapsed Gibbs integrates out \(\theta\) and \(\phi\) analytically and samples only the discrete assignments \(z\), trading bias for asymptotically exact samples.

2
Why the Posterior is Intractable
▼

LDA Generative Model (recap)

For each document \(d\): draw topic proportions \(\theta_d \sim \text{Dir}(\alpha)\). For each topic \(k\): draw word distribution \(\phi_k \sim \text{Dir}(\beta)\). For each token position \((d,n)\): draw topic \(z_{dn} \sim \text{Cat}(\theta_d)\), then word \(w_{dn} \sim \text{Cat}(\phi_{z_{dn}})\).

The joint is clean:

$$p(w,z,\theta,\phi \mid \alpha,\beta) = \prod_k p(\phi_k|\beta)\prod_d p(\theta_d|\alpha)\prod_n p(z_{dn}|\theta_d)\,p(w_{dn}|\phi_{z_{dn}})$$

Why the posterior blows up

We want \(p(\theta,\phi,z \mid w)\). By Bayes: \(p(\theta,\phi,z|w) = p(w,z,\theta,\phi) / p(w)\). But

$$p(w) = \int\!\int \sum_z p(w,z,\theta,\phi)\,d\theta\,d\phi$$

This integral is intractable: marginalizing out \(\theta\) and \(\phi\) requires evaluating a product of Dirichlet-multinomial mixtures, and the \(K^{N_d}\) sum over topic assignments grows exponentially in vocabulary and document length. There is no closed form.

🚨 The Core Difficulty

Even though each factor in the joint is conjugate (Dirichlet-Multinomial), summing over \(z\) before integrating out \(\theta,\phi\) couples all words in a document, destroying the independence that makes integration easy. This is why naive EM fails for LDA.

3
Variational EM — Mean-Field Approximation
▼

The ELBO decomposition

Replace the true posterior with a factored variational family \(q\). By Jensen's inequality, for any \(q\):

$$\log p(w) = \mathcal{L}(q) + \mathrm{KL}(q \| p(\cdot|w)) \geq \mathcal{L}(q)$$

where the Evidence Lower BOund is:

$$\mathcal{L}(q) = \mathbb{E}_q[\log p(w,z,\theta,\phi)] - \mathbb{E}_q[\log q(z,\theta,\phi)]$$

Mean-field family for LDA

Blei et al. (2003) chose a fully factored \(q\):

$$q(z,\theta,\phi \mid \gamma,\phi_q,\lambda) = \prod_d q(\theta_d|\gamma_d)\prod_{d,n}q(z_{dn}|\varphi_{dn})\prod_k q(\phi_k|\lambda_k)$$

with \(q(\theta_d|\gamma_d)=\text{Dir}(\gamma_d)\), \(q(z_{dn}|\varphi_{dn})=\text{Cat}(\varphi_{dn})\), \(q(\phi_k|\lambda_k)=\text{Dir}(\lambda_k)\).

Coordinate ascent updates (E and M steps)

E-step — for each document, update local variational parameters until convergence:

$$\varphi_{dnk} \propto \exp\!\left(\Psi(\gamma_{dk}) + \Psi(\lambda_{kw_{dn}}) - \Psi\!\left(\textstyle\sum_v \lambda_{kv}\right)\right)$$
$$\gamma_{dk} = \alpha_k + \sum_n \varphi_{dnk}$$

where \(\Psi\) is the digamma function (derivative of log-gamma).

M-step — update global topic parameters:

$$\lambda_{kv} = \beta_v + \sum_d \sum_n \varphi_{dnk}\,\mathbf{1}[w_{dn}=v]$$
🔑 Key Insight

The mean-field factorization assumes \(\theta_d\), \(z_{dn}\), and \(\phi_k\) are independent in \(q\). This is false in the true posterior (knowing \(\theta_d\) constrains \(z\)). The resulting \(q\) is a biased approximation, but the ELBO is tractable and the coordinate updates are closed-form.

⚠️ Digamma, not log

The expected log of a Dirichlet draw is \(\mathbb{E}[\log \theta_k] = \Psi(\gamma_k) - \Psi(\sum_j \gamma_j)\). Using \(\log \gamma_k\) instead of \(\Psi(\gamma_k)\) is a common implementation bug that silently produces wrong ELBO values.

4
Collapsed Gibbs Sampling
▼

Integrating out \(\theta\) and \(\phi\) analytically

Because Dirichlet is conjugate to Multinomial, we can marginalize out the continuous parameters exactly. The collapsed joint over only topic assignments \(z\) is:

$$p(z,w|\alpha,\beta) = \prod_k \frac{\Delta(n_k^{(\cdot)} + \beta)}{\Delta(\beta)} \cdot \prod_d \frac{\Delta(n_d^{(\cdot)} + \alpha)}{\Delta(\alpha)}$$

where \(n_{dk}\) = count of tokens in document \(d\) assigned to topic \(k\), \(n_{kv}\) = count of word \(v\) assigned to topic \(k\), and \(\Delta\) is the multivariate beta (ratio of gamma products).

The Gibbs update

Sample each \(z_{dn}\) from its full conditional, holding all others fixed. Remove token \((d,n)\) from counts, then:

$$p(z_{dn}=k \mid z_{-dn}, w) \propto \frac{n_{kw_{dn}}^{-dn} + \beta_{w_{dn}}}{\sum_v n_{kv}^{-dn} + \beta_v} \cdot (n_{dk}^{-dn} + \alpha_k)$$

The superscript \(-dn\) means "excluding the current token." This is just: (word \(v\) fits topic \(k\)'s word distribution) × (document \(d\) likes topic \(k\)) — intuitive and exact given the marginalized-out parameters.

Recovering \(\theta\) and \(\phi\) after sampling

$$\hat{\theta}_{dk} = \frac{n_{dk} + \alpha_k}{\sum_k n_{dk} + \alpha_k}, \qquad \hat{\phi}_{kv} = \frac{n_{kv} + \beta_v}{\sum_v n_{kv} + \beta_v}$$

These are just the posterior means of the Dirichlet distributions, computed from the final count matrices.

💡 Why Collapsing Helps

Integrating out \(\theta\) and \(\phi\) reduces the state space from continuous \(\mathbb{R}^{DK + KV}\) plus discrete \(z\) down to just the discrete assignments. The Markov chain mixes much faster — fewer parameters means less correlation between consecutive samples, and the variance of Monte Carlo estimates drops dramatically.

Tradeoffs — When to Use Which
▼
Variational EM

Pros: Deterministic — same result each run. Fast convergence (often 10–50 ELBO iterations). Lower-bound on log-likelihood for model selection. Naturally parallelizable per-document E-step. Online variant (Hoffman 2010) scales to millions of docs.

Cons: Approximation bias — mean-field underestimates posterior variance and can miss multimodal posteriors. Sensitive to initialization. ELBO can plateau at bad local optima. Hyperparameter sensitivity (needs \(\alpha\), \(\beta\) schedule).

Use when: Large corpus, need speed, online learning, or a likelihood lower bound for model comparison.

Collapsed Gibbs

Pros: Asymptotically exact — converges to the true posterior (in theory). Simple implementation (just two count matrices + multinomial draw). Collapsed space mixes faster than full Gibbs. No free variational parameters to tune.

Cons: Stochastic — requires many samples, burn-in period. Slow for very large corpora. Cannot easily parallelize (shared count matrices). No explicit bound on convergence speed; hard to diagnose mixing.

Use when: Small to medium corpus, need asymptotic correctness, or diagnostic mixing analysis matters.

📊 Complexity Comparison

Per iteration: VEM E-step is \(O(D \cdot N_d \cdot K)\) per document; M-step is \(O(K \cdot V)\). Collapsed Gibbs is \(O(N_{\text{total}} \cdot K)\) per sweep — same asymptotic order. In practice VEM converges in far fewer "passes" but each pass is deterministic. Gibbs needs ~500–2000 sweeps for stable estimates. For \(D=10^6\), only online VEM is practical.
