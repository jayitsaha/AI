Poisson Process

The canonical model of random events arriving continuously in time

1
Understanding the Poisson Process
▼

What is it? Imagine sitting by a road counting cars passing. They arrive randomly — sometimes two arrive close together, sometimes there's a long gap. If the average rate is constant and arrivals happen independently, the number of cars in any time window follows a Poisson distribution. The Poisson process is the full mathematical model describing this: how many events happen by time \(t\), how long you wait between events, and how counts in non-overlapping intervals relate.

Real-World Analogy: Think of a popcorn kernel popping at a rate of \(\lambda\) pops per second. Each kernel "decides" to pop independently of all others, and the chance of a pop in any tiny window \(\Delta t\) is approximately \(\lambda \Delta t\). The Poisson process models the sequence of pop times. The total count of pops in 10 seconds is \(\text{Poisson}(\lambda \cdot 10)\), and the waiting time until the next pop is \(\text{Exponential}(\lambda)\).

Who cares? The Poisson process is the bedrock of queueing theory (M/M/1 queues), telecommunications (packet arrivals), reliability engineering (failure events), neuroscience (spike trains), finance (trade arrivals), and spatial statistics (point processes). Any phenomenon where independent, rare events accumulate in time or space is well-approximated by a Poisson process.

💡 If you remember one thing

A Poisson process with rate \(\lambda\) has three equivalent characterizations: (1) counts in disjoint intervals are independent Poisson r.v.s, (2) inter-arrival times are i.i.d. \(\text{Exp}(\lambda)\), and (3) the infinitesimal probability of one event in \([t, t+dt)\) is \(\lambda\,dt\). All three say the same thing.

2
Three Defining Properties
▼

Axiom 1: Stationarity

The probability of \(k\) events in any interval of length \(t\) depends only on \(t\), not on where the interval starts. Formally, \(N(s+t) - N(s) \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda t)\) for all \(s \geq 0\). This says the process has no "memory of the clock" — Tuesday morning is statistically identical to Sunday evening.

Axiom 2: Independent Increments

For any non-overlapping intervals \([a,b)\) and \([c,d)\) (with \(b \leq c\)), the counts \(N(b)-N(a)\) and \(N(d)-N(c)\) are statistically independent. What happened before has no bearing on what happens next. This is the "no clustering from past events" property — critically, it implies the inter-arrival times are memoryless.

Axiom 3: Orderliness (No Simultaneous Events)

In any infinitesimally small window \([t, t+dt)\): the probability of exactly one event is \(\lambda\,dt + o(dt)\), and the probability of two or more events is \(o(dt)\) (negligible compared to \(dt\) as \(dt \to 0\)). Events arrive one at a time, never in exact simultaneous clusters.

🔑 Key Property: Memorylessness

If you've been waiting for the next event for time \(s\), the distribution of the remaining wait is still \(\text{Exp}(\lambda)\) — exactly as if you just started waiting. Formally: \(P(T > s+t \mid T > s) = P(T > t) = e^{-\lambda t}\). The exponential distribution is the only continuous memoryless distribution.

Consequence: The Count Distribution

From these three axioms alone, one can derive that the number of events in interval \([0,t]\) must be Poisson distributed:

$$P(N(t) = k) = \frac{e^{-\lambda t}(\lambda t)^k}{k!}, \quad k = 0,1,2,\ldots$$

The mean is \(\mathbb{E}[N(t)] = \lambda t\) and variance is \(\operatorname{Var}(N(t)) = \lambda t\) — a hallmark of Poisson: mean equals variance. For \(\lambda = 2\) and \(t = 3\), we get \(\mathbb{E}[N(3)] = 6\), with the mode at \(k=5\) and \(k=6\) (both equal 0.1606).

⚠️ Common Mistake

The Poisson process is a model. Real-world processes often have burstiness (over-dispersion, variance > mean) or inhibition (under-dispersion) and require Cox processes, Hawkes processes, or renewal processes instead. Always check: is the variance-to-mean ratio (index of dispersion) close to 1?

3
The Mathematics
▼

Derivation of the Poisson PMF from First Principles

Let \(p_k(t) = P(N(t)=k)\). Consider how \(p_k(t)\) changes over the infinitesimal interval \([t, t+dt)\). By orderliness and independence of increments:

$$p_k(t+dt) = p_k(t)\,(1-\lambda\,dt) + p_{k-1}(t)\,\lambda\,dt + o(dt)$$

Rearranging and dividing by \(dt\), then taking \(dt \to 0\) gives the system of ODEs:

$$p_k'(t) = -\lambda\, p_k(t) + \lambda\, p_{k-1}(t), \quad k \geq 1; \qquad p_0'(t) = -\lambda\, p_0(t)$$

Solving with initial conditions \(p_0(0)=1\), \(p_k(0)=0\) for \(k\geq1\):

Step 1: \(p_0(t) = e^{-\lambda t}\).

Step 2: For \(k=1\): \(p_1'(t) = -\lambda p_1(t) + \lambda e^{-\lambda t}\). Integrating factor \(e^{\lambda t}\): \(\frac{d}{dt}[e^{\lambda t}p_1(t)] = \lambda\), so \(p_1(t) = \lambda t e^{-\lambda t}\).

Step 3: By induction, assume \(p_{k-1}(t) = e^{-\lambda t}(\lambda t)^{k-1}/(k-1)!\). Then \(p_k(t) = e^{-\lambda t}(\lambda t)^k/k!\). \(\square\)

$$\boxed{P(N(t)=k) = \frac{(\lambda t)^k e^{-\lambda t}}{k!}}, \quad \mathbb{E}[N(t)] = \lambda t, \quad \operatorname{Var}(N(t)) = \lambda t$$

Inter-Arrival Times

Let \(T_n\) be the \(n\)th inter-arrival time (gap between event \(n-1\) and event \(n\)). Then \(\{T_n\}_{n\geq1}\) are i.i.d. \(\text{Exponential}(\lambda)\):

$$P(T_1 > t) = P(N(t)=0) = e^{-\lambda t} \implies f_{T}(t) = \lambda e^{-\lambda t}, \quad t \geq 0$$

The \(n\)th arrival time \(S_n = T_1+\cdots+T_n \sim \text{Gamma}(n, \lambda)\), with density \(f_{S_n}(t) = \lambda^n t^{n-1}e^{-\lambda t}/(n-1)!\).

Superposition and Thinning

Superposition: If \(N_1 \sim \text{PP}(\lambda_1)\) and \(N_2 \sim \text{PP}(\lambda_2)\) are independent, their merge \(N_1+N_2 \sim \text{PP}(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)\).

Thinning: If each event of a \(\text{PP}(\lambda)\) is independently kept with probability \(p\), the kept events form a \(\text{PP}(\lambda p)\). Together, the kept and deleted streams are independent Poisson processes.

📐 Moment Generating Function

The MGF of \(N(t)\) is \(M_{N(t)}(s) = \mathbb{E}[e^{sN(t)}] = \exp\!\bigl(\lambda t(e^s-1)\bigr)\). This confirms \(\mathbb{E}[N(t)]=\lambda t\) (first cumulant) and \(\operatorname{Var}(N(t))=\lambda t\) (second cumulant). All cumulants equal \(\lambda t\).

Key Connections

Queueing (M/M/1): Arrivals follow PP(\(\lambda\)), service times are Exp(\(\mu\)); the steady-state queue length is geometric with mean \(\rho/(1-\rho)\) where \(\rho=\lambda/\mu\).

Spatial Point Processes: Replace time with 2D space; the Poisson point process models uniformly random point patterns (e.g., tree locations, base stations in a wireless network).

Poisson Approximation: The Poisson distribution approximates Binomial\((n,p)\) when \(n\) is large and \(p\) is small, with \(\lambda = np\). This is the "law of rare events."
