Understanding Scaling Laws
▼

What is it? Scaling laws describe how the performance (typically test loss) of large neural networks improves predictably as more resources are invested. Specifically, they quantify the relationship between model size (\(N\), parameters), dataset size (\(D\), tokens), and training compute (\(C\), FLOPs) with the final model quality. These laws are empirical power-law relationships, discovered through extensive experimentation on large language models.

Analogy: Imagine you're building a software product. You have a budget (compute \(C\)) and two main ways to spend it: hiring more engineers (model size \(N\)) or getting more user feedback/data (dataset size \(D\)). If you hire many engineers but have no feedback, they might build the wrong thing. If you have tons of feedback but no engineers, nothing gets built. Scaling laws tell you the optimal balance between engineers and feedback to get the best product for your budget.

💡 If you remember one thing

For a given compute budget, there's an optimal allocation between model parameters (\(N\)) and training data tokens (\(D\)). Kaplan et al. (2020) showed separate power laws, while Hoffmann et al. (2022) (Chinchilla) found that for compute-optimal training, \(N\) and \(D\) should scale roughly proportionally, i.e., larger models need proportionally more data.

2
How Scaling Laws Are Derived
▼

Kaplan et al. (2020): Separate Power Laws

The first major work by Kaplan et al. (2020) systematically studied the scaling of transformer language models. They found that test loss \(L\) followed distinct power-law relationships with \(N\), \(D\), and \(C\) when the other factors were held constant:

  • \(L \propto N^{-\alpha_N}\) (loss decreases as model size grows)
  • \(L \propto D^{-\alpha_D}\) (loss decreases as dataset size grows)
  • \(L \propto C^{-\alpha_C}\) (loss decreases as compute grows)

These studies typically involved training many models of varying sizes on different amounts of data, then fitting power-law curves to the observed test loss. A key finding was that models continued to improve without saturation, simply by scaling up resources.

Hoffmann et al. (2022) (Chinchilla): Compute-Optimal Scaling

Building on Kaplan's work, Hoffmann et al. (2022) revisited the question of how to best allocate a fixed compute budget. They found that Kaplan's earlier models were often "under-trained" — they had too many parameters for the amount of data they saw. For a fixed compute budget \(C\), there exists an optimal balance between \(N\) and \(D\) that minimizes loss.

Specifically, for large language models, the Chinchilla paper suggests that for compute-optimal training, the number of training tokens \(D\) should be roughly 20 times the number of model parameters \(N\) (i.e., \(D \approx 20N\)). This means if you double your compute, you should scale *both* \(N\) and \(D\) proportionally, maintaining this ratio, rather than just scaling one factor heavily.

Why It Matters: Efficiency and Performance

The Chinchilla scaling laws have profound implications for training large models:

  • Resource Allocation: They provide a principled way to design future models, dictating how to spend a given compute budget most effectively.
  • Cost Savings: Training larger models than necessary on insufficient data is wasteful. Chinchilla showed that a 70B parameter model trained optimally could outperform a 280B parameter model trained sub-optimally, for the same compute budget.
  • Predictability: These empirical laws allow researchers to predict the performance of future, even larger models, guiding long-term research roadmaps.
🔑 Key Property

The compute required to train a transformer language model to convergence is approximately \(C \approx 6ND\) FLOPs (for a non-embedding-dominant model). This simple relationship, combined with the loss scaling laws, allows us to find the optimal \(N, D\) pair for any given \(C\).

3
Why It Works — The Empirical Formulas
▼

Test Loss Formula (Simplified Kaplan)

The test loss \(L\) can be approximated as the sum of a term dependent on model size, a term dependent on dataset size, and an irreducible loss floor:

$$L(N, D) = L_{irr} + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha_N}} + \frac{B}{D^{\alpha_D}}$$

where \(L_{irr}\) is the irreducible loss (e.g., due to noise or fundamental limits of the data), \(A\) and \(B\) are constants, and \(\alpha_N, \alpha_D\) are positive exponents. Typical values for LLMs are \(L_{irr} \approx 1.5\), \(A \approx 3.0\), \(B \approx 1.0\), \(\alpha_N \approx 0.07\), \(\alpha_D \approx 0.07\).

Compute (\(C\)) for Transformers

For a transformer language model, the dominant training compute cost (in FLOPs) is proportional to the product of parameters \(N\) and tokens \(D\):

$$C \approx 6ND$$

This empirical rule means that doubling either \(N\) or \(D\) roughly doubles the compute, while doubling both quadruples it. This relationship is crucial for understanding how to allocate a fixed budget.

Compute-Optimal Scaling (Chinchilla)

To find the compute-optimal configuration, we want to minimize \(L(N, D)\) subject to the constraint \(C = 6ND\). This can be done using Lagrange multipliers, but the empirical finding simplifies to a proportional relationship:

$$D_{optimal} \approx k \cdot N_{optimal}$$

For large language models, Hoffmann et al. found \(k \approx 20\). Substituting this into the compute equation: \(C \approx 6 N_{optimal} (k N_{optimal}) = 6k N_{optimal}^2\). Solving for \(N_{optimal}\) and \(D_{optimal}\) gives:

$$N_{optimal} \approx \sqrt{\frac{C}{6k}} \quad \text{and} \quad D_{optimal} \approx k \sqrt{\frac{C}{6k}}$$

This shows that for optimal training, both model size and dataset size should grow as the square root of the compute budget. This is the "Chinchilla optimal path" — a specific trajectory in the (N, D) space that yields the best performance for a given compute budget.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

These scaling laws are empirical observations, not fundamental physical laws. Their exact exponents and constants (\(A, B, \alpha_N, \alpha_D, k\)) can vary slightly depending on model architecture, tokenizer, and specific training setup. They provide strong guidance but are not absolute guarantees for all future models or domains.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to apply scaling laws:

  • Strategic Planning: When designing and budgeting for the next generation of large models, these laws guide the allocation of precious compute resources.
  • Performance Prediction: To forecast the achievable performance (loss) of a model given a certain compute budget, or conversely, the compute needed for a target performance.
  • Benchmarking: To understand if a model is "on the frontier" of what's achievable for its compute budget, or if it's under- or over-trained.

Limitations and considerations:

  • Empirical Nature: The laws are derived from experiments, not first principles. They might change with new architectures, data modalities, or training techniques.
  • Domain Specificity: The specific constants and exponents (\(\alpha, k\)) are found for language models. They might differ for vision, multimodal, or other domains, requiring new empirical studies.
  • Irreducible Loss: The existence of an \(L_{irr}\) means there's a limit to how good models can get just by scaling, suggesting other research avenues like algorithmic improvements.
  • Other Bottlenecks: Scaling laws focus on compute. Other factors like memory, latency, or data quality can become bottlenecks not captured by these simple formulas.
💡 Big Picture

Scaling laws have transformed large model development from an art to a science, providing a roadmap for efficiently pushing the boundaries of AI capabilities. They highlight that simply making models bigger isn't enough; balancing model capacity with sufficient, diverse data is key.

Code Trace (Python/NumPy)
▼
import numpy as np
# --- Empirical Constants (from Chinchilla paper-like observations) ---
L_IRR = 1.5 # Irreducible loss floor
A = 3.0 # N-dependent loss constant
B = 1.0 # D-dependent loss constant
ALPHA_N = 0.07 # Exponent for N-scaling
ALPHA_D = 0.07 # Exponent for D-scaling
K_CHINCHILLA = 20 # Optimal D/N ratio for Chinchilla (D = k*N)
COMPUTE_FACTOR = 6 # FLOPs = 6 * N * D for transformer training
def calculate_loss(N, D):
"""Calculates approximate test loss given N (params) and D (tokens)."""
loss = L_IRR + A * (N**(-ALPHA_N)) + B * (D**(-ALPHA_D))
return loss
def calculate_compute(N, D):
"""Calculates approximate training FLOPs given N and D."""
return COMPUTE_FACTOR * N * D
def find_chinchilla_optimal(C_budget):
"""Finds optimal N, D for a given compute budget C."""
# C = 6 * N * D and D = k * N
# C = 6 * N * (k * N) = 6 * k * N^2
N_optimal = np.sqrt(C_budget / (COMPUTE_FACTOR * K_CHINCHILLA))
D_optimal = K_CHINCHILLA * N_optimal
loss_optimal = calculate_loss(N_optimal, D_optimal)
return N_optimal, D_optimal, loss_optimal
# --- Example Usage ---
C_target = 1e22 # 10^22 FLOPs
N_opt, D_opt, L_opt = find_chinchilla_optimal(C_target)
print(f"For C = {C_target:.0e} FLOPs:")
print(f" Optimal N = {N_opt:.2e} parameters")
print(f" Optimal D = {D_opt:.2e} tokens")
print(f" Achievable Loss = {L_opt:.4f}")
