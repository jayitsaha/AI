CT, MRI & X-Ray: Modality-Specific Challenges for Deep Learning

Why Hounsfield units, k-space physics, and 2D projection geometry each break naive computer-vision pipelines
Advanced Computer Vision Medical Imaging Multi-Modal Physics
1
Understanding the Challenge
▼

What is it? CT, MRI, and X-ray are not just "grayscale photos of the inside of the body" — they are three completely different physical measurement processes, each with its own units, noise model, dimensionality, and failure modes. A deep learning pipeline built for natural images (ImageNet-style RGB, 0–255, roughly consistent lighting) breaks in specific, predictable ways on each of these modalities unless you account for the underlying physics.

Analogy: Imagine three rulers that all claim to measure "density." One ruler (CT) is perfectly calibrated against water and air, so a "40" always means the same tissue everywhere in the world. The second ruler (MRI) has no fixed zero point — a "40" on one machine might be a "400" on another, because the ruler itself is retuned for every scan. The third instrument (X-ray) doesn't even measure a single point — it measures the sum of everything the light passed through on its way to the sensor, so a bone directly behind a lung looks identical to a bone floating in open space.

Summary: CT gives you a standardized 3D density map that must be non-linearly windowed; MRI gives you a scanner- and sequence-dependent 3D map that has no absolute intensity scale at all; X-ray gives you a 2D projection that has already thrown away depth. Each modality demands a different preprocessing and modeling strategy.

💡 If you remember one thing

CT problems are mostly about range compression (windowing 12-bit HU into 8-bit contrast), MRI problems are mostly about lack of a fixed scale (per-scan/per-site normalization), and X-ray problems are mostly about lost dimensionality (2D projections superimpose 3D structure that cannot be recovered).

2
The Physics — What Each Modality Actually Measures
▼

CT: a calibrated attenuation map

CT reconstructs the linear X-ray attenuation coefficient \(\mu(x,y,z)\) at every voxel from many projection angles (filtered back-projection or iterative reconstruction), then rescales it onto the Hounsfield scale, fixed by definition at \(-1000\) for air and \(0\) for water. Because the scale is anchored to physical constants, a "50 HU" liver on a scanner in Boston means the same thing as a "50 HU" liver on a scanner in Mumbai — CT is the one modality with a genuinely standardized intensity unit. The catch: the full range spans roughly \(-1000\) to \(+3000\) (about 4096 levels at 12-bit precision), but the tissue contrast radiologists care about lives in a tiny sub-band (soft tissue differences of 20–100 HU), so naive 8-bit rescaling destroys it. CT also suffers reconstruction-specific artifacts: beam hardening (lower-energy photons are preferentially absorbed, biasing HU near dense structures), metal streak artifacts (photon starvation and scatter around implants), and quantum noise that scales with dose.

MRI: relative signal, no fixed unit

MRI measures the RF signal emitted by relaxing hydrogen nuclei after an excitation pulse, sampled in the spatial-frequency domain (k-space) and reconstructed via inverse Fourier transform. Contrast is entirely a function of the pulse sequence — repetition time (TR) and echo time (TE) — interacting with each tissue's \(T_1\) and \(T_2\) relaxation constants. Critically, there is no physical anchor analogous to water=0: the same tissue can produce wildly different raw intensities across scanners, coils, and even repeated scans of the same patient. On top of that, RF coil sensitivity and B1 field inhomogeneity introduce a smooth multiplicative bias field across the image, and multi-minute acquisition times make MRI exquisitely sensitive to patient motion, which corrupts k-space and produces coherent ghosting artifacts rather than simple blur.

X-ray: a 2D projection, not a reconstruction

Radiography never reconstructs a volume — a single exposure integrates attenuation along each ray path (Beer-Lambert law) and records one 2D image. Every tissue the beam crosses — skin, fat, muscle, rib, lung, and any pathology — is summed into a single pixel value. Bit depth is high (typically 12–16 bit / 4096–65536 gray levels) to capture the huge dynamic range from open lung to dense bone, but naive linear display compression saturates bone while crushing lung detail to near-uniform gray.

Downstream deep-learning consequences

These physical differences cascade into concrete modeling problems: (1) domain shift across scanners, reconstruction kernels, and sites, especially severe for MRI; (2) class imbalance and label scarcity, since only expert radiologists can annotate and most studies are normal; (3) patient-level data leakage when multiple slices/views from one patient land in both train and test splits; (4) 3D volumetric compute cost for CT/MRI (memory-bound 3D convolutions, patch-based training); (5) anisotropic voxel spacing (in-plane resolution often 3–10× finer than slice thickness); (6) depth ambiguity unique to X-ray, where 2D projection cannot distinguish "structure behind another structure" from "structure that isn't there."

🔑 Design decision: why not one universal preprocessing pipeline?

Because the three modalities disagree on the most basic question — what does a pixel value mean? CT pixels are absolute physical quantities (clip and window them). MRI pixels are relative and must be normalized per-scan (z-score or histogram matching) after bias-field correction. X-ray pixels are already-collapsed projections that need dynamic-range compression (log/CLAHE), not clipping. Applying CT-style windowing to MRI, or MRI-style z-scoring to X-ray, systematically destroys signal.

Why It Works — The Math
▼

CT: Hounsfield units and windowing

$$HU(x,y,z) = 1000 \times \frac{\mu(x,y,z) - \mu_{water}}{\mu_{water} - \mu_{air}}$$

Display windowing is a clipped linear map defined by window level \(WL\) (center) and window width \(WW\) (range):

$$v = \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{HU - (WL - WW/2)}{WW},\; 0,\; 1\right)$$

X-ray: the Beer-Lambert projection integral

Radiographic intensity attenuates exponentially with path length through tissue of coefficient \(\mu\):

$$I = I_0 \, e^{-\int \mu(x,y,z)\,dz} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad -\ln(I/I_0) = \int \mu(x,y,z)\,dz$$

This is exactly why CT needs many projection angles to invert \(\mu(x,y,z)\) (via the inverse Radon transform), while a single X-ray exposure only ever recovers the line integral — a fundamentally lossy, non-invertible summary of the 3D volume.

MRI: signal as a function of pulse sequence

For a spin-echo sequence, the observed signal at proton density \(\rho_0\) is:

$$S = \rho_0\left(1 - e^{-TR/T_1}\right) e^{-TE/T_2}$$

Contrast is engineered by the operator's choice of \(TR, TE\): short-\(TR\)/short-\(TE\) sequences weight by \(T_1\); long-\(TR\)/long-\(TE\) sequences weight by \(T_2\). Because \(\rho_0\) and the achieved \(TR,TE\) both vary by hardware and protocol, \(S\) is only meaningful relative to other pixels in the same acquisition — never as an absolute unit. Bias-field corruption is modeled multiplicatively:

$$S_{obs}(x) = S_{true}(x)\cdot B(x) + \eta, \qquad B(x) \text{ smooth, low-frequency}$$

Because \(B(x)\) is smooth and low-frequency while anatomical detail is high-frequency, N4-style correction estimates \(\hat B\) by low-pass filtering (in log space, so the multiplicative corruption becomes additive) and divides it out: \(\hat S_{true} = S_{obs}/\hat B\).

Class imbalance: effective number of samples

Rare-pathology classes are reweighted using the effective-number formulation (Cui et al., 2019), which interpolates between "weight ∝ 1/n" (small n) and uniform weighting (large n) via a decay hyperparameter \(\beta \in [0,1)\):

$$E_n = \frac{1-\beta^{n}}{1-\beta}, \qquad w_{class} \propto \frac{1}{E_n}$$
⚠️ Common pitfall

Treating HU values as if they were already display-ready 8-bit pixels (skipping windowing) silently destroys the exact soft-tissue contrast a model needs. Conversely, treating MRI intensities as absolute (skipping per-scan normalization) means a model trained on Site A's 1.5T scanner can fail catastrophically on Site B's 3T scanner — even though the anatomy and pathology are identical. Neither failure shows up as a crash; both show up as silently degraded validation metrics that don't transfer.

When each challenge dominates:

  • Building a CT pipeline? Get the windowing (or multi-window / raw-HU-as-channel) strategy right first — it matters more than architecture choice.
  • Building an MRI pipeline? Invest in bias-field correction and per-scan/per-site harmonization before touching the model; raw intensities are not comparable across studies.
  • Building an X-ray pipeline? Accept the 2D depth ambiguity as a ceiling on achievable accuracy for depth-dependent findings, and prioritize dynamic-range-preserving preprocessing (log compression) over naive clipping.
💡 Hierarchy

All three modalities share the same second-order problems — class imbalance, patient-level leakage, and label scarcity — but only after the modality-specific first-order physics (windowing, bias field, projection geometry) has been handled correctly. Fixing the second-order problems on top of broken first-order preprocessing just produces a model that is imbalanced-aware but still blind to half its input signal.

Code Trace — From-Scratch Preprocessing
▼
import numpy as np
# ── CT: Hounsfield-unit windowing ──
def hu_window(hu, wl, ww):
lo, hi = wl - ww / 2, wl + ww / 2
return np.clip((hu - lo) / (hi - lo), 0.0, 1.0)
# ── MRI: N4-style multiplicative bias-field correction ──
def n4_correct(img, sigma=8.0):
from scipy.ndimage import gaussian_filter
log_img = np.log1p(np.clip(img, 1e-6, None))
low_freq = gaussian_filter(log_img, sigma=sigma) # smooth bias estimate
corrected = np.expm1(log_img - low_freq + low_freq.mean())
return corrected
# ── X-ray: Beer-Lambert projection of a CT-like volume ──
def beer_lambert_projection(mu_volume, dz=0.5):
line_integral = mu_volume.sum(axis=0) * dz # sum over depth (z)
return line_integral # == -ln(I / I0)
# ── Patient-level split (prevents leakage across slices/views) ──
def patient_level_split(df, patient_col="patient_id", test_frac=0.2, seed=0):
patients = df[patient_col].unique()
rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
rng.shuffle(patients)
n_test = int(len(patients) * test_frac)
test_ids = set(patients[:n_test])
return df[~df[patient_col].isin(test_ids)], df[df[patient_col].isin(test_ids)]
# ── Class-balanced loss weights (effective number of samples) ──
def class_balanced_weights(counts, beta=0.999):
counts = np.asarray(counts, dtype=np.float64)
effective_num = (1.0 - np.power(beta, counts)) / (1.0 - beta)
weights = 1.0 / effective_num
return weights / weights.sum() * len(counts) # normalize
