Distance-Based Anomaly Detection: KNN & LOF

How k-nearest neighbour distances and Local Outlier Factor detect anomalies — including local outliers hidden in varying-density datasets where global thresholds blindly fail.

Advanced Anomaly Detection Unsupervised Learning O(n² log n)
1
Understanding the Concept
▾

Anomaly detection asks: which points in a dataset don't belong? Distance-based methods answer this by reasoning: normal points live close to their neighbours; outliers live far away.

KNN-Distance Score

The simplest approach: for each point \(x\), compute its distance to its \(k\)-th nearest neighbour, \(d_k(x)\). A large \(d_k(x)\) means the point is isolated — a candidate outlier. Threshold this score globally and you have a detector.

The fundamental problem: real data has clusters of different density. A point that is far from everything in an overall sense might still be perfectly normal within a low-density region — and a point that is close to its neighbours in absolute terms might still be anomalous relative to its local neighbourhood.

Local Outlier Factor (LOF)

LOF (Breunig et al., 2000) fixes this by comparing each point's local density to the average local density of its neighbours. If your neighbourhood is much less dense than your neighbours' neighbourhoods, you're a local outlier — even if no global threshold would catch you.

Key Insight

LOF is a ratio of densities, not an absolute distance. A point with LOF ≈ 1 has density similar to its surroundings (normal). LOF ≫ 1 means it lives in a region far sparser than its neighbours — an outlier relative to its local context.

Analogy

Imagine villages scattered across a country. Most villages are in dense clusters near cities. A lone farmhouse far from any city looks obviously isolated (high KNN-distance). But now consider an arctic research station: it's in a sparse region, surrounded by other research stations at similar distances — totally normal locally. LOF captures this relativity; KNN-distance alone cannot.

2
How It Works Internally
▾

Step 1 — k-Distance and Neighbourhood

For each point \(p\), find its \(k\)-nearest neighbours \(\mathcal{N}_k(p)\). The k-distance \(d_k(p)\) is the distance to the \(k\)-th closest point (ties included in the neighbourhood, so \(|\mathcal{N}_k(p)| \geq k\)).

Step 2 — Reachability Distance

Raw distances are noisy for nearby points. LOF smooths them with reachability distance:

$$\text{reach-dist}_k(p, o) = \max\!\bigl(d_k(o),\; d(p, o)\bigr)$$

This clamps short distances to at least \(d_k(o)\) — the core of \(o\)'s own neighbourhood radius. It prevents statistical fluctuations from inflating density estimates in tight clusters.

Step 3 — Local Reachability Density (lrd)

The lrd of \(p\) is the inverse of the average reachability distance from \(p\) to its neighbours:

$$\text{lrd}_k(p) = \left(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}_k(p)|}\sum_{o \in \mathcal{N}_k(p)} \text{reach-dist}_k(p,o)\right)^{-1}$$

High lrd means \(p\) is close to its neighbours (dense). Low lrd means \(p\) is far from them (sparse).

Step 4 — LOF Score

Compare \(p\)'s density to its neighbours' densities:

$$\text{LOF}_k(p) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}_k(p)|}\sum_{o \in \mathcal{N}_k(p)} \frac{\text{lrd}_k(o)}{\text{lrd}_k(p)}$$
Interpretation

LOF ≈ 1: neighbours have similar density — \(p\) is normal.
LOF ≫ 1: neighbours are in a much denser region — \(p\) is an outlier.
LOF < 1: \(p\) is in a denser region than its neighbours (core of a cluster).

Design Choice: Why Reachability Distance?

Without clamping, two points that happen to be very close would each inflate the other's density estimate, producing artificially high lrd values. The max-clamp stabilises density estimates in tight clusters and makes LOF well-defined even when points coincide.

3
The Math Behind It
▾

KNN Outlier Score (Global Threshold)

Let \(\mathcal{X} = \{x_1, \ldots, x_n\} \subset \mathbb{R}^d\). Define:

$$\text{score}_{\text{KNN}}(x) = d_k(x) = \min_{S \subset \mathcal{X},\, |S|=k} \max_{o \in S} d(x, o)$$

i.e., the distance to the \(k\)-th nearest neighbour. Flag \(x\) as an outlier if \(d_k(x) > \tau\) for global threshold \(\tau\).

LOF Derivation

Define the k-neighbourhood with tie-handling:

$$\mathcal{N}_k(p) = \bigl\{o \in \mathcal{X} \setminus \{p\} : d(p,o) \leq d_k(p)\bigr\}$$

Reachability distance (smoothing operator):

$$\text{reach-dist}_k(p, o) = \max\bigl(d_k(o),\; d(p,o)\bigr)$$

Local reachability density (pseudo-density):

$$\text{lrd}_k(p) = \left(\frac{\sum_{o \in \mathcal{N}_k(p)} \text{reach-dist}_k(p,o)}{|\mathcal{N}_k(p)|}\right)^{-1}$$

LOF score (relative density ratio averaged over neighbourhood):

$$\text{LOF}_k(p) = \frac{\sum_{o \in \mathcal{N}_k(p)} \tfrac{\text{lrd}_k(o)}{\text{lrd}_k(p)}}{|\mathcal{N}_k(p)|}$$

Numerical Trace (k = 2)

Consider 4 points in 1D: \(A=0, B=1, C=2, O=10\). We compute LOF for \(O\).

  • \(d_2(O)\): nearest are \(C\) at 8, \(B\) at 9 → \(d_2(O) = 9\). \(\mathcal{N}_2(O) = \{B,C\}\)
  • \(d_2(B) = d(B,C) = 1\) (2nd NN of B is A at dist 1). \(d_2(C) = 1\).
  • \(\text{reach-dist}(O, B) = \max(d_2(B), d(O,B)) = \max(1, 9) = 9\)
  • \(\text{reach-dist}(O, C) = \max(d_2(C), d(O,C)) = \max(1, 8) = 8\)
  • \(\text{lrd}_2(O) = \left(\frac{9+8}{2}\right)^{-1} = \frac{2}{17} \approx 0.118\)
  • \(\text{lrd}_2(B) = \left(\frac{\max(1,1)+\max(1,1)}{2}\right)^{-1} = 1\). Similarly \(\text{lrd}_2(C) = 1\).
  • \(\text{LOF}_2(O) = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{0.118} + \frac{1}{0.118}\right) = \frac{1}{0.118} \approx 8.5\)

LOF = 8.5 ≫ 1 → \(O\) is flagged as an outlier. The cluster points \(A, B, C\) each score ≈ 1.

Theoretical Guarantee

For points deep inside a cluster of uniform density \(\lambda\), \(\text{LOF}_k(p) \to 1\) as \(n \to \infty\). For a true outlier at distance \(\Delta \gg d_k\) from any cluster, \(\text{LOF}_k(p) \approx \Delta / d_k \to \infty\). LOF is consistent in this sense.

When to Use LOF

Use LOF when: data has clusters of varying density, local context matters, you need interpretable per-point scores, dataset is small/medium (<50k points).
Prefer KNN-distance for: very simple uniform-density data, maximum interpretability, when speed matters less than correctness.
Prefer Isolation Forest for: large datasets (millions), fast inference, high-dimensional data.

Key Hyperparameter: k

Small \(k\) (3–5): detects micro-outliers, sensitive to noise. Large \(k\) (20–50): smoother, sees larger-scale structure. Rule of thumb: \(k \in [10, 30]\) for most datasets. LOF is typically evaluated for a range of \(k\) and the maximum LOF across the range is used.

From-Scratch & Production Code
▾

LOF from Scratch (NumPy)

import numpy as np
from scipy.spatial.distance import cdist

def lof(X, k=5):
    """Local Outlier Factor from scratch.
    Returns LOF scores; score >> 1 means outlier."""
    n = len(X)
    # Pairwise distances (n x n)
    D = cdist(X, X)
    np.fill_diagonal(D, np.inf)   # exclude self

    # k-distance for each point (distance to k-th NN)
    sorted_D = np.sort(D, axis=1)           # shape (n, n-1)
    k_dist = sorted_D[:, k-1]              # k-th NN distance

    # k-neighbourhood (all points within k_dist, ties allowed)
    # reach-dist(p, o) = max(k_dist[o], d(p,o))
    # lrd(p) = 1 / mean(reach-dist(p, o) for o in N_k(p))
    lrd = np.zeros(n)
    neighbours = []
    for i in range(n):
        # Neighbourhood: all points with d(i,j) <= k_dist[i]
        nb = np.where(D[i] <= k_dist[i])[0]
        neighbours.append(nb)
        reach_dists = np.maximum(k_dist[nb], D[i][nb])
        lrd[i] = 1.0 / (reach_dists.mean() + 1e-12)

    # LOF = average ratio of neighbour lrd to own lrd
    scores = np.zeros(n)
    for i in range(n):
        nb = neighbours[i]
        scores[i] = lrd[nb].mean() / (lrd[i] + 1e-12)
    return scores

# Example
X = np.random.randn(100, 2)
X = np.vstack([X, [8, 8]])   # clear outlier
scores = lof(X, k=10)
print(f"Outlier LOF: {scores[-1]:.2f}")   # >> 1
print(f"Normal mean LOF: {scores[:-1].mean():.2f}")  # ~ 1
