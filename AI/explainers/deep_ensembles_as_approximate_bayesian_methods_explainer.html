Deep Ensembles as Approximate Bayesian Methods

Capturing model uncertainty by averaging multiple neural network predictions
Intermediate Deep Learning Uncertainty Estimation O(M) inference, O(M) training
1
Understanding Deep Ensembles
▼

What is it? A deep ensemble is a collection of several neural networks (typically 5 to 15), each trained independently from different random initializations or on different subsets of the data. At inference time, their predictions are averaged. While simple, this technique is remarkably effective at improving predictive accuracy, robustness, and crucially, providing a strong estimate of model uncertainty.

Analogy: Imagine asking a panel of expert meteorologists to predict tomorrow's weather. If they all predict "sunny," you're quite confident. If half say "sunny" and half say "rain," you know there's high uncertainty, even if their average prediction is "partly cloudy." A single meteorologist might be overconfident. Deep ensembles work similarly: each network is an "expert," and their collective disagreement signals uncertainty in regions where the data is sparse or ambiguous.

💡 If you remember one thing

Deep ensembles approximate the posterior predictive distribution of a Bayesian Neural Network (BNN). Each ensemble member can be seen as a sample from the posterior distribution over network weights, \(p(w|D)\). Averaging their predictions then approximates the integral over this posterior, yielding both a robust mean prediction and a measure of uncertainty.

How It Works Internally
▼

The Recipe: Train, Predict, Average

The process for building and using a deep ensemble is straightforward:

  1. Train multiple models: Instantiate \(M\) identical neural network architectures. For each model \(m=1, \dots, M\), train it independently on the full training dataset (or a bootstrap sample) using different random initializations for weights and biases.
  2. Collect predictions: For a new input \(x^*\), obtain a prediction \(y_m^*(x^*)\) from each trained model \(m\). For classification, this is usually a vector of class probabilities; for regression, a scalar value.
  3. Combine predictions:
    • Mean prediction: For regression, the final prediction is the average of individual predictions: \(\hat{y}^*(x^*) = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M y_m^*(x^*)\). For classification, average the class probabilities.
    • Uncertainty: The variance (or standard deviation) of the individual predictions \((y_m^*(x^*))\) across the ensemble is used as a measure of model uncertainty. High variance implies high uncertainty.

The key insight is that even with identical architectures and training data, different random initializations lead to different local minima in the loss landscape, resulting in diverse predictions that collectively explore the space of plausible functions.

Design Decision: Why Independent Training?

The strength of deep ensembles comes from the diversity of its members. Training models independently from different random initializations is the simplest way to achieve this diversity. While other techniques like snapshot ensembles (where different models are saved at different points during a single training run) exist, independent training typically yields more diverse and higher-quality uncertainty estimates because the models explore more distinct regions of the parameter space.

🔑 Key Property

The variance of ensemble predictions naturally decomposes into two components: aleatoric uncertainty (inherent noise in the data, which even a perfect model cannot reduce) and epistemic uncertainty (uncertainty due to limited data or model capacity, which can be reduced with more data or a better model). Deep ensembles primarily capture epistemic uncertainty through model disagreement.

3
Why It Works — The Bayesian Connection
▼

Bayesian Neural Networks (BNNs)

In a fully Bayesian approach, we don't just find a single point estimate for the network weights \(w\). Instead, we aim to infer a posterior distribution over weights, \(p(w|D)\), given the training data \(D\). This posterior is typically intractable to compute exactly for deep networks:

$$p(w|D) = \frac{p(D|w)p(w)}{p(D)} = \frac{p(D|w)p(w)}{\int p(D|w')p(w')dw'}$$

Once we have the posterior, the goal is to compute the posterior predictive distribution for a new input \(x^*\):

$$p(y^*|x^*, D) = \int p(y^*|x^*, w) p(w|D) dw$$

This integral averages the predictions \(p(y^*|x^*, w)\) of all possible networks, weighted by how probable their weights \(w\) are under the posterior. This captures both predictive mean and uncertainty.

Ensembles as an Approximation

Deep ensembles effectively approximate this intractable integral. Each independently trained network \(m\) (with weights \(\hat{w}_m\)) can be viewed as a sample from the posterior \(p(w|D)\) (or at least, from a region of high posterior density). With \(M\) such samples, the integral can be approximated by a Monte Carlo sum:

$$p(y^*|x^*, D) \approx \frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M p(y^*|x^*, \hat{w}_m)$$

This approximation holds surprisingly well in practice. The ensemble mean \(\frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M y_m^*(x^*)\) approximates the Bayesian posterior predictive mean, and the variance of these predictions \(\frac{1}{M} \sum_{m=1}^M (y_m^*(x^*) - \hat{y}^*(x^*))^2\) approximates the posterior predictive variance. This variance is a powerful indicator of epistemic uncertainty.

⚠️ Not a True Bayesian Method

While deep ensembles provide a good approximation, they are not a "true" Bayesian method. Each member is a point estimate, not a full posterior. The "samples" \(\hat{w}_m\) are typically from different local optima, not necessarily from the global posterior distribution. Nevertheless, for practical purposes, deep ensembles often outperform more rigorous (and computationally expensive) BNN methods in terms of both accuracy and uncertainty calibration.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use deep ensembles:

  • When you need reliable uncertainty estimates (e.g., in medical diagnosis, autonomous driving, financial forecasting) where knowing "what the model doesn't know" is critical.
  • To improve predictive performance and robustness, as averaging predictions reduces variance and generalizes better than any single model.
  • When you have access to sufficient computational resources to train \(M\) models, but a full Bayesian Neural Network is too complex or slow.
  • As a strong baseline for uncertainty quantification, often outperforming more complex Bayesian inference methods for deep learning.

When NOT to use:

  • When computational resources are extremely limited, as training \(M\) models and running \(M\) inferences is \(M\) times more expensive than a single model.
  • If the models in the ensemble are not sufficiently diverse (e.g., all converge to the exact same local minimum), the ensemble will not provide meaningful uncertainty estimates.
  • For extremely simple tasks where a single model already achieves near-perfect performance and uncertainty is not a concern.
💡 Practical Advice

While training multiple independent models is the most common approach, techniques like Stochastic Weight Averaging (SWA) or Snapshot Ensembles can provide some of the benefits of ensembles at a lower computational cost by extracting multiple models from a single training run. However, their uncertainty estimates might not be as well-calibrated as those from independently trained ensembles.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import numpy as np
# Define a simple Neural Network
class SimpleNN(nn.Module):
def __init__(self):
super().__init__()
self.fc1 = nn.Linear(1, 64)
self.relu = nn.ReLU()
self.fc2 = nn.Linear(64, 1)
def forward(self, x):
return self.fc2(self.relu(self.fc1(x)))
# --- Training Phase ---
num_ensemble_members = 5
models = []
for i in range(num_ensemble_members):
model = SimpleNN()
optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=0.01)
criterion = nn.MSELoss()
# Simulate training (actual training loop omitted for brevity)
# for epoch in range(num_epochs):
# outputs = model(X_train)
# loss = criterion(outputs, y_train)
# optimizer.zero_grad()
# loss.backward()
# optimizer.step()
models.append(model) # Store the trained model
# --- Inference Phase ---
X_test = torch.linspace(-5, 5, 100).unsqueeze(1) # New inputs
ensemble_predictions = []
for model in models:
model.eval() # Set model to evaluation mode
with torch.no_grad():
predictions = model(X_test).squeeze().numpy()
ensemble_predictions.append(predictions)
# Convert list of arrays to a 2D array (M x N_test_points)
ensemble_predictions = np.array(ensemble_predictions)
# Calculate mean and standard deviation
mean_prediction = np.mean(ensemble_predictions, axis=0)
std_prediction = np.std(ensemble_predictions, axis=0)
print("Ensemble Mean:", mean_prediction[:5])
print("Ensemble Std Dev (Uncertainty):", std_prediction[:5])
