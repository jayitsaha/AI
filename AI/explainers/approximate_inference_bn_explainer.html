Approximate Inference: Loopy BP & Sampling

When exact inference is intractable — message-passing on cyclic graphs & MCMC sampling for marginals

Advanced Probabilistic ML Bayesian Networks High Complexity
1
Why Exact Inference Fails ▾

Exact probabilistic inference computes marginals like \(P(X_i)\) by summing over all assignments of the remaining variables — the variable elimination or junction tree algorithm. On a tree-structured graph, this runs in time linear in the number of variables. But the moment cycles appear, elimination creates factors (cliques) that can grow exponentially large.

Core Barrier
Inference in a general Bayesian Network (or MRF) is #P-hard. Even approximate inference is NP-hard in the worst case. Practical algorithms trade exactness for tractability.

Consider a grid MRF — the standard model in image segmentation. Each pixel node is connected to four neighbours, forming dense cycles. Variable elimination would produce cliques spanning entire rows, making it exponential. We need approximate methods.

Two major families: Variational methods (minimise a divergence; Loopy BP is one) and Monte Carlo / MCMC (sample from the distribution and estimate marginals from histograms).

Key Insight
Loopy BP pretends cycles don't exist and still works empirically; MCMC turns sampling into integration, avoiding the combinatorial sum entirely.

Loopy Belief Propagation ▾

Belief Propagation (BP) on a tree is exact. The message from node \(i\) to neighbour \(j\) is:

$$\mu_{i \to j}(x_j) = \sum_{x_i} \psi_i(x_i)\,\psi_{ij}(x_i,x_j) \prod_{k \in N(i)\setminus j} \mu_{k \to i}(x_i)$$

On a tree each message is computed once; beliefs are:

$$b_i(x_i) \propto \psi_i(x_i) \prod_{j \in N(i)} \mu_{j \to i}(x_i)$$

Loopy BP (LBP) applies exactly these update rules on a graph with cycles, iterating until convergence. Messages loop around cycles, potentially forever — hence "loopy."

Algorithm

  1. Initialise all messages \(\mu_{i\to j}^{(0)}(x_j) = 1\) (uniform).
  2. Repeat until convergence:
      For each edge \((i,j)\) in some schedule: $$\mu_{i \to j}^{(t+1)}(x_j) = \sum_{x_i} \psi_i(x_i)\,\psi_{ij}(x_i,x_j) \prod_{k \neq j} \mu_{k \to i}^{(t)}(x_i)$$
  3. Read off approximate beliefs \(b_i \propto \psi_i \prod_j \mu_{j\to i}\).
Warning: No Convergence Guarantee
LBP may oscillate or diverge on graphs with strong couplings and many short cycles. In practice, damping (blend new and old messages) helps:
\(\mu^{(t+1)} \leftarrow \lambda\,\mu^{(t+1)}_{\text{new}} + (1-\lambda)\,\mu^{(t)}\), with \(\lambda \approx 0.5\).

Numerical Trace (3-node loop, binary variables)

Nodes A–B–C form a triangle. All potentials \(\psi_{ij}(x_i,x_j) = \exp(0.8\cdot\mathbb{1}[x_i=x_j])\), unary \(\psi_A(1)=0.7, \psi_A(0)=0.3\), others uniform.

Iter 0: mu_A→B = [1,1], mu_B→A = [1,1], mu_C→A = [1,1], ...
Iter 1: mu_A→B(0) ∝ ψ_A(0)·e^0·mu_C→A(0) + ψ_A(0)·e^0.8·mu_C→A(0)
= 0.3*[e^0 + e^0.8] = 0.3*[1 + 2.226] = 0.968
mu_A→B(1) = 0.7*[e^0.8 + e^0] = 0.7*3.226 = 2.258 → normalise
normalised: mu_A→B ≈ [0.300, 0.700] (unchanged — uniform init)
Iter 5: mu_A→B ≈ [0.241, 0.759], b_A ≈ [0.218, 0.782]
Iter 10: converged b_A ≈ [0.214, 0.786] ← approximate marginal

Sampling Methods: Rejection, Importance & Gibbs MCMC ▾

Sampling-based inference estimates \(\mathbb{E}_P[f(X)] \approx \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^N f(x^{(n)})\) where \(x^{(n)} \sim P\). The key insight: integration by the law of large numbers, no combinatorial sum.

Rejection Sampling

Sample \(x \sim q(x)\) and accept with probability \(p(x)/(Mq(x))\) where \(M = \max p/q\). Accepted samples are exactly distributed as \(p\). Breaks in high dimensions — acceptance rate \(\to 0\).

$$P(\text{accept}) = \frac{1}{M}\int p(x)\,dx = \frac{1}{M}$$

Importance Sampling

Instead of rejecting, weight each sample by \(w(x) = p(x)/q(x)\):

$$\mathbb{E}_P[f] \approx \frac{\sum_n w(x^{(n)}) f(x^{(n)})}{\sum_n w(x^{(n)})}$$

Variance of the estimator is controlled by how well \(q\) covers \(p\). Self-normalised IS is consistent even when only the unnormalised \(\tilde{p}\) is available.

Gibbs Sampling (MCMC)

Gibbs constructs a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is exactly \(P(X)\). At each step, sample one variable from its conditional given the rest:

$$x_i^{(t+1)} \sim P(X_i \mid X_{-i} = x_{-i}^{(t)})$$

After a burn-in period (discard early samples), the chain mixes and samples approximate the target. For a Bayesian Network:

$$P(X_i \mid X_{-i}) \propto \psi_i(x_i) \prod_{j \in \text{MB}(i)} \psi_{ij}(x_i, x_j)$$

where MB(\(i\)) is the Markov blanket — parents, children, and co-parents.

Gibbs is MCMC without Metropolis
Each Gibbs step has acceptance probability 1 — no rejection needed. But mixing time can be exponential in strong dependency regimes (multimodal distributions).

Decision Rule
Treewidth ≤ ~15 → exact junction tree. Sparse graph, good results empirically → Loopy BP. Need unbiased marginals, enough compute → Gibbs/HMC. Need \(Z = \int p\) → IS or AIS.

Why Loopy BP Works

Yedidia, Freeman & Weiss (2001) showed LBP fixed points correspond to stationary points of the Bethe free energy, a tractable approximation to the exact free energy. On graphs with weak correlations and long cycles, the Bethe approximation is tight.
