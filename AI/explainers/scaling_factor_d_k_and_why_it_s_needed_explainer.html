The Scaling Factor \(\sqrt{d_k}\) in Attention

Why we divide by the square root of the key dimension in Transformer attention
Foundational Deep Learning Transformers Core Concept
1
Understanding Scaled Dot-Product Attention
▼

What is it? The "Scaled Dot-Product Attention" mechanism is a core component of the Transformer architecture, introduced by Vaswani et al. in "Attention Is All You Need". It calculates attention scores between a Query vector (\(Q\)) and a set of Key vectors (\(K\)), then uses these scores to weigh a set of Value vectors (\(V\)). The crucial part is the "scaling": dividing the dot product of \(Q\) and \(K\) by the square root of the dimension of the keys, \(\sqrt{d_k}\).

Analogy: Imagine you're rating how relevant different books (Keys) are to your current research topic (Query). You calculate a "match score" for each book. If your scoring system (dot product) tends to produce wildly different scores when you consider more details (higher \(d_k\)), some books might get extremely high scores while others get near-zero, making the system too "confident" in one choice. The \(\sqrt{d_k}\) factor is like normalizing your scores so that they remain in a sensible range, preventing extreme biases and allowing for more nuanced relevance judgments.

💡 If you remember one thing

The dot product \(Q \cdot K\) tends to grow in magnitude (and variance) as the dimension \(d_k\) increases. Without scaling, these large values push the softmax function into regions where its gradients are extremely small, hindering model training. Dividing by \(\sqrt{d_k}\) stabilizes the variance of the dot products, leading to more stable and effective training.

2
The Problem with Unscaled Dot Products
▼

The Attention Formula

The core of scaled dot-product attention is defined as:

$$\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V$$

Here, \(Q\) is the query matrix (or vector), \(K\) is the key matrix, \(V\) is the value matrix, and \(d_k\) is the dimension of the key (and query) vectors. The term \(QK^\top\) computes the dot products between each query and all keys. This is where the scaling factor comes into play.

Why Dot Products Grow with \(d_k\)

Consider two random vectors, \(q\) and \(k\), whose components are independent random variables with mean 0 and variance 1. Their dot product is \(q \cdot k = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i k_i\). Each term \(q_i k_i\) has a mean of 0 and a variance of 1 (since \(\operatorname{Var}(XY) = E[X^2]E[Y^2] - (E[X]E[Y])^2\), and for mean 0, unit variance, this is \(1 \cdot 1 - 0 = 1\)).

When you sum \(d_k\) such independent random variables, the variance of the sum is the sum of their variances. Thus, \(\operatorname{Var}(q \cdot k) = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} \operatorname{Var}(q_i k_i) = d_k \cdot 1 = d_k\). This means the standard deviation of the dot product is \(\sqrt{d_k}\). As \(d_k\) increases, the dot product values tend to become larger in magnitude, both positive and negative.

The Softmax "Squeeze"

The softmax function, \(\sigma(x)_i = \frac{e^{x_i}}{\sum_j e^{x_j}}\), is highly sensitive to large input values. If one input to softmax is significantly larger than the others, its exponential term will dominate, causing its output probability to approach 1 and all other probabilities to approach 0. This creates a "spiky" distribution, effectively making the attention mechanism focus almost exclusively on a single key, ignoring others.

⚠️ Vanishing Gradients

When softmax outputs are extremely close to 0 or 1, the gradients with respect to its inputs become very small. This is the problem of vanishing gradients, which makes it difficult for the model to learn meaningful attention weights during backpropagation, especially in deep Transformer networks.

3
Mathematical Justification for \(\sqrt{d_k}\)
▼

Variance of Dot Products

Let's formally derive the variance of the dot product between two random vectors. Assume \(q, k \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k}\) are vectors whose elements \(q_i, k_i\) are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables with mean \(E[q_i] = E[k_i] = 0\) and variance \(\operatorname{Var}(q_i) = \operatorname{Var}(k_i) = 1\). The dot product is \(S = q^\top k = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i k_i\).

First, the expected value of the dot product:

$$E[S] = E\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i k_i\right] = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} E[q_i k_i]$$ $$E[q_i k_i] = E[q_i] E[k_i] = 0 \cdot 0 = 0 \quad \text{(due to independence)}$$ $$E[S] = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} 0 = 0$$

Next, the variance of the dot product:

$$\operatorname{Var}(S) = E[S^2] - (E[S])^2 = E[S^2] - 0^2 = E[S^2]$$ $$E[S^2] = E\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d_k} q_i k_i\right)^2\right] = E\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d_k} (q_i k_i)^2 + \sum_{i \neq j} q_i k_i q_j k_j\right]$$ $$E[(q_i k_i)^2] = E[q_i^2 k_i^2] = E[q_i^2] E[k_i^2] = (\operatorname{Var}(q_i) + E[q_i]^2)(\operatorname{Var}(k_i) + E[k_i]^2) = (1+0)(1+0) = 1$$ $$E[q_i k_i q_j k_j] = E[q_i]E[k_i]E[q_j]E[k_j] = 0 \quad \text{(for } i \neq j \text{, due to independence and zero mean)}$$ $$\operatorname{Var}(S) = \sum_{i=1}^{d_k} 1 + \sum_{i \neq j} 0 = d_k$$

So, the standard deviation of the dot product \(q^\top k\) is \(\sqrt{d_k}\). This means that as \(d_k\) grows, the typical magnitude of the dot product values increases proportionally to \(\sqrt{d_k}\).

The Role of Scaling

To stabilize the inputs to the softmax function, we want to ensure that the variance of the attention scores remains roughly constant, regardless of \(d_k\). By dividing the dot product \(q^\top k\) by \(\sqrt{d_k}\), we effectively normalize its variance:

$$\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{q^\top k}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) = \frac{1}{(\sqrt{d_k})^2} \operatorname{Var}(q^\top k) = \frac{1}{d_k} \cdot d_k = 1$$

After scaling, the attention scores \(\frac{q^\top k}{\sqrt{d_k}}\) have an expected value of 0 and a variance of 1, similar to the initial components \(q_i, k_i\). This keeps the inputs to softmax in a more stable range, preventing the "spiky" distributions and ensuring that gradients are well-behaved during training.

🔑 Key Property

The scaling factor \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d_k}}\) is a form of variance normalization. It ensures that the attention scores maintain a consistent statistical distribution, preventing numerical instability and vanishing gradients in the softmax layer, which is crucial for effective Transformer training.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use \(\sqrt{d_k}\) scaling:

  • Always in Scaled Dot-Product Attention, as described in the original Transformer paper.
  • When using dot products to measure similarity between high-dimensional vectors that serve as inputs to a softmax-like function.
  • To prevent vanishing gradients and numerical instability during the training of deep neural networks, especially those relying on attention mechanisms.

Impact of NOT using scaling:

  • Spiky Softmax Distributions: Attention weights become concentrated on a single key, making the model "overconfident" and less able to consider multiple relevant contexts.
  • Vanishing Gradients: The extreme values from softmax lead to gradients close to zero, hindering learning and making the model difficult to train effectively.
  • Slower Convergence: Training becomes less efficient and may require more careful hyperparameter tuning.
  • Reduced Model Performance: The model may fail to learn complex dependencies and perform poorly on tasks.
💡 Beyond Transformers

The principle of stabilizing inputs to softmax is broadly applicable. While \(\sqrt{d_k}\) is specific to dot-product attention, the underlying idea of normalizing inputs to prevent extreme values and improve gradient flow is a common theme in deep learning (e.g., batch normalization, layer normalization).

Code Trace

import numpy as np
def softmax(x):
e_x = np.exp(x - np.max(x)) # Subtract max for numerical stability
return e_x / e_x.sum(axis=-1, keepdims=True)
# --- Parameters ---
d_k = 64 # Dimension of Query/Key vectors
num_keys = 3
# --- 1. Generate Query and Key vectors (simulated high-dim) ---
# For demonstration, we'll use fixed dot products to represent similarity
# In reality, Q, K are generated from input embeddings via linear layers
# Assume base dot products for d_k=4 are [0.9, 0.1, 0.5]
base_dot_products = np.array([0.9, 0.1, 0.5])
# --- 2. Calculate raw dot products (simulating effect of d_k) ---
# Dot products tend to scale with d_k. Here we simulate this by multiplying
# by a factor (d_k / base_dk_for_simulation)
sim_factor = d_k / 4.0 # Base d_k for simulation is 4
unscaled_scores = base_dot_products * sim_factor
print(f"Unscaled scores (d_k={d_k}): {unscaled_scores}")
# Example for d_k=64: [14.4, 1.6, 8.0]
# --- 3. Apply Softmax to unscaled scores ---
unscaled_attention_weights = softmax(unscaled_scores)
print(f"Unscaled attention weights: {unscaled_attention_weights}")
# Example for d_k=64: [0.999999999998, 0.0, 0.0] (spiky)
# --- 4. Apply scaling factor ---
scaling_factor = 1.0 / np.sqrt(d_k)
scaled_scores = unscaled_scores * scaling_factor
print(f"Scaled scores (d_k={d_k}): {scaled_scores}")
# Example for d_k=64: [1.8, 0.2, 1.0]
# --- 5. Apply Softmax to scaled scores ---
scaled_attention_weights = softmax(scaled_scores)
print(f"Scaled attention weights: {scaled_attention_weights}")
# Example for d_k=64: [0.55, 0.12, 0.33] (smoother)
