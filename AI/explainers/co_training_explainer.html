Co-training

Two classifiers, two views — labeling the unlabeled world for each other
Intermediate Semi-Supervised Learning Classical ML Part III · §14.1
1
Understanding Co-training

Most supervised learning is hungry for labeled data — expensive to gather and slow to annotate. Co-training is a clever trick: given two "views" of the same data (two independent feature sets describing the same example), train two separate classifiers, one on each view. The classifier that is most confident on an unlabeled example labels it for the other classifier's training pool. They teach each other.

💡 Key Insight
The conditional independence assumption — \(P(x^{(1)}, x^{(2)} \mid y) = P(x^{(1)} \mid y)\,P(x^{(2)} \mid y)\) — means neither view "leaks" information to the other. So a confident prediction from view-1 is genuinely new signal for view-2, not just a reflection of the same facts.

Everyday analogy: Imagine two doctors reviewing the same patient — one reads only the X-ray, the other reads only the blood-work report. When the radiologist is 99% certain it's pneumonia (high confidence from X-ray view), her diagnosis is added to the blood-work doctor's training examples, and vice versa. Because their information sources are independent, each confident labeling is trustworthy new evidence for the other.

📌 One Sentence
Co-training bootstraps supervised learning by having two classifiers trained on conditionally independent feature views iteratively label each other's most-confident unlabeled examples.
2
The Mechanism — Step by Step

Co-training requires three ingredients: (1) a small labeled set \(L\), (2) a large unlabeled pool \(U\), and (3) a feature split into two views \(x = (x^{(1)}, x^{(2)})\) that are (approximately) conditionally independent given the label \(y\).

The algorithm, due to Blum & Mitchell (1998), iterates:

Algorithm: Co-training

1. Train \(h_1\) on \(\{(x_i^{(1)}, y_i)\}_{i \in L}\), train \(h_2\) on \(\{(x_i^{(2)}, y_i)\}_{i \in L}\).
2. Each classifier scores every \(x_j \in U\).
3. \(h_1\) picks its \(k\) most confident predictions (highest \(\max_y P(y \mid x_j^{(1)})\)) → adds those \((x_j^{(2)}, \hat{y}_j)\) to \(L_2\).
4. \(h_2\) picks its \(k\) most confident predictions → adds those \((x_j^{(1)}, \hat{y}_j)\) to \(L_1\).
5. Remove chosen examples from \(U\); replenish \(U\) from the remaining pool.
6. Repeat until \(U\) is exhausted or convergence.

⚠️ Critical Assumption
The conditional independence assumption \(x^{(1)} \perp x^{(2)} \mid y\) must hold (approximately). If the two views are highly correlated, confident predictions from one are already "known" by the other — no new information is injected, and error propagation accelerates.
3
Why It Works — The Theory

Blum & Mitchell (1998) proved that under the conditional independence assumption and with a compatible concept class, co-training can learn from exponentially fewer labeled examples than standard supervised learning.

The key PAC-style argument: if each classifier achieves error rate \(\epsilon\) on its view, the probability that a mislabeling event passes undetected (i.e., the mislabeled example is confidently wrong in view-1 and accepted by view-2) decreases multiplicatively because of independence:

$$P(\text{wrong label added to } L_2) \;\leq\; P(h_1 \text{ wrong, confident}) \;\leq\; \epsilon_1$$

Because view-2 only sees \(x^{(2)}\), and the error events are independent across views, the "noise" injected into each classifier's pool is bounded and diminishes as labeling improves.

Formally, if we define the disagreement region as examples where \(h_1(x^{(1)}) \neq h_2(x^{(2)})\), Blum & Mitchell show the algorithm reduces disagreement at each round, converging to the target concept.

$$\Pr_{x \sim \mathcal{D}}\bigl[h_1(x^{(1)}) \neq h_2(x^{(2)})\bigr] \;\to\; 0 \quad \text{as rounds} \to \infty$$

The confidence threshold \(\tau\) controls the precision-recall tradeoff in pseudo-labeling: higher \(\tau\) means fewer but more reliable labels injected per round.

🎯 Decision Rule
Use co-training when: (a) a natural two-view split exists (text & metadata, image & caption, audio & video), (b) labeled data is scarce, and (c) the views are approximately conditionally independent. If no natural split exists, consider self-training instead.
  • Classic use-case: Web page classification — view 1 = page text, view 2 = anchor text of hyperlinks pointing to the page (Blum & Mitchell, 1998)
  • Multi-view extensions: Co-EM (probabilistic), Democratic co-learning (3+ classifiers), Co-regularization
  • Risk: Error propagation — if one classifier makes systematic errors, the other inherits them. Mitigate with high confidence thresholds and smaller \(k\).
