Distant Supervision — Knowledge-Base-Guided Labeling

Auto-generate training labels by aligning entity pairs from a KB to text — at scale, with noise
Intermediate Weak Supervision Classical ML Part III · 14.2
1
Understanding the Concept

What is it?

Distant supervision is a technique for creating large labeled training datasets automatically — without paying a single human annotator. You have a knowledge base (KB) full of known facts like "Apple was founded-by Steve Jobs", and a big pile of raw text. The algorithm scans every sentence, finds sentences that mention both "Apple" and "Steve Jobs", and automatically stamps them with the label "founded-by". No human reads any sentence.

Real-World Analogy

Imagine you want to train a dog to recognize cats, but you're lazy. Instead of showing the dog pictures and saying "cat / not cat", you take a folder of 10 million photos with filenames like cat_001.jpg and dog_002.jpg and just use the filename as the label — without checking whether the photo actually shows what the filename says. Sometimes the cat photo is mislabeled (someone named their file wrong). The dog learns anyway, because most filenames are right. That's distant supervision.

The Distant Supervision Assumption

If a KB triple \((e_1, r, e_2)\) is true, then ANY sentence containing both \(e_1\) and \(e_2\) is assumed to express relation \(r\). This assumption is almost never perfectly true — it's the source of all noise in the method.

If You Remember One Thing

Distant supervision trades annotation cost for label noise. You get millions of free labels, but some fraction will be wrong. Managing that noise — not generating the labels — is the hard part.

How It Works Internally

The Three-Step Pipeline

Step 1 — Build or import a Knowledge Base. A KB is a collection of triples \((e_1, r, e_2)\) where \(e_1\) and \(e_2\) are named entities and \(r\) is a semantic relation. Examples: Freebase, Wikidata, DBpedia.

Step 2 — Entity Linking. Run a Named Entity Recognition (NER) tagger over raw text to find mentions of entities. Map surface forms ("Apple Inc.", "Apple") to canonical KB identifiers. This step is imperfect — entity linking itself introduces noise.

Step 3 — Heuristic Label Assignment. For each sentence, find all entity pairs \((e_1, e_2)\) co-occurring in it. Look up whether any KB triple \((e_1, r, e_2)\) exists. If yes, label that sentence with relation \(r\). If no matching triple exists, label as NA (no relation). Sentences mentioning two entities with multiple KB relations get multi-label treatment.

Two Noise Sources

False positives: The sentence mentions both entities but expresses a different relation than the KB triple predicts. E.g., "Jobs left Apple in 1985" is labeled founded-by.

False negatives: A sentence genuinely expresses a relation, but that relation isn't in the KB (KB incompleteness), so the sentence gets labeled NA.

Noise Rate Formula

Let \(N\) = total sentences labeled positive for relation \(r\). Let \(N_{FP}\) = sentences labeled \(r\) that don't actually express \(r\). The empirical noise rate is:

$$\text{noise\_rate}(r) = \frac{N_{FP}}{N}$$

In practice, noise rates range from 30% to 70% depending on the KB, corpus, and relation type. The Mintz et al. (2009) original paper reported ~5–15% precision on some relations — meaning noise was very high but models still learned.

Design Decision: Sentence-Level vs Bag-Level

Sentence-level: Treat each sentence as an independent training example. Simple, but amplifies noise.
Bag-level (multi-instance learning): Group all sentences that mention the same entity pair into a "bag". Predict the relation at the bag level. If at least one sentence in the bag expresses the relation, the bag is positive. This reduces noise significantly and is the standard approach.

Why It Works — Math & Theory

The Mintz et al. (2009) Formulation

Let the training set be \(\mathcal{D} = \{(s_i, \tilde{y}_i)\}_{i=1}^N\) where \(\tilde{y}_i\) is the noisy KB-derived label for sentence \(s_i\). The true label \(y_i\) satisfies:

$$P(\tilde{y}_i \neq y_i) = \epsilon_r \quad \text{(relation-specific noise rate)}$$

A linear model scores each sentence using a feature vector \(\phi(s)\):

$$\hat{y} = \underset{r}{\operatorname{argmax}} \; w_r^\top \phi(s)$$

Training minimizes the empirical risk on noisy labels:

$$\hat{w} = \underset{w}{\operatorname{argmin}} \sum_{i=1}^N \ell(w^\top \phi(s_i), \tilde{y}_i) + \lambda \|w\|_2^2$$

Even with noise, the model learns because the signal (true relation patterns) appears consistently across many sentences while the noise is more random. The crucial insight is that noise decorrelates across examples — the mislabeled examples don't all share the same spurious features.

Multi-Instance Learning (PCNN-ATT, Riedel 2010)

Let \(\mathcal{B}_{e_1,e_2} = \{s_1, \ldots, s_k\}\) be the bag of all sentences mentioning entity pair \((e_1, e_2)\). The at-least-one assumption says the bag is positive for relation \(r\) if any one sentence truly expresses it. The bag-level objective:

$$P(r \mid \mathcal{B}) = \max_{s \in \mathcal{B}} P(r \mid s)$$

Or with attention weighting \(\alpha_i\) (Attention-based RE, Lin et al. 2016):

$$P(r \mid \mathcal{B}) = \sigma\!\left(\sum_{i} \alpha_i \cdot w_r^\top h_i\right), \quad \alpha_i = \operatorname{softmax}(e_i)$$

where \(h_i\) is the CNN/LSTM encoding of sentence \(s_i\) and \(e_i = h_i^\top A r\) is the attention score (how well sentence \(i\) aligns with relation \(r\)'s query vector \(r\)).

Key Property

Under the assumption that noise is random conditional on entities (not adversarial), and the signal-to-noise ratio \((1 - \epsilon_r)/\epsilon_r > 1\), the model trained on noisy labels converges to the same decision boundary as a model trained on clean labels, up to a label-noise-dependent scaling of the margin.

When Theory Breaks Down

The theory fails when noise is systematic: if the mislabeled sentences share the same lexical pattern (e.g., all "X left Y" sentences are labeled founded-by because Jobs+Apple co-occur), the model learns the spurious pattern. This is precisely what happens with high-frequency entity pairs.

Key Takeaway

Distant supervision works best when: (1) the KB has high precision (few wrong triples), (2) entities have unambiguous surface forms, (3) the relation of interest is clearly lexically expressed in text, and (4) you have enough sentences per entity pair to use bag-level inference.

Key Takeaways & Related Topics
  • Scale without annotation: Millions of noisy labels from KB alignment, zero annotator cost.
  • The DS assumption is the core bias: entity co-occurrence ≠ relation expression.
  • Multi-instance learning (MIL) is the dominant noise-reduction framework.
  • BERT-based encoders (2019+) dramatically reduce the impact of noise because contextual representations are richer — the model can distinguish "Jobs left Apple" from "Jobs founded Apple" without needing perfect labels for every example.
  • False negatives are underappreciated: KB incompleteness means many valid relation sentences are silently mislabeled as NA.
  • Closest relatives: Snorkel/data programming (labeling functions instead of KB), crowdsourcing, semi-supervised learning.

Key Papers

  • Mintz et al. (2009) — "Distant supervision for relation extraction without labeled data" — the founding paper (ACL 2009)
  • Riedel et al. (2010) — "Modeling relations and their mentions without labeled text" — MIL formulation (ECML-PKDD)
  • Hoffmann et al. (2011) — "Knowledge-based weak supervision with incomplete KB" — multi-label MIL
