Turning the noisy tail of SGD training into a cheap Bayesian posterior over weights
Bayesian Deep Learning Uncertainty Estimation Optimization O(K·D) memory
1
Understanding SWAG
▼

What is it? SWAG (SWA-Gaussian) is a cheap, practical way to get Bayesian-style uncertainty out of an ordinary SGD training run. It notices that when you train a neural network with a constant, aggressively high learning rate, the weights don't converge to one point — they keep bouncing around a wide, flat region of low loss. SWAG treats that entire cloud of bouncing weights as samples from an approximate posterior distribution, fits a Gaussian to it, and then samples new weight vectors from that Gaussian to build an ensemble — with almost no extra training cost.

Analogy: Imagine SGD as a marble rolling into a wide bowl-shaped valley. With a small learning rate the marble settles at the very bottom and stops — that's a single point estimate, like ordinary SGD or SWA's plain average. But with a high, constant learning rate the marble never fully stops: it keeps rattling around the bottom of the bowl, visiting slightly different spots each "epoch." SWAG watches where the marble rattles, fits an ellipse (a Gaussian) around that rattling cloud, and then imagines many plausible marbles scattered inside that ellipse — each one a slightly different, equally-plausible network.

💡 If you remember one thing

SWA takes the mean of SGD iterates for a better single model. SWAG goes one step further and also estimates the covariance of those iterates, giving a full Gaussian $$\theta \sim \mathcal{N}\!\big(\bar\theta_{SWA},\, \Sigma_{SWAG}\big)$$ that can be sampled to approximate Bayesian model averaging — turning one training run into an entire posterior over networks.

2
How SWAG Works Internally
▼

Phase 1 — Run SGD With a High, Constant Learning Rate

After a normal burn-in (a few epochs of standard training so the model is already in a good loss basin), SWAG switches to a constant, relatively large learning rate instead of decaying it to zero. Ordinary SGD theory says a decaying LR is needed to converge to a point; a constant LR instead makes SGD behave like a Markov chain that samples repeatedly from a stationary distribution concentrated around the basin — it never truly stops moving.

Phase 2 — Collect Snapshots, Not Just the Mean

SWA (the plain, non-Bayesian ancestor of SWAG) collects one weight snapshot \(\theta_i\) per epoch during this phase and simply averages them: \(\bar\theta_{SWA} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_i \theta_i\). This average sits deeper in the flat basin than any single noisy iterate, and flat minima are known to generalize better. SWAG keeps going: it also tracks the second moment and a low-rank set of deviation vectors, so it can reconstruct not just where the iterates centered, but how they spread out and along which directions they were correlated.

Phase 3 — Fit a Gaussian: Diagonal + Low-Rank

A full \(D \times D\) covariance matrix over millions of weights is impossible to store. SWAG approximates it cheaply with two pieces: a diagonal term (per-weight variance, capturing how much each individual weight wiggles) plus a low-rank term built from the last \(K\) deviation vectors \(D_i = \theta_i - \bar\theta_{SWA}\) (capturing correlated wiggling between weights — the fact that the noise cloud is usually a tilted ellipse, not an axis-aligned one).

Phase 4 — Sample and Average Predictions

To make a prediction, SWAG draws several weight vectors \(\tilde\theta \sim \mathcal{N}(\bar\theta_{SWA}, \Sigma_{SWAG})\), runs the same input through each sampled network, and averages the outputs — this Monte-Carlo average approximates the Bayesian posterior predictive \(p(y|x) \approx \frac{1}{S}\sum_s p(y | x, \tilde\theta_s)\), and the spread across samples gives a calibrated uncertainty estimate for free.

🔑 Key Property

SWAG requires no architecture changes, no extra backward passes, and no second training run. It re-uses the same SGD trajectory that a normal training run already produces — the "Bayesian-ness" comes almost entirely from watching the noise SGD was already generating, instead of averaging it away or ignoring it.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Running Mean (SWA)

After each new snapshot \(\theta_{n+1}\), the running SWA mean updates in O(D) time without storing all prior snapshots:

$$\bar\theta_{SWA}^{(n+1)} = \frac{n\,\bar\theta_{SWA}^{(n)} + \theta_{n+1}}{n+1}$$

Diagonal Covariance

SWAG also tracks the running mean of the squared weights, \(\overline{\theta^2}\), so the per-weight variance falls out as a second moment identity — no need to keep every snapshot:

$$\Sigma_{diag} = \operatorname{diag}\big(\overline{\theta^2} - \bar\theta_{SWA}^2\big)$$

Low-Rank Covariance

Only the last \(K\) deviation vectors are kept, stacked as columns of a \(D \times K\) matrix \(\hat D = [\,D_1, \dots, D_K\,]\) with \(D_i = \theta_i - \bar\theta_{SWA}^{(i)}\). This gives a low-rank correction that captures correlations the diagonal term misses:

$$\Sigma_{low\text{-}rank} = \frac{1}{K-1}\hat D \hat D^\top$$

The Full SWAG Posterior

The two pieces are combined (each halved, per the original paper, so the diagonal and low-rank terms contribute comparably):

$$\Sigma_{SWAG} = \tfrac{1}{2}\Sigma_{diag} + \tfrac{1}{2}\Sigma_{low\text{-}rank}$$

A new weight sample is then drawn using two independent standard-normal vectors \(z_1 \in \mathbb{R}^D\), \(z_2 \in \mathbb{R}^K\):

$$\tilde\theta \;=\; \bar\theta_{SWA} \;+\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\Sigma_{diag}^{1/2} z_1 \;+\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{2(K-1)}}\,\hat D z_2$$
⚠️ Common Pitfall

The Gaussian approximation is only local — it describes the noise around one basin, not the multi-modal landscape of the whole loss surface. SWAG captures uncertainty within a mode, not across genuinely different modes (that's what Deep Ensembles are better at). Also, batch norm statistics must be recomputed after each sampled \(\tilde\theta\) — sampled weights need a fresh forward pass over training data to update running BN stats before they're used for prediction.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use SWAG:

  • You want calibrated uncertainty (e.g. for OOD detection, active learning, safety-critical predictions) but cannot afford training an ensemble of separate models
  • You already have a working SGD training pipeline — SWAG bolts onto the tail end of it
  • You need Bayesian model averaging within a single, reasonably well-understood loss basin

When NOT to use:

  • The loss surface has drastically different, far-apart modes you care about capturing — SWAG only models local (within-basin) uncertainty; use Deep Ensembles instead
  • You need exact posterior inference — SWAG is a low-rank Gaussian approximation, not exact Bayesian inference
  • Your model uses batch norm heavily and you can't afford a BN-statistics recomputation pass per sample at inference time

💡 The Core Trick

SWAG's central insight is that SGD noise is not garbage to be averaged away — it is a signal about posterior uncertainty. A high constant learning rate turns SGD into an approximate sampler; SWAG just fits a Gaussian to what it was already producing.
