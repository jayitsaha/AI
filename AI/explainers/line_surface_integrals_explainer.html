Line Integrals & Surface Integrals

Integrating a vector field along a curve (work) and through a surface (flux)
Intermediate Vector Calculus Math Foundations Core for Physics & PDE
1
Understanding Line & Surface Integrals
▼

What is a line integral? Ordinary integration sums a function over an interval on the number line. A line integral extends this: you sum a function (or the component of a vector field) along an arbitrary curve in space. The curve is the "path of integration," not a straight line.

Two flavours: A line integral of a scalar field \(f\) along curve \(C\) accumulates \(f\) weighted by arc length — useful for mass of a wire. More important in ML/physics is the line integral of a vector field \(\mathbf{F}\): it accumulates the component of \(\mathbf{F}\) that points along the curve at each point, giving the work done by the force field as a particle traverses \(C\).

💡 If you remember one thing

A line integral \(\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) is the total dot product of the vector field with infinitesimal displacement vectors along the path — it measures how much the field helps (or hinders) motion along \(C\). Positive work = field pushes with you; negative = field pushes against you.

Surface integrals generalize further: instead of a curve, the domain of integration is a 2D surface \(S\) in 3D space. The surface integral \(\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S}\) measures flux — how much of the vector field passes through the surface per unit time. This is central to Maxwell's equations, fluid flow, and divergence/curl theorems.

Everyday analogy: Imagine rowing a boat across a river with a current. The line integral of the current field along your rowing path tells you how much the current helped (or fought) your journey. If you instead ask "how much water flows through an imaginary net held across the river," that's a surface integral.

2
How Line & Surface Integrals Work Internally
▼

Line Integral — Step by Step

Parametrize the curve \(C\) by \(t \in [a,b]\): the curve is \(\mathbf{r}(t) = (x(t), y(t))\) (2D) or \((x(t),y(t),z(t))\) (3D). The velocity vector is \(\mathbf{r}'(t) = (x'(t), y'(t))\), which is tangent to the curve. At each infinitesimal step \(dt\), the displacement is \(d\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{r}'(t)\,dt\). The integrand becomes:

$$\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_a^b \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t)\, dt$$

Each term \(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t))\cdot\mathbf{r}'(t)\,dt\) is the signed contribution to work at parameter \(t\): positive when \(\mathbf{F}\) aligns with the direction of travel, negative when it opposes it.

Numerical Example (2D)

Let \(\mathbf{F}(x,y) = (-y, x)\) (a counterclockwise vortex field) and let \(C\) be the semicircle \(\mathbf{r}(t) = (\cos t, \sin t)\) for \(t \in [0, \pi]\). Then \(\mathbf{r}'(t) = (-\sin t, \cos t)\). The integrand is:

$$\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t) = (-\sin t)(-\sin t) + (\cos t)(\cos t) = \sin^2 t + \cos^2 t = 1$$

So \(\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_0^\pi 1\, dt = \pi\). The vortex field perfectly aligns with the semicircle at every point — maximum possible work!

Surface Integral — Flux

Parametrize surface \(S\) by \(\mathbf{r}(u,v)\) over region \(D\). The surface element is the cross product: \(d\mathbf{S} = \left(\frac{\partial \mathbf{r}}{\partial u} \times \frac{\partial \mathbf{r}}{\partial v}\right)du\,dv\). Its magnitude is the area of the parallelogram spanned by the two tangent vectors; its direction is the outward normal \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\).

$$\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iint_D \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(u,v)) \cdot \left(\frac{\partial \mathbf{r}}{\partial u} \times \frac{\partial \mathbf{r}}{\partial v}\right) du\,dv$$
🔑 Key Property — Path Independence & Conservative Fields

If \(\mathbf{F} = \nabla \phi\) (a gradient field / conservative field), then \(\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \phi(\mathbf{r}(b)) - \phi(\mathbf{r}(a))\) — the line integral depends only on the endpoints, not the path. This is the Fundamental Theorem for Line Integrals. Non-conservative fields (like vortex fields) do depend on path.

3
The Math Behind It
▼

Stokes' Theorem — The Bridge Between Line and Surface Integrals

Stokes' theorem is the deep connection: the line integral of \(\mathbf{F}\) around a closed curve \(\partial S\) equals the surface integral of the curl of \(\mathbf{F}\) over any surface \(S\) bounded by that curve:

$$\oint_{\partial S} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot d\mathbf{S}$$

In 2D, this reduces to Green's Theorem: for a region \(D\) with boundary \(C\):

$$\oint_C (P\,dx + Q\,dy) = \iint_D \left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right) dA$$

The Divergence Theorem (Gauss) connects surface integrals to volume integrals:

$$\oiint_{\partial V} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iiint_V (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F})\, dV$$

Scalar Line Integral (Arc Length Formulation)

For a scalar field \(f\), the line integral with respect to arc length \(ds = |\mathbf{r}'(t)|\,dt\) is:

$$\int_C f\, ds = \int_a^b f(\mathbf{r}(t))\, |\mathbf{r}'(t)|\, dt$$

This does not depend on orientation — it's always non-negative if \(f\geq 0\). Example: total mass of a wire with linear density \(\rho(x,y)\).

⚠️ Common Pitfalls

Orientation matters: reversing the direction of \(C\) negates \(\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) (but not \(\int_C f\,ds\)). Reparametrization that preserves orientation leaves the integral unchanged. Choice of normal: for a non-closed surface, the sign of the flux depends on which side you call the "outward" normal — always state convention explicitly.

Key Takeaways & Applications
▼

Line integrals appear in:

  • Physics: Work done by a force along a path; circulation of a fluid velocity field
  • ML & Optimization: Path length in parameter space; neural ODE trajectories
  • Electromagnetism: Ampère's law (magnetic field around a current loop)
  • Geometry: Arc length, mass of curved wire, reparametrization-invariant quantities

Surface integrals appear in:

  • Fluid mechanics: Volume flow rate through a surface (flux)
  • Maxwell's equations: Gauss's laws for electric and magnetic flux
  • Computer graphics: Irradiance integrals over hemispheres; rendering equations
  • Probability: Marginalizing joint distributions over curved manifolds
💡 The Grand Hierarchy

FTC: \(\int_a^b f'\,dx = f(b)-f(a)\)  ⊂ FT Line Integrals: \(\int_C \nabla\phi\cdot d\mathbf{r} = \phi(B)-\phi(A)\)  ⊂ Green's (2D) / Stokes' (3D) / Divergence Thm — all generalize the idea that a "boundary integral" equals an "interior integral" of a derivative.

Code Trace — Numerical Integration
▼

Numerically computing the line integral \(\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\) for \(\mathbf{F}=(-y,x)\) along a semicircle.

import numpy as np
# Vector field F(x,y) = (-y, x) — vortex field
def F(x, y): return np.array([-y, x])
# Parametrize curve C: semicircle r(t) = (cos t, sin t), t in [0, pi]
def r(t): return np.array([np.cos(t), np.sin(t)])
def dr(t): return np.array([-np.sin(t), np.cos(t)]) # r'(t)
# Numerical integration via Riemann sum (or use scipy.integrate.quad)
N = 10000 # number of sub-intervals
t_vals = np.linspace(0, np.pi, N+1)
dt = np.pi / N
work = 0.0
for t in t_vals[:-1]:
pt = r(t) # point on curve
Fval = F(pt[0], pt[1]) # field at that point
drval = dr(t) # tangent direction
work += np.dot(Fval, drval) * dt # F·r' dt
print(f"Line integral = {work:.6f}") # → 3.141593 (= π)
# ── Surface Integral (Flux through z=0 plane, unit square) ──
def G(x,y,z): return np.array([x, y, z]) # radial field
n_hat = np.array([0, 0, 1]) # upward normal
u = np.linspace(0,1,500); v = np.linspace(0,1,500)
U, V = np.meshgrid(u, v)
dA = (1/500)**2 # area element
flux = np.sum(G(U, V, 0)[2]) * dA # F·n dA summed
print(f"Flux = {flux:.6f}") # → 0 (z-comp of G=0 at z=0)
