Speech Representation: MFCC & Log-Mel Spectrogram

Turning a raw audio waveform into features a model can actually learn from
Intermediate Speech & Audio Signal Processing O(N log N) FFT
1
Understanding Speech Features
▼

What is it? A microphone gives you one number per sample — air pressure over time. That raw waveform is technically all the information there is, but it is a terrible input for a model: pitch, phonemes, and speaker identity are all tangled together in a 1-D signal sampled 8,000–16,000 times per second. MFCC (Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients) and the log-mel spectrogram are two ways of re-expressing that waveform as "which frequencies are present, and how loud, at every instant" — in a way that matches how the human ear resolves pitch and loudness.

Analogy: Think of the difference between a seismograph readout of a piano performance (raw waveform — a wiggly line, useless to a musician) and sheet music (a spectrogram — time on one axis, pitch on the other, darkness = loudness). MFCCs go one step further: instead of writing every note, they summarize the "timbre" of each 25ms slice of sound with about 13 numbers — like describing a chord by its shape rather than listing every harmonic.

💡 If you remember one thing

Both features are built by the same pipeline: window a short frame → FFT → take power → pass through a bank of triangular mel-scaled filters → take the log. Stop there and you have a log-mel spectrogram. Apply a DCT on top and keep the first ~13 coefficients, and you have MFCCs: $$\text{MFCC} = \operatorname{DCT}\Big(\log\big(\text{MelFilter}(|\operatorname{STFT}(x)|^2)\big)\Big)$$

2
How the Pipeline Works Internally
▼

1. Framing — speech is only stationary for ~20-30ms

A vowel's spectral shape changes continuously as the vocal tract moves, so you cannot take one giant FFT of a whole sentence — the frequency content isn't fixed. Instead the signal is sliced into short overlapping frames (typically 25ms long, 10ms hop) short enough that within one frame the vocal tract is roughly frozen, so the frame is quasi-stationary and a single spectrum is meaningful.

2. Windowing — killing edge discontinuities

Cutting a rectangular chunk out of a continuous signal creates sharp artificial edges. An FFT of a signal with sharp edges "leaks" energy into neighboring frequency bins (spectral leakage) because a rectangular window's own spectrum is a wide sinc function. Multiplying the frame by a tapered window (Hamming/Hann) forces the edges toward zero smoothly, so the window's own spectrum is much narrower and leaks far less.

3. FFT → power spectrum

The Fast Fourier Transform computes the Discrete Fourier Transform of the windowed frame in \(O(N\log N)\) instead of the naive \(O(N^2)\), giving a complex spectrum \(X[k]\). We discard phase and keep the power \(|X[k]|^2\) — for recognition/representation tasks, the magnitude carries almost all the useful information, and phase is largely discarded by the ear too.

4. Mel filterbank — matching the cochlea

The cochlea does not resolve frequency linearly: it resolves low frequencies with fine detail and high frequencies coarsely. The mel scale mimics this: a bank of ~20-40 overlapping triangular filters, densely packed below ~1kHz and log-spaced above, each summing a weighted region of the power spectrum into one filterbank energy.

5. Log compression

Loudness is perceived roughly logarithmically (Weber–Fechner law) — the difference between a whisper and normal speech "feels" similar in magnitude to the difference between normal speech and a shout, even though the physical energy ratio is huge. Taking log of each filter energy compresses this dynamic range to match perception, and as a side effect turns multiplicative channel/gain effects into additive offsets that are easy to normalize away later.

6. DCT → MFCC (optional final step)

Adjacent mel filters overlap, so the log-mel vector is strongly correlated bin-to-bin. The Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT-II) acts as a near-optimal decorrelating transform for this kind of locally-correlated signal, concentrating most of the energy into the first few coefficients. Keeping only the lowest 12-13 coefficients yields a compact, roughly independent feature — exactly what classical Gaussian models (GMM-HMM) with diagonal covariance need.

🔑 Design decision: MFCC vs. log-mel

The DCT was essential when acoustic models were GMMs assuming diagonal (independent) feature covariance. Modern CNN/Transformer acoustic models can learn correlations themselves, so many state-of-the-art systems skip the DCT entirely and feed the richer log-mel spectrogram straight into a 2-D convolution, treating it like an image.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

The short-time Fourier transform

For a frame of \(N\) samples \(x[n]\), windowed by \(w[n]\), the DFT is:

$$X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\,w[n]\,e^{-i 2\pi kn/N}, \qquad k = 0,\dots,N-1$$

The FFT computes this exactly via Cooley–Tukey divide-and-conquer (splitting even/odd indices recursively), giving identical values in \(O(N\log N)\) instead of \(O(N^2)\). The power spectrum \(P[k] = \tfrac{1}{N}|X[k]|^2\) is a (high-variance) estimate of the frame's power spectral density — the periodogram.

The mel scale

The perceptual pitch scale is approximated by:

$$m = 2595 \log_{10}\!\left(1 + \frac{f}{700}\right) \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad f = 700\left(10^{m/2595} - 1\right)$$

Mel filter centers are placed at points equally spaced in \(m\), then converted back to Hz — which is why they crowd together at low frequency and spread out at high frequency. Filter \(m\)'s energy is a weighted sum of the power spectrum:

$$S_m = \sum_{k} H_m[k]\,P[k], \qquad L_m = \log(S_m + \varepsilon)$$

where \(H_m[k]\) is a triangular weight peaking at 1 at the filter's center frequency and 0 at its neighbors' centers.

The DCT-II and why it decorrelates

$$c_i = \sum_{m=0}^{M-1} L_m \cos\!\left[\frac{\pi i (2m+1)}{2M}\right], \qquad i = 0, \dots, M-1$$

A classical result in signal processing: for a signal whose covariance matrix is (approximately) Toeplitz — true of log-mel vectors because nearby filters overlap and correlate similarly regardless of position — the eigenvectors of that covariance are well approximated by cosine basis functions. That means the DCT approximates the data-dependent Karhunen–Loève transform (PCA) without needing to estimate any covariance from data, packing most variance into the first few \(c_i\) and leaving the rest near-independent and low-energy — safe to discard.

⚠️ Common misconception

MFCCs are not phoneme labels and don't map 1-to-1 onto articulatory features — they are a lossy, compressed acoustic summary. Also, \(c_0\) is (up to scaling) the log total frame energy, not a "frequency" coefficient — it's often replaced by an explicit log-energy term or dropped for loudness-invariance.

When to use MFCC: constrained compute, classical statistical models (GMMs), speaker/keyword-spotting pipelines, or whenever you need a compact, roughly independent feature.

When to use log-mel: deep learning acoustic models (CNN, RNN, Conformer, Whisper-style encoders) — convolution over the time-frequency image exploits local correlations the DCT would have thrown away.

💡 Hierarchy

Waveform → STFT power spectrum → mel filterbank energies → log-mel spectrogram → (optional DCT) → MFCC. Each arrow both compresses information and imposes a structural assumption (stationarity, perceptual frequency weighting, perceptual loudness scaling, local decorrelation) — know which assumption you need before picking where in the chain to stop.

import numpy as np
# 1) Frame the signal: 25ms window, 10ms hop
frame_len, hop = int(0.025*sr), int(0.010*sr)
frames = np.array([x[i:i+frame_len]
for i in range(0, len(x)-frame_len, hop)])
# 2) Window each frame (Hamming) to reduce spectral leakage
window = np.hamming(frame_len)
frames_w = frames * window
# 3) FFT -> magnitude -> power spectrum
spectrum = np.fft.rfft(frames_w, n=n_fft)
power = (np.abs(spectrum) ** 2) / n_fft
# 4) Mel filterbank (triangular, mel-spaced centers)
mel_fb = build_mel_filterbank(n_filters=20, n_fft=n_fft, sr=sr)
mel_energy = power @ mel_fb.T # (n_frames, n_filters)
# 5) Log compression -> log-mel spectrogram (final feature, option A)
log_mel = np.log(mel_energy + 1e-10)
# 6) DCT-II -> MFCC, keep first 13 coefficients (option B)
from scipy.fftpack import dct
mfcc = dct(log_mel, type=2, axis=1, norm='ortho')[:, :13]
