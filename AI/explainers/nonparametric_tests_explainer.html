Understanding Non-Parametric Tests
▼

What is it? Classical hypothesis tests like the t-test and ANOVA assume your data is normally distributed and has equal variance. Non-parametric tests make no such assumption about the underlying distribution — they work on the ranks of observations rather than their raw values, making them robust to outliers and skewed data.

Analogy: Imagine comparing how satisfied customers are with two restaurants, measured on a 1–10 scale. Instead of computing mean ratings (which assumes scores behave like normal variables), you ask: "Which restaurant tends to get higher scores more often?" You replace each score with its rank (1st, 2nd, 3rd…) among all scores combined, then compare the rank sums. The absolute values stop mattering; only the ordering does.

If you remember one thing

Non-parametric tests replace raw observations with their ranks (or compare empirical distributions), letting the test remain valid when normality fails. The key trade-off: they lose power when normality truly holds, but remain valid (correct Type I error) when it doesn't.

The Five Tests at a Glance

  • Mann-Whitney U — two independent groups, compares rank distributions
  • Wilcoxon Signed-Rank — one group measured twice (paired), compares rank sums of differences
  • Kruskal-Wallis H — three or more independent groups, generalization of Mann-Whitney
  • Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) — compares entire distributions, not just location; measures maximum ECDF gap
  • Permutation test — exactly distributes the test statistic under the null by exhaustive or Monte Carlo relabelling

How Each Test Works Internally
▼

Mann-Whitney U (two independent groups)

Pool all observations from groups A (size \(n_1\)) and B (size \(n_2\)) and rank them jointly. The U statistic counts the number of times a value from A exceeds a value from B:

$$U = \sum_{i=1}^{n_1}\sum_{j=1}^{n_2} \mathbf{1}[X_i > Y_j]$$

Equivalently, \(U_A = R_A - \frac{n_1(n_1+1)}{2}\), where \(R_A\) is the sum of ranks for group A. Under \(H_0\) (no difference), \(\mathbb{E}[U] = n_1 n_2 / 2\). For large samples, \(U\) is approximately normal.

Wilcoxon Signed-Rank (one group, paired)

Compute differences \(d_i = X_i - Y_i\) for each pair. Rank the absolute differences \(|d_i|\). Let \(W^+\) be the sum of ranks where \(d_i > 0\) and \(W^-\) the sum where \(d_i < 0\). The test statistic is \(W = \min(W^+, W^-)\). Under \(H_0\), positive and negative ranks are equally likely, so \(\mathbb{E}[W^+] = n(n+1)/4\).

Kruskal-Wallis H (k groups)

Rank all \(N = \sum n_i\) observations jointly. The H statistic measures how spread the group rank means are:

$$H = \frac{12}{N(N+1)} \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{R_i^2}{n_i} - 3(N+1)$$

where \(R_i\) is the sum of ranks in group \(i\). Under \(H_0\), \(H \sim \chi^2(k-1)\) asymptotically.

Kolmogorov-Smirnov (full distribution comparison)

Compute the empirical CDFs \(\hat{F}_1(x)\) and \(\hat{F}_2(x)\). The KS statistic is the maximum absolute gap:

$$D = \sup_x \left|\hat{F}_1(x) - \hat{F}_2(x)\right|$$

A large \(D\) suggests the two samples come from different distributions. The KS test is sensitive to differences in both location and shape (variance, skewness, tails).

Permutation Test (exact, any statistic)

Under \(H_0\) (no group difference), all labellings of the data are equally likely. Compute the observed statistic \(T_\text{obs}\) (e.g., difference in means). Then randomly relabel group memberships many times, computing \(T\) for each permutation to build the null distribution. The p-value is the fraction of permuted \(T\)s as extreme as \(T_\text{obs}\).

Design Principle

Rank-based tests (Mann-Whitney, Wilcoxon, Kruskal-Wallis) achieve distribution-freedom by replacing observations with their ranks: the marginal distribution of ranks under \(H_0\) is always uniform over \(\{1,\ldots,N\}!\), regardless of the original data distribution.

Why They Work — The Math
▼

Why Ranks Are Distribution-Free

If \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) are i.i.d. with a continuous distribution, then any permutation of ranks \((1, 2, \ldots, n)\) is equally likely under \(H_0\). The probability of any particular ranking is \(1/n!\). This holds for any continuous distribution — hence "distribution-free."

$$P(\text{rank vector} = (r_1,\ldots,r_n)) = \frac{1}{n!} \quad \text{under } H_0$$

Mann-Whitney U — Exact Mean & Variance

Under \(H_0\) (identical populations):

$$\mathbb{E}[U] = \frac{n_1 n_2}{2}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(U) = \frac{n_1 n_2 (n_1 + n_2 + 1)}{12}$$

The normal approximation \(z = (U - \mathbb{E}[U]) / \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(U)}\) works well for \(n_1, n_2 \geq 8\). For ties, the variance is corrected by a tie-correction factor.

Wilcoxon Signed-Rank — Moments

For \(n\) pairs with all non-zero differences:

$$\mathbb{E}[W^+] = \frac{n(n+1)}{4}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(W^+) = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{24}$$

KS Test — Limiting Distribution

The Kolmogorov distribution is the limiting law of \(\sqrt{n}\, D_n\) as \(n \to \infty\):

$$P\!\left(\sqrt{n}\, D_n \leq x\right) \xrightarrow{n\to\infty} 1 - 2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (-1)^{k-1} e^{-2k^2 x^2} \quad (x > 0)$$

This remarkable result (Kolmogorov 1933) is distribution-free — the limiting law doesn't depend on \(F\).

Permutation Test — Exact Type I Error

The permutation p-value is exactly valid under exchangeability (observations can be permuted under \(H_0\)). No asymptotic approximation is needed. With \(M\) random permutations:

$$\hat{p} = \frac{1 + \#\{T_m : |T_m| \geq |T_\text{obs}|\}}{M + 1}$$

(The +1 in numerator and denominator accounts for the observed statistic itself, ensuring conservativeness.)

Common Pitfall

These tests are not assumption-free: they require independence within and between groups. Wilcoxon signed-rank additionally requires that the distribution of differences be symmetric under \(H_0\). The KS test requires a continuous distribution (ties inflate the statistic). And all rank-based tests lose some power vs. a t-test when normality truly holds (asymptotic relative efficiency of Mann-Whitney vs. t-test = \(3/\pi \approx 0.955\)).

Key Takeaways & When to Use Each Test
▼

Decision guide

  • Two independent groups, location shift? → Mann-Whitney U (non-parametric t-test analogue)
  • One group, before vs. after? → Wilcoxon signed-rank (non-parametric paired t-test analogue)
  • Three or more independent groups? → Kruskal-Wallis (non-parametric one-way ANOVA analogue)
  • Compare full distributions (shape, tails, spread)? → Kolmogorov-Smirnov
  • Small sample, no distributional assumptions, any statistic? → Permutation test

Power considerations

When normality holds, rank-based tests lose ~5% efficiency vs. parametric counterparts (ARE = 3/π ≈ 0.955 for Mann-Whitney vs. t-test). When distributions are heavy-tailed or skewed, non-parametric tests can be more powerful than parametric tests.

Rule of Thumb

Use non-parametric tests when: (1) \(n < 30\) and normality is uncertain, (2) data contains outliers that cannot be justified as errors, (3) variables are ordinal (not truly continuous), or (4) you want a test that is robust to any shape of distribution. For large \(n\), parametric tests are robust via CLT and non-parametric tests offer little benefit.

Code Trace — All Five Tests
▼
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
# --- Mann-Whitney U ---
group_a = [2.1, 3.4, 4.0, 5.2, 6.1]
group_b = [1.0, 2.5, 3.0, 4.5, 7.2]
u_stat, p = stats.mannwhitneyu(group_a, group_b, alternative='two-sided')
# u_stat=15.0, p=0.6905 (no significant difference)
# --- Wilcoxon Signed-Rank ---
before = [120, 115, 130, 125, 118]
after = [110, 112, 119, 118, 115]
w_stat, p = stats.wilcoxon(before, after, alternative='two-sided')
# w_stat=0.0, p=0.0625 (all diffs same sign: marginal)
# --- Kruskal-Wallis ---
g1 = [3.2, 4.1, 5.0, 3.8] # low group
g2 = [7.2, 8.1, 6.5, 9.0] # high group
g3 = [4.5, 5.3, 6.2, 4.9] # mid group
h_stat, p = stats.kruskal(g1, g2, g3)
# h_stat=8.769, p=0.0125 (significant!)
# --- Kolmogorov-Smirnov ---
s1 = [1.2, 2.3, 2.8, 3.4, 4.1]
s2 = [0.8, 1.9, 3.0, 4.5, 5.2]
d_stat, p = stats.ks_2samp(s1, s2)
# d_stat=0.40, p=0.873 (not significant)
# --- Permutation test (from scratch) ---
obs_a = np.array([5.2, 6.1, 4.9, 7.3])
obs_b = np.array([3.1, 2.8, 4.0, 3.5])
obs_diff = np.mean(obs_a) - np.mean(obs_b) # 2.525
combined = np.concatenate([obs_a, obs_b])
null = [np.mean(np.random.permutation(combined)[:4]) -
np.mean(np.random.permutation(combined)[4:]) for _ in range(5000)]
p_perm = np.mean(np.abs(null) >= abs(obs_diff)) # ≈ 0.006
