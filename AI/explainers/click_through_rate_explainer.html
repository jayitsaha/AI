Click-Through Rate (CTR) Prediction

From linear scores to boosted trees, deep networks, and multi-task towers
Foundational Recommender Systems Ads / Ranking LR · GBDT · DNN · MTL
1
Understanding CTR Prediction
▼

What is it? Every time an ad, search result, or feed item is shown to a user (an "impression"), a system must estimate the probability that the user will click it — the predicted CTR, \(\hat{p}(\text{click} \mid x)\). This single number drives ad ranking (rank by \(\text{bid} \times \hat{p}(\text{click})\)), budget pacing, and personalized feed ordering. Getting it right (and well-calibrated) is worth billions of dollars a year at scale.

Analogy: Think of a seasoned street vendor sizing up passersby. A simple rule ("well-dressed + walking slowly = likely buyer") is like logistic regression — fast, interpretable, but limited to combinations the vendor thinks to check. A vendor who has learned thousands of little "if-then" heuristics from experience ("if raining AND near lunch AND carrying an umbrella...") behaves like a gradient-boosted tree ensemble. A vendor who has an intuitive, hard-to-verbalize gut feeling built from a lifetime of pattern exposure is like a deep network. And a vendor who simultaneously predicts "will they stop?" and "will they buy if they stop?" to decide who to approach is doing multi-task learning.

💡 If you remember one thing

CTR models all reduce to the same objective — minimize log-loss on binary click labels — but differ in how they turn raw features \(x\) into a probability. The trend over the last decade has been: linear (LR) → additive trees (GBDT) → learned nonlinear crosses (DNN) → shared, multi-objective towers (multi-task), with each stage solving a limitation of the previous one.

2
How the Four Approaches Work Internally
▼

1. Logistic Regression (LR)

The workhorse of ad-serving for a decade. Features (often massively sparse one-hot IDs: user ID, ad ID, publisher, hour-of-day) are combined linearly and squashed through a sigmoid: \(\hat p = \sigma(w \cdot x + b)\). Trained online with algorithms like FTRL-Proximal so the model updates within seconds of new clicks. Fast, interpretable, trivially scalable — but it can only learn effects that are linear in the given features. Feature crosses (e.g. "user likes shoes" × "ad is shoes") must be hand-engineered.

2. Gradient-Boosted Decision Trees (GBDT)

Instead of one linear function, GBDT builds an additive ensemble of shallow decision trees, each one trained to correct the residual errors of all previous trees: \(F_M(x) = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \eta \, h_m(x)\), with the final probability \(\hat p = \sigma(F_M(x))\). Because trees split on thresholds, GBDT discovers feature interactions and nonlinearities automatically — no manual crossing needed — and handles heterogeneous, unscaled tabular features gracefully. It is comparatively weak on ultra-high-cardinality sparse IDs (millions of ad/user IDs), which is where deep models take over.

3. Deep Neural Networks (DNN)

Sparse categorical IDs are mapped to dense learned embeddings, concatenated, and passed through fully-connected layers with nonlinear activations (ReLU). The network learns feature crosses implicitly inside its hidden layers — a hallmark architecture is Wide & Deep (Google, 2016), which keeps a wide linear part for memorization alongside a deep part for generalization; DeepFM and xDeepFM replace the wide part with explicit factorization-machine-style cross terms. DNNs need much more data and compute than LR/GBDT but capture far richer interactions.

4. Multi-Task Models

A single "will they click?" model ignores a crucial downstream signal: what happens after the click (e.g. did they convert / purchase / watch to completion?). Multi-task architectures like MMoE (Multi-gate Mixture-of-Experts) or ESMM (Entire Space Multi-Task Model) share a bottom representation across several prediction heads (towers) — one for CTR, one for CVR (conversion rate given a click) — so that data-rich tasks help data-sparse ones, and the whole pipeline is optimized end-to-end rather than as disconnected models.

🔑 Key Property

Each architecture is a strict generalization in representational power — LR ⊂ GBDT/DNN in what functions of \(x\) they can express — but each also trades away some of LR's speed, interpretability, and online-update simplicity. Production systems often stack them: e.g. GBDT leaves as features fed into an LR (Facebook's "Practical Lessons" paper), or a DNN whose deepest layer feeds multiple task heads.

Why It Works — The Math
▼

Shared Objective: Log-Loss

Every model below is trained to minimize binary cross-entropy over click labels \(y \in \{0,1\}\):

$$\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\Big[y_i \log \hat p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat p_i)\Big]$$

Logistic Regression Gradient

With \(\hat p = \sigma(w\cdot x + b)\), the gradient has the beautifully simple form \(\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial w} = (\hat p - y)\,x\) — this is exactly what FTRL-Proximal updates online, one impression at a time.

GBDT: Fitting the Negative Gradient

Boosting builds each new tree \(h_m\) to approximate the negative gradient (pseudo-residual) of the loss with respect to the current score \(F_{m-1}(x)\):

$$r_i = -\left.\frac{\partial \mathcal L(y_i,\, \sigma(F))}{\partial F}\right|_{F=F_{m-1}(x_i)} = y_i - \hat p_i^{(m-1)}$$

Modern implementations (XGBoost/LightGBM) go one step further and use a second-order (Newton) approximation, so a leaf's optimal value is \(w_j^\star = -\dfrac{\sum_{i \in j} g_i}{\sum_{i\in j} h_i + \lambda}\), where \(g_i,h_i\) are the first/second derivatives of the loss and \(\lambda\) is an L2 regularizer on leaf weights.

DNN Forward Pass

$$a^{(1)} = \text{ReLU}(W^{(1)} e(x) + b^{(1)}), \quad a^{(2)} = \text{ReLU}(W^{(2)} a^{(1)} + b^{(2)}), \quad \hat p = \sigma(w^{(3)\top} a^{(2)} + b^{(3)})$$

where \(e(x)\) is the concatenation of embedding vectors for categorical features. Gradients flow back through every layer and into the embedding tables via standard backpropagation.

Multi-Task: ESMM's Entire-Space Trick

Naively training a CVR model only on clicked impressions suffers from sample selection bias (clicked traffic isn't representative of all impressions) and extreme data sparsity. ESMM instead predicts CTCVR directly as a product of two full-space heads that share the bottom network:

$$\hat p_{\text{CTCVR}} = \hat p_{\text{CTR}}(x) \times \hat p_{\text{CVR}}(x), \qquad \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{CTR}}(y_{click}, \hat p_{CTR}) + \mathcal{L}_{\text{CTCVR}}(y_{conv}, \hat p_{CTCVR})$$

Both losses are computed over all impressions (not just clicked ones), so \(\hat p_{CVR}\) is trained implicitly through the CTCVR loss and never sees the selection bias directly.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

A model can have excellent AUC / ranking quality while being badly miscalibrated (e.g. predicting 30% when the true rate is 8%). Since ad revenue = \(\text{bid} \times \hat p(\text{click})\), miscalibration directly costs money. Always check reliability diagrams / apply Platt scaling or isotonic regression on top of raw model scores before serving.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to reach for each model:

  • Logistic Regression: tight latency budgets, need for online (streaming) updates via FTRL, strong interpretability requirements, or as the final "combiner" layer on top of GBDT-leaf or DNN-embedding features.
  • GBDT (XGBoost/LightGBM/CatBoost): tabular features with moderate cardinality, limited training data, need for automatic feature-interaction discovery without an army of engineers, and strong performance with far less tuning than a DNN.
  • DNN (Wide&Deep, DeepFM, DIN, DCN): huge sparse ID spaces (billions of user/item IDs) where embeddings shine, abundant training data, and willingness to pay extra latency/infra cost for the accuracy gain.
  • Multi-task (MMoE, ESMM, PLE): whenever you care about a downstream event correlated with but distinct from clicks (conversion, watch-time, add-to-cart) — sharing representations reduces data sparsity for the rare downstream label and keeps predictions consistent (CTCVR = CTR × CVR).

Production considerations:

  • Calibration matters as much as ranking quality — always monitor reliability diagrams in production, not just AUC/log-loss offline.
  • Feature/label freshness: click labels are near-instant, but conversion labels can lag hours to days — this delayed-feedback problem is a major reason multi-task/CVR models are hard to get right.
  • Latency budgets often force hybrid designs: precompute embeddings offline, cache GBDT leaf indices, use quantized DNNs, or fall back to an LR "combiner" for the final scoring hop.
💡 Hierarchy

LR (linear score) ⊂ GBDT (additive nonlinear trees) ⊂ DNN (learned dense nonlinear crosses) ⊂ Multi-task DNN (shared representation, multiple correlated objectives). Each layer relaxes an assumption of the one before it, at the cost of more data, compute, and engineering complexity.
