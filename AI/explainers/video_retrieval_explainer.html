Understanding Video Retrieval
▼

What is it? Video retrieval answers "which video matches this sentence?" (text→video) or "which caption describes this clip?" (video→text) — without ever comparing pixels to words directly. Instead, a video encoder and a text encoder are trained so that matching pairs land near each other in one shared numerical space, called an embedding space. Search then becomes: turn the query into a point in that space, and find the closest video points.

Analogy: Imagine a librarian who has watched every video in the library and, for each one, drawn a dot on a giant map based on its "vibe" — not its title, its meaning. When you whisper a sentence, she instantly finds where that sentence's vibe sits on the same map and points at the nearest dots. She never re-reads or re-watches anything at search time — the map (embeddings) was built once, offline.

💡 If you remember one thing

Video retrieval works because a text encoder \(g_\phi\) and a video encoder \(f_\theta\) are trained jointly so that \(\cos(g_\phi(\text{text}), f_\theta(\text{video}))\) is high for true pairs and low otherwise. At search time, retrieval is just $$\arg\max_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \; \frac{q \cdot v}{\|q\|\,\|v\|}$$ — a single dot product per candidate, computed against a pre-built index of millions of videos.

How It Works Internally
▼

Two Towers, One Space

A video retrieval system is a dual-encoder (two-tower) architecture. The text tower is usually a transformer (e.g. CLIP's text transformer) that maps a caption into a single vector \(q \in \mathbb{R}^d\). The video tower first samples \(T\) frames from the clip (uniformly, e.g. \(T{=}8\)–\(12\)), runs each frame through an image encoder (e.g. a CLIP ViT) to get per-frame vectors \(f_1,\dots,f_T \in \mathbb{R}^d\), then aggregates them into one video vector \(v\). Both towers project into the same \(d\)-dimensional space and L2-normalize their outputs so that dot products equal cosine similarities.

Frame Aggregation — the CLIP4Clip design space

The aggregation step is where video retrieval architectures differ most. CLIP4Clip (Luo et al., 2021) studies three variants: (1) Parameter-free — plain mean-pooling of frame embeddings, \(v=\text{mean}(f_1,\dots,f_T)\); zero extra parameters, surprisingly strong. (2) Sequential — feed the frame sequence through an LSTM or GRU and take the final hidden state, capturing order. (3) Tight-type — a temporal transformer (with position embeddings) processes frames jointly with the text via cross-attention before pooling, capturing the richest temporal + cross-modal interaction at the highest compute cost.

Design Decision: Why contrastive, not classification?

Early video-text models used classification heads over a fixed label vocabulary. That caps retrieval to seen categories. Contrastive training instead treats every caption as its own "class," so the model generalizes to any free-form text query at inference — the same reason CLIP works for arbitrary images.

🔑 Key Property

Encoders are decoupled at inference: every video in the gallery is embedded once, offline, and stored in an index. A query only runs the (cheap) text tower, then does a similarity search — never re-running the video tower. This is what makes retrieval scale to millions of videos in milliseconds.

Why It Works — The Math
▼

Cosine Similarity as the Scoring Function

Once both encoders output normalized vectors, similarity is just a dot product:

$$\text{sim}(q, v) = \frac{q \cdot v}{\|q\|\,\|v\|} = \hat{q} \cdot \hat{v} \in [-1, 1]$$

Because \(\hat q,\hat v\) are unit vectors, this is exactly the cosine of the angle between them — identical "meaning direction" gives \(\text{sim}=1\); orthogonal (unrelated) gives \(\text{sim}=0\).

Training Objective: Symmetric InfoNCE

Given a batch of \(N\) matched (text, video) pairs \((q_i, v_i)\), the model is trained so \(q_i\) scores highest against its own \(v_i\) and lowest against every other \(v_j\) in the batch — a softmax classification over the batch, in both directions:

$$\mathcal{L}_{t \to v} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log \frac{\exp(\text{sim}(q_i, v_i)/\tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} \exp(\text{sim}(q_i, v_j)/\tau)}$$

and symmetrically \(\mathcal{L}_{v \to t}\) swapping the roles of query and gallery. The final loss averages both directions:

$$\mathcal{L} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{L}_{t \to v} + \mathcal{L}_{v \to t}\right)$$

\(\tau\) (temperature) is a learned or fixed scalar that sharpens the softmax — small \(\tau\) makes the model punish near-miss negatives harder. Gradients push \(q_i\) and \(v_i\) closer together while pushing \(q_i\) away from every negative \(v_j\) in the batch simultaneously, which is why larger batches (more negatives per step) make contrastive training more sample-efficient.

Retrieval at Inference: Approximate Nearest Neighbors

Exact retrieval computes \(\text{sim}(q, v)\) against every gallery vector — \(O(N \cdot d)\), fine for thousands of videos but too slow for billions. Production systems build an ANN index (e.g. FAISS with HNSW or IVF-PQ) over the pre-computed video vectors, trading a small amount of recall for sub-linear (often \(O(\log N)\) or better, amortized) query time.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

In-batch negatives are only approximate negatives — a batch might accidentally contain two videos that are actually near-duplicates or semantically similar, which the loss will incorrectly push apart. This is why some systems mine hard negatives explicitly, or filter near-duplicate batches, rather than trusting random batch composition alone.

Key Takeaways
▼

Aggregation choice matters:

  • Mean-pooling (parameter-free) — fast, no extra params, strong baseline when actions don't depend on order
  • Sequential (LSTM/GRU) — captures temporal order, more parameters, can overfit on small datasets
  • Tight-type transformer — best accuracy, cross-attends text with frames, but far more expensive at index-build time

When to use text↔video retrieval:

  • Searching large, unlabeled video libraries with free-form natural language
  • Video moment/clip search, content moderation triage, recommendation candidate generation
  • Zero-shot classification of video clips against a set of candidate captions
💡 Hierarchy

Image-text (CLIP) ⊂ Video-text, mean-pooled (CLIP4Clip parameter-free) ⊂ Video-text, temporal-aware (CLIP4Clip seq/tight, X-CLIP, InternVideo). Each level adds temporal modeling on top of a frozen or fine-tuned image-text backbone, at increasing compute cost.
