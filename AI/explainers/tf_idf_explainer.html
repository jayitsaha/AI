TF-IDF: Term Frequency, Inverse Document Frequency

Scoring how important a word is to a document within a corpus
Foundational NLP Information Retrieval O(N·V)
1
Understanding TF-IDF
▼

What is it? TF-IDF is a numeric score that tells you how important a word is to one document, relative to a whole collection (corpus) of documents. It combines two opposing signals: how often a word appears in a document (term frequency), and how rare that word is across all documents (inverse document frequency). A high TF-IDF score means a word shows up a lot in this document but rarely elsewhere — a strong signature of what the document is actually about.

Analogy: Imagine reading letters from pen pals. The word "the" appears in every letter — it tells you nothing about any particular one. But if only one friend keeps writing "kayak," that word instantly identifies their letter. TF-IDF is a formal way of saying "boring, universal words score near zero; rare, distinctive words score high" — exactly the intuition a human reader applies without thinking.

💡 If you remember one thing

TF-IDF multiplies "how often" by "how rare": $$\text{tfidf}(t,d) = \text{tf}(t,d) \times \text{idf}(t)$$ A word that is frequent in a document but also frequent across the whole corpus (like "the") gets driven toward zero, because \(\text{idf}(t) \to 0\) as the word appears in more and more documents.

2
How TF-IDF Works Internally
▼

Step 1 — Term Frequency (TF)

For a term \(t\) and document \(d\), raw term frequency is just a count: how many times \(t\) appears in \(d\). Because longer documents naturally contain more occurrences of everything, we normalize by document length: \(\text{tf}(t,d) = \frac{\text{count}(t,d)}{|d|}\), where \(|d|\) is the total token count of \(d\). This puts short and long documents on a comparable scale.

Step 2 — Document Frequency (DF) and Inverse Document Frequency (IDF)

Document frequency \(\text{df}(t)\) counts how many documents in the corpus contain \(t\) at least once (not how many times total — just presence/absence). A term appearing in every document is uninformative for distinguishing documents; a term appearing in only one is highly distinguishing. IDF inverts and log-compresses this count so common terms are penalized and rare terms are boosted.

Step 3 — Combine

Multiplying tf by idf rewards words that are locally frequent (they matter to this document) and globally rare (they aren't just corpus-wide filler). This is why "the" or "and" — present in nearly every document — collapse to a TF-IDF near zero, while a domain word like "kayak" or "birds" that shows up in only one document keeps a large, sharp score.

🔑 Design Decision: Why the logarithm in IDF?

Without a log, document frequency ratios would dominate the score with huge swings between "rare" and "common" terms, and IDF would scale linearly with corpus size \(N\). The logarithm compresses this into a smoother, sublinear penalty — going from \(\text{df}=1\) to \(\text{df}=2\) matters a lot, but going from \(\text{df}=1000\) to \(\text{df}=1001\) barely moves the score. This mirrors how information content itself scales logarithmically (think Shannon entropy).

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

The Formulas

For a corpus of \(N\) documents, term \(t\), document \(d\):

$$\text{tf}(t,d) = \frac{\text{count}(t,d)}{|d|} \qquad \text{idf}(t) = \ln\!\left(\frac{N}{\text{df}(t)}\right) \qquad \text{tfidf}(t,d) = \text{tf}(t,d)\cdot\text{idf}(t)$$

Notice the boundary cases. If \(t\) occurs in every document, \(\text{df}(t) = N\), so \(\text{idf}(t) = \ln(1) = 0\), forcing \(\text{tfidf}(t,d)=0\) for every document regardless of how often \(t\) appears locally. If \(t\) occurs in only one document out of \(N\), \(\text{idf}(t) = \ln(N)\), the maximum possible value — the rarest words get the largest multiplier.

Interpreting the Scale

As \(\text{df}(t) \to N\), \(\text{idf}(t) \to 0\) — the term becomes worthless for discrimination. As \(\text{df}(t) \to 1\), \(\text{idf}(t) \to \ln N\) — the term becomes maximally discriminating. Because \(\ln\) is monotonically increasing, IDF preserves the ranking "rarer is more informative" while compressing the raw ratio \(N/\text{df}(t)\) into a gentler curve.

Vector Space Interpretation

In practice, every document \(d\) becomes a vector in \(\mathbb{R}^{|V|}\) (one dimension per vocabulary term \(V\)), with each coordinate equal to \(\text{tfidf}(t,d)\). Documents can then be compared with cosine similarity: \(\cos(\theta) = \frac{d_1 \cdot d_2}{\|d_1\|\|d_2\|}\), which is the backbone of classic search engines and document clustering before dense embeddings existed.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

Plain \(\text{idf}(t)=\ln(N/\text{df}(t))\) is undefined (division by zero avoided, but zero-score) issues arise for terms absent from the training corpus at inference time, and it can spike to \(\ln(N)\) for a term seen in just one training document. Production libraries (e.g. scikit-learn) use smoothed IDF: \(\text{idf}(t) = \ln\!\left(\frac{1+N}{1+\text{df}(t)}\right) + 1\), which avoids division-by-zero and never fully zeroes out a term.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use TF-IDF:

  • Classic search/retrieval — ranking documents against a keyword query (BM25 is a refinement)
  • Fast, interpretable baseline features for text classification (e.g. spam filters, topic tagging)
  • Keyword extraction — the highest-scoring terms per document summarize what it's "about"
  • Document similarity / clustering when you need something cheap and explainable, no GPU required

When NOT to use:

  • When word order or syntax matters — TF-IDF is a bag-of-words model, it discards sequence
  • When you need semantic similarity ("car" vs "automobile") — dense embeddings capture meaning TF-IDF can't
  • Very short documents/queries — sparse vectors with few nonzero terms give unstable rankings
💡 Hierarchy

Raw counts (ignore length) → TF (length-normalized) → TF-IDF (also corpus-rarity-weighted) → BM25 (TF-IDF with saturation + length normalization tuning) → dense embeddings (learned semantic vectors). Each step trades interpretability and cost for better ranking quality.

Code Trace
▼
import numpy as np
from collections import Counter
docs = [
"the cat sat on the mat",
"the dog chased the cat",
"birds fly in the sky",
"the cat and the dog are friends",
]
tokenized = [d.split() for d in docs]
N = len(docs)
def tf(term, tokens):
return tokens.count(term) / len(tokens)
def df(term, tokenized):
return sum(1 for toks in tokenized if term in toks)
def idf(term, tokenized):
return np.log(len(tokenized) / df(term, tokenized))
def tfidf(term, tokens, tokenized):
return tf(term, tokens) * idf(term, tokenized)
# Score every doc for term "cat"
for i, toks in enumerate(tokenized):
print(i, tfidf("cat", toks, tokenized))
# Production usage: scikit-learn
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
vec = TfidfVectorizer()
X = vec.fit_transform(docs) # sparse (N_docs, |vocab|) matrix
print(vec.get_feature_names_out())
