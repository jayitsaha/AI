Why large transformer/LLM training runs spike, diverge, or NaN — and how to keep them stable

1
Understanding Training Instability
▼

What is it? Training instability is when a large model's loss stops smoothly decreasing and instead spikes upward, oscillates, or turns into NaN/Inf — sometimes recovering on its own after a few hundred steps, sometimes destroying the run entirely. It became a headline problem once models grew past a few billion parameters: GPT-3, OPT-175B, PaLM, BLOOM, and Meta's OPT logbook all documented mid-training loss spikes that engineers had to detect and fix by hand.

Analogy: Think of training as driving a car down a winding mountain road (the loss landscape) at a fixed speed (the learning rate). On straight, gentle curves you cruise fine. But hit a suddenly sharp hairpin (a spike in local curvature — caused by things like attention logits or activation values blowing up) while going too fast, and you fly off the road (the loss explodes) instead of following the bend. Slow down in advance (warmup), install guardrails (gradient clipping), and widen the road (better normalization/architecture) and the same speed becomes safe.

💡 If you remember one thing

Instability is a mismatch between the step size your optimizer takes and the local curvature of the loss surface at that moment. Curvature is not constant during LLM training — it spikes unpredictably — so a learning rate that was safe for 10,000 steps can suddenly become unsafe at step 10,001. Every remedy either lowers the effective step size, caps it reactively, or flattens the curvature itself.

2
How Instability Arises Internally
▼

Where instability actually comes from

Four mechanisms dominate in practice, and they usually compound:

1. Learning rate vs. curvature mismatch. The optimizer takes a step \(\Delta w = -\eta \cdot g\). If the local curvature \(c\) of the loss surface is high (a sharp bowl), the same \(\eta\) that was safe elsewhere now overshoots the minimum and bounces to a worse point — repeated overshoots compound into a visible spike.

2. Under-warmed optimizer statistics. Adam/AdamW maintain running estimates of the gradient's first and second moments (\(m_t, v_t\)). In the first few hundred steps these estimates are noisy and biased toward zero, so the bias-corrected step size can transiently overshoot — this is exactly why warmup exists: it throttles \(\eta_t\) until the moment estimates stabilize.

3. Numerical range failures in mixed precision. fp16 has a tiny dynamic range (max ≈ 65504, and denormals underflow near zero). Activations or gradients that grow large — common in deep transformer stacks — overflow to Inf, and the very next backward pass produces NaN. bf16 keeps fp32's exponent range so it doesn't overflow, but it trades away mantissa precision.

4. Architectural amplifiers. Un-normalized attention logits, growing residual-stream norms in very deep pre-LN stacks, and large embedding/logit magnitudes all act as curvature multipliers — they make mechanism (1) worse. This is why "spiky" training correlates with model depth and width, not just learning rate.

🔑 Key Property

Every practical fix falls into exactly one of three buckets: reduce the effective step size (warmup, LR decay, lower peak LR), cap the step reactively (gradient clipping, loss-scale backoff), or flatten the curvature itself (QK-norm, z-loss, better init/normalization). Understanding which bucket a remedy is in tells you which failure mode it actually addresses.

3
Why It Works — The Math of Stability
▼

The linear-stability condition

Reduce the problem to a scalar quadratic loss \(L(w) = \tfrac12 c w^2\) with curvature \(c>0\) (a local second-order approximation of the true loss around the current point). Gradient descent gives:

$$w_{t+1} = w_t - \eta \cdot c \cdot w_t = (1 - \eta c)\, w_t$$

This is a linear recurrence. It converges to 0 only if \(|1 - \eta c| < 1\), i.e.:

$$0 < \eta < \frac{2}{c}$$

If \(\eta c > 2\), \(w_t\) grows without bound — every step overshoots further than the last. This is precisely why loss spikes correlate with sudden curvature increases \(c\): a fixed \(\eta\) that satisfied \(\eta c < 2\) yesterday can violate it the instant \(c\) jumps (e.g. from an attention-entropy collapse), producing a visible divergent spike in the loss curve.

Gradient clipping as a safety valve

Clipping rescales the gradient (or its global norm across all parameters) whenever it exceeds a threshold \(\tau\):

$$g_t^{clip} = g_t \cdot \min\!\left(1, \frac{\tau}{\|g_t\|}\right)$$

This does not fix the underlying curvature spike, but it bounds the step magnitude so \(w_{t+1}\) cannot fly arbitrarily far — trading a clean convergence guarantee for a much smaller worst-case excursion.

Loss scaling in fp16

To keep small gradients from underflowing fp16's representable range, the loss is multiplied by a scale factor \(S\) before backprop (gradients scale linearly with it), then divided out before the optimizer step: \(g = \frac{1}{S}\nabla(S \cdot L)\). Dynamic loss scaling grows \(S\) when no overflow is seen and halves it immediately after one — this is why fp16 training logs show periodic scale-factor drops correlated with skipped steps.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

Gradient clipping and loss scaling are reactive — they contain damage after curvature has already spiked. They do not prevent the spike itself. Teams that rely on clipping alone still see loss "notches" (brief upward jumps) even though the run never fully diverges; eliminating the notches requires reducing curvature growth (better normalization, QK-norm, smaller init) or lowering the effective LR near risky regions.

Key Takeaways
▼

Diagnose instability by watching:

  • Global gradient norm — a sudden multi-x jump usually precedes a loss spike by 1-2 steps
  • Loss-scale factor in fp16 logs — frequent halving means you're near the overflow boundary
  • Per-layer activation/logit magnitudes — growth concentrated in a few layers points at architecture, not LR

Remedy toolbox, roughly in order of adoption:

  • Warmup + cosine/linear decay LR schedule (throttles early Adam-statistics noise)
  • Global gradient-norm clipping, typically \(\tau \approx 1.0\)
  • bf16 (or fp16 + dynamic loss scaling) mixed precision
  • Pre-LN / RMSNorm placement, QK-norm on attention logits, z-loss on final logits
  • Checkpoint-and-rollback: detect a spike, restart a few hundred steps back with a lower LR or a reshuffled batch (used by PaLM and OPT)
💡 Hierarchy

Prevent (architecture, init, warmup) \(\rightarrow\) bound (clipping, loss scaling) \(\rightarrow\) recover (checkpoint rollback). Modern large-scale recipes stack all three layers rather than relying on any single fix — instability in trillion-token runs is treated as inevitable, not exceptional.
