Capabilities that appear suddenly in large language models, and how to unlock them

1
Understanding Emergent Abilities
▼

What are they? Emergent abilities are capabilities of large language models (LLMs) that are not present in smaller models but appear suddenly as model scale (number of parameters, training data, compute) increases. Their performance is often flat or random until a "critical scale" is reached, after which they show a sharp, non-linear improvement. These abilities are not explicitly programmed but arise from the model's vast knowledge and pattern recognition acquired during pre-training.

Analogy: Imagine teaching a child to read. For a long time, they might struggle, recognizing individual letters but not forming words. Then, seemingly overnight, they "emerge" with the ability to read fluently. The underlying cognitive machinery was always there, but it required a certain threshold of experience and data to "click" into a new, higher-level capability. Similarly, LLMs, once sufficiently scaled, can suddenly perform tasks like complex reasoning or code generation that were impossible for slightly smaller versions.

💡 If you remember one thing

Emergent abilities are observed when performance on a task is much worse than random chance for small models, but rapidly improves to above-random performance once a certain model scale is exceeded. They are a hallmark of scaling laws in deep learning, indicating a qualitative shift in model behavior.

2
How Emergent Abilities Manifest
▼

The Scaling Hypothesis

The core idea behind emergent abilities is the scaling hypothesis: as LLMs are trained on more data with more parameters, their internal representations become richer and more capable of capturing complex patterns. This doesn't mean a linear improvement in all tasks; rather, certain tasks require a minimum "cognitive capacity" to be solvable at all. Once this capacity is met, the ability "switches on."

Three Key Emergent Abilities

While many abilities emerge, three highly impactful ones are:

  1. In-Context Learning (ICL): The ability to learn a new task from a few examples provided directly in the prompt, without any weight updates. The model adapts its behavior based on the input sequence structure.
  2. Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Reasoning: The ability to break down complex problems into intermediate, explicit reasoning steps. Simply adding "Let's think step by step" to a prompt can dramatically improve performance on multi-step reasoning tasks (e.g., arithmetic, logical puzzles).
  3. Tool Use: The ability to identify when an external tool (e.g., a calculator, a search engine, a code interpreter) is needed, generate the correct API call, execute it, and integrate the tool's output back into its reasoning process to produce a final answer.

These abilities are not always distinct; CoT can be seen as an internal tool, and tool use often requires CoT to plan and execute calls effectively.

Design Decision: Why "Emergent" and not just "Improved"?

The term "emergent" is used because the performance curve often shows a sudden, sharp increase from near-random to significantly above-random, rather than a gradual, linear improvement. This suggests a qualitative shift in how the model processes information, rather than merely doing the same thing "better." It implies a phase transition in capability, often linked to the model's ability to generalize or abstract at a higher level.

🔑 Key Property

Emergent abilities are not directly trained for; they are properties that arise from the scaling of general-purpose unsupervised pre-training. They highlight that LLMs are not just sophisticated pattern matchers but can exhibit forms of reasoning and adaptation.

3
Formalizing Emergence and Its Drivers
▼

The Scaling Law Perspective

Emergent abilities are often described by scaling laws, which relate model performance \(L\) to scale factors like model size \(N\), dataset size \(D\), and compute \(C\). A typical scaling law might look like:

$$L(N, D, C) = \left( \frac{N_0}{N} \right)^{\alpha_N} + \left( \frac{D_0}{D} \right)^{\alpha_D} + \left( \frac{C_0}{C} \right)^{\alpha_C} + L_{min}$$

where \(L_{min}\) is an irreducible loss. For emergent abilities, the performance metric \(P\) (e.g., accuracy) might be defined as \(P = 1 - L\), but the "emergence" part is that for specific tasks, \(P\) remains near 0 until \(N\) (or \(D\), \(C\)) crosses a threshold. This non-linearity is what makes them "emergent."

In-Context Learning as Pattern Completion

ICL can be viewed as the model learning a "meta-task" from the prompt. Given a sequence of \(k\) input-output pairs \(\{(x_1, y_1), \dots, (x_k, y_k)\}\) and a new input \(x_{k+1}\), the model generates \(y_{k+1}\). Mathematically, this is about finding the conditional probability:

$$P(y_{k+1} \mid x_1, y_1, \dots, x_k, y_k, x_{k+1})$$

The model learns to infer the underlying task from the examples and apply it to the new input, all within the forward pass. This is distinct from traditional fine-tuning where weights are updated.

Chain-of-Thought Reasoning: Decomposing Complexity

CoT reasoning can be seen as a method to transform a complex problem into a sequence of simpler, more manageable sub-problems. For a problem \(Q\) with a direct answer \(A\), CoT introduces intermediate steps \(S = \{s_1, s_2, \dots, s_m\}\):

$$P(A, S \mid Q) = P(A \mid S, Q) \cdot P(S \mid Q)$$

By explicitly generating \(S\), the model leverages its ability to generate coherent text to guide its own reasoning, effectively increasing the "effective depth" of its computation and improving the probability of finding the correct \(A\).

⚠️ Not True Intelligence (Yet)

While impressive, emergent abilities do not necessarily imply true understanding or consciousness. They are sophisticated statistical correlations and pattern completions. The "emergence" might simply be a measurement artifact where a task's success metric only registers above a certain threshold of internal capability, making the improvement appear sudden.

Key Takeaways

    
▼

  

  

    
Implications of Emergent Abilities:

    
    
      
  • Scaling is Key: Many advanced capabilities are simply not present in smaller models, regardless of clever prompting. Raw scale (parameters, data, compute) is a prerequisite.
    • 
      
  • Prompt Engineering is Powerful: Even with large models, how you phrase the prompt (e.g., few-shot examples, "think step by step") can unlock or significantly boost these abilities.
    • 
      
  • Hybrid AI Systems: Tool use highlights the future of AI: not just a single monolithic model, but intelligent agents that know when and how to leverage external resources.
    • 
      
  • Unpredictability: The non-linear nature of emergence makes it hard to predict what new capabilities will appear with the next generation of larger models.
    • 
    

    
Challenges and Future Directions:

    
    
      
  • Understanding Mechanisms: The exact mechanisms behind emergence are still not fully understood. Is it a true qualitative shift or an artifact of evaluation?
    • 
      
  • Controlling Emergence: How can we reliably elicit and control these abilities, especially for safety-critical applications?
    • 
      
  • Efficiency: Running large models for emergent abilities is computationally expensive. Research into more efficient architectures and methods is ongoing.
    • 
    

    

      
💡 The Future of LLMs

      
Emergent abilities underscore that LLMs are not just interpolating their training data. They are developing internal representations that allow for complex reasoning, adaptation, and interaction with the external world, pushing the boundaries of what purely data-driven systems can achieve.

apple -> pomme

      
house -> maison

      
cat -> """

      
response = model.generate(prompt_icl)

      
# Expected: "chat" (model infers task from examples)

prompt_cot = """Q: If a baker made 20 cookies, and sold 12, then baked 10 more, how many does he have now?

      
Let's think step by step."""

      
response = model.generate(prompt_cot)

      
# Expected: "The baker started with 20 cookies. He sold 12, so 20 - 12 = 8 cookies. Then he baked 10 more, so 8 + 10 = 18 cookies. The final answer is 18."
