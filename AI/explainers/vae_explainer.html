1
Understanding β-VAE
▼

What is it? A Variational Autoencoder (VAE) compresses data \(x\) into a latent code \(z\) using an encoder \(q(z|x)\), then reconstructs \(x\) from \(z\) using a decoder \(p(x|z)\). A β-VAE is the same machine with one dial added: it multiplies the KL-divergence penalty in the training objective by a constant \(\beta\). Turning that dial up forces the encoder's latent distributions to squeeze closer to the prior \(p(z)=\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) — and, remarkably, this pressure tends to make each latent dimension track one independent, human-interpretable factor of variation (e.g. rotation, or object scale) instead of a tangled mixture of several.

Analogy: Imagine describing a face using a control panel of sliders — one for age, one for smile, one for hair length. A plain autoencoder is free to wire the sliders however is most convenient for copying the photo exactly, so one slider might secretly control "age AND smile AND lighting" at once. β-VAE puts a tax on using slider capacity at all (the KL term), so the network can only afford to keep the sliders that carry real, independent information — and it is cheapest to make each surviving slider do exactly one clean job.

One-sentence summary: β-VAE disentangles representations by over-penalizing the KL term in the ELBO, pushing the approximate posterior toward the isotropic, factorized prior and thereby rewarding statistically independent, axis-aligned latent codes.

💡 If you remember one thing

The β-VAE objective is the standard ELBO with the KL term reweighted: $$\mathcal{L}_\beta = \mathbb{E}_{q(z|x)}[\log p(x|z)] - \beta \cdot D_{KL}\big(q(z|x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)$$ \(\beta=1\) recovers the ordinary VAE; \(\beta>1\) trades reconstruction sharpness for disentanglement and interpretability.

2
How β-VAE Works Internally
▼

The Machinery

The encoder network outputs \(\mu(x)\) and \(\log\sigma^2(x)\) for a diagonal Gaussian \(q(z|x)=\mathcal{N}(\mu(x),\operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2(x)))\). A sample is drawn via the reparameterization trick \(z=\mu(x)+\sigma(x)\odot\varepsilon,\ \varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\), which keeps the sampling step differentiable. The decoder maps \(z\) back to \(\hat{x}\), and the two loss terms — reconstruction error and \(D_{KL}(q(z|x)\|p(z))\) — are backpropagated jointly. β multiplies only the KL term, so during training it scales exactly one gradient pressure: the pull of every latent dimension toward matching the standard normal prior in both mean (→0) and variance (→1).

Design Decision: Why Weight Only the KL Term?

Higgins et al. (2017) motivate β-VAE as a Lagrangian relaxation of a constrained optimization problem: maximize reconstruction likelihood subject to a hard cap on representational capacity, \(D_{KL}(q(z|x)\|p(z)) < \epsilon\). Introducing a Lagrange multiplier \(\beta \ge 0\) for that inequality constraint turns the constrained problem into the unconstrained β-ELBO above — \(\beta\) is literally the shadow price of latent "information budget." Larger \(\beta\) means a tighter budget, so the encoder can only afford to spend bits on the factors that reduce reconstruction error the most, and it is cheapest (lowest KL per unit of error reduction) to encode each such factor along its own separate axis, because the isotropic prior imposes no preferred rotation — independence is the path of least resistance once capacity is scarce.

🔑 Key Property

β does not directly reward "disentanglement" — it rewards minimal KL per dimension. Disentanglement is an emergent side-effect: when the model must spend its scarce KL budget efficiently and the true generative factors are (close to) statistically independent, axis-aligned coding is the cheapest way to satisfy the budget.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

From Constrained Optimization to the β-ELBO

Start from Higgins et al.'s constrained objective and its Lagrangian (KKT form, \(\beta\ge0\), constant \(\epsilon\) dropped since it does not affect the argmax):

$$\max_{\phi,\theta}\ \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z|x)}[\log p_\theta(x|z)]\quad \text{s.t.}\quad D_{KL}\big(q_\phi(z|x)\,\|\,p(z)\big) < \epsilon \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \mathcal{L}_\beta = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}[\log p_\theta(x|z)] - \beta D_{KL}\big(q_\phi(z|x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)$$

Closed Form for a Linear-Gaussian Channel

To see exactly how β reshapes the posterior, solve the toy case explicitly: prior \(p(z)=\mathcal{N}(0,1)\), likelihood \(p(x|z)=\mathcal{N}(z,\sigma^2)\). For fixed \(x\), the function \(q\) that maximizes \(\mathcal{L}_\beta(q)=\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x|z)] - \beta D_{KL}(q\|p)\) over all densities \(q\) is found via calculus of variations. Rewriting \(D_{KL}\) and collecting terms under the entropy of \(q\):

$$\mathcal{L}_\beta(q) = \mathbb{E}_q\Big[\log p(x|z) + \beta\log p(z) - \beta\log q(z)\Big]$$

This is maximized by the annealed (\("\)tempered\("\)) posterior \(q^*(z) \propto p(z)\cdot p(x|z)^{1/\beta}\). Substituting the Gaussians, \(p(x|z)^{1/\beta}\propto\mathcal{N}(z; x,\beta\sigma^2)\) as a function of \(z\), so \(q^*\) is the product of two Gaussians — itself Gaussian by conjugacy, with precision-weighted mean:

$$\mu^*=\frac{x}{\beta\sigma^2+1}, \qquad v^*=\frac{\beta\sigma^2}{\beta\sigma^2+1}$$

This is the exact formula used in Card 2's numeric trace and in the live visualization below. As \(\beta\to0\): \(\mu^*\to x\), \(v^*\to0\) — the posterior collapses to a delta at the true value (perfect, unregularized encoding, like a plain autoencoder). As \(\beta\to\infty\): \(\mu^*\to0\), \(v^*\to1\) — the posterior collapses onto the prior, discarding all information about \(x\) ("posterior collapse").

KL and Reconstruction in Closed Form

With \(q=\mathcal{N}(\mu^*,v^*)\) and prior \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\):

$$D_{KL}(q\|p) = \tfrac12\big(v^* + (\mu^*)^2 - 1 - \ln v^*\big), \qquad \mathbb{E}_q\big[-\log p(x|z)\big] \propto \frac{(x-\mu^*)^2 + v^*}{2\sigma^2}$$
⚠️ Common Misconception

"\(\beta>1\) guarantees disentanglement" — false. β only guarantees pressure toward a factorized, isotropic posterior. Whether that pressure yields axis-aligned semantic factors also depends on the true generative factors actually being close to independent and on decoder capacity being well matched. Push β too high and every dimension in the trace above marches toward \((\mu,v)=(0,1)\) regardless of \(x\) — total posterior collapse, not disentanglement.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to increase β:

  • You need interpretable, independently-controllable latent axes for downstream editing or analysis
  • You want to measure or improve disentanglement (e.g. for fairness, causal factor discovery)
  • Your true generative factors are plausibly close to statistically independent

When NOT to push β too high:

  • Pure sample-fidelity / generation-quality tasks — high β blurs reconstructions and can fully collapse weak factors
  • Factors are strongly correlated in the data — there is no clean axis-aligned solution to find
  • Consider capacity annealing (Burgess et al. 2018, "Understanding disentangling in β-VAE") — ramp a target KL capacity \(C\) up over training instead of fixing β, to disentangle without starving reconstruction
💡 Hierarchy

Plain Autoencoder (\(\beta=0\), no KL pressure, unstructured latents) ⊂ Standard VAE (\(\beta=1\), valid ELBO, balanced) ⊂ β-VAE (\(\beta>1\), disentanglement pressure, degraded reconstruction) ⊂ Total collapse (\(\beta\to\infty\), \(q(z|x)\to p(z)\), latents carry zero information).

Code Trace
▼
import torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F
class BetaVAE(nn.Module):
def __init__(self, in_dim, z_dim, beta=4.0):
super().__init__()
self.beta = beta
self.enc = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(in_dim, 128), nn.ReLU())
self.mu_head = nn.Linear(128, z_dim)
self.logvar_head = nn.Linear(128, z_dim)
self.dec = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(z_dim, 128), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(128, in_dim))
def reparameterize(self, mu, logvar):
std = torch.exp(0.5 * logvar) # sigma(x)
eps = torch.randn_like(std) # epsilon ~ N(0, I)
return mu + eps * std # z = mu + sigma * epsilon
def forward(self, x):
h = self.enc(x)
mu, logvar = self.mu_head(h), self.logvar_head(h)
z = self.reparameterize(mu, logvar)
x_hat = self.dec(z)
return x_hat, mu, logvar
def loss(self, x):
x_hat, mu, logvar = self.forward(x)
recon = F.mse_loss(x_hat, x, reduction='sum') / x.size(0)
# closed-form KL(N(mu,var) || N(0,I)) per dim, summed
kl = -0.5 * torch.sum(1 + logvar - mu.pow(2) - logvar.exp())
kl = kl / x.size(0)
return recon + self.beta * kl, recon, kl # beta scales ONLY the KL term
