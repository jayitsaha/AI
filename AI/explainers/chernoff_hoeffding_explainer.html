Understanding Concentration Inequalities
▼

What are concentration inequalities? When you average many random measurements, the average tends to be close to the true mean — but how close, and with what probability? Concentration inequalities give you precise guarantees: they bound the probability that a random variable deviates far from its expected value. They are the mathematical backbone of machine learning generalization theory, randomized algorithms, and statistical sampling.

Analogy — Opinion Polls: A pollster asks \(n\) randomly chosen voters their preference. Each voter is a random variable; the poll average estimates the true population proportion \(\mu\). A concentration inequality tells you: "With 95% confidence, your poll estimate is within ±3 percentage points of the truth." This is exactly what Hoeffding's inequality computes, rigorously.

Why a hierarchy of bounds? Different bounds make different assumptions and achieve different tightness. Markov only needs the mean; Chebyshev also needs the variance; Chernoff and Hoeffding exploit the full distributional structure (moment-generating function / boundedness) to get exponentially smaller tails — far tighter in practice.

💡 If you remember one thing

Hoeffding's inequality says: for \(n\) independent bounded random variables \(X_i \in [a_i, b_i]\), the sample mean \(\bar{X}\) satisfies $$P(|\bar{X} - \mu| \geq t) \leq 2\exp\!\left(\frac{-2n^2 t^2}{\sum_i (b_i - a_i)^2}\right).$$ The tail decays exponentially in \(n\) — double your sample size and the error probability squares. This is exponentially better than Chebyshev's \(1/(nt^2)\) polynomial decay.

The Hierarchy: Markov → Chebyshev → Chernoff → Hoeffding
▼

1. Markov's Inequality (weakest — needs only mean)

For any non-negative random variable \(X\) with finite mean \(\mu = \mathbb{E}[X]\) and any threshold \(t > 0\):

$$P(X \geq t) \leq \frac{\mu}{t}$$

Proof sketch: \(\mu = \mathbb{E}[X] \geq \mathbb{E}[X \cdot \mathbf{1}_{X \geq t}] \geq t \cdot P(X \geq t)\). Rearrange. The proof is a single line — its simplicity is both its strength and its weakness. Example: if \(\mu = 0.5\) and \(t = 0.8\), Markov gives \(P(X \geq 0.8) \leq 0.625\) — barely informative.

2. Chebyshev's Inequality (needs mean + variance)

For any random variable \(X\) with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), for any \(t > 0\):

$$P(|X - \mu| \geq t) \leq \frac{\sigma^2}{t^2}$$

Apply Markov to \((X-\mu)^2\) (which is non-negative) with threshold \(t^2\). For the sample mean of \(n\) i.i.d. variables, \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar{X}) = \sigma^2/n\), so \(P(|\bar{X}-\mu| \geq t) \leq \sigma^2/(nt^2)\). For Uniform\([0,1]\): \(\sigma^2 = 1/12\), so at \(n=10,\, t=0.2\): bound \(= \frac{1/12}{10 \cdot 0.04} \approx 0.208\). The bound shrinks as \(1/n\) — polynomially.

3. Chernoff Bounds (MGF-based — exponential tails)

The key trick: for any \(s > 0\) and any random variable \(X\):

$$P(X \geq t) = P(e^{sX} \geq e^{st}) \leq \frac{\mathbb{E}[e^{sX}]}{e^{st}}$$

by Markov applied to \(e^{sX}\). Now minimize over \(s > 0\) to get the tightest bound. The function \(M_X(s) = \mathbb{E}[e^{sX}]\) is the moment-generating function (MGF). If \(M_X(s)\) is finite and small near \(s=0\), the exponential decay in \(t\) is guaranteed. For a sum \(S_n = X_1+\cdots+X_n\) of independent variables, \(M_{S_n}(s) = \prod_i M_{X_i}(s)\), which enables tight product bounds.

4. Hoeffding's Inequality (bounded r.v.s — cleanest closed form)

Hoeffding's Lemma is the key building block: if \(X \in [a, b]\) with \(\mathbb{E}[X] = 0\), then for all \(s \in \mathbb{R}\):

$$\mathbb{E}[e^{sX}] \leq \exp\!\left(\frac{s^2(b-a)^2}{8}\right)$$

This shows bounded variables are sub-Gaussian with parameter \(\sigma^2 = (b-a)^2/4\). Applying the Chernoff trick to the sum and optimizing over \(s\) yields Hoeffding's inequality in its most useful form. For \(n\) i.i.d. variables in \([a,b]\):

$$P\!\left(|\bar{X} - \mu| \geq t\right) \leq 2\exp\!\left(\frac{-2nt^2}{(b-a)^2}\right)$$
🔑 Sub-Gaussian Interpretation

A random variable \(X\) is sub-Gaussian with parameter \(\sigma^2\) if \(\mathbb{E}[e^{s(X-\mu)}] \leq e^{s^2\sigma^2/2}\) for all \(s\). Hoeffding's Lemma shows any bounded \(X \in [a,b]\) is sub-Gaussian with \(\sigma^2 = (b-a)^2/4\). This is a stronger condition than finite variance: sub-Gaussian tails decay like a Gaussian, not polynomially.

Full Derivation of Hoeffding's Inequality

Setup: Let \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) be independent with \(X_i \in [a_i, b_i]\) and \(\mathbb{E}[X_i] = \mu_i\). Let \(\bar{X} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i X_i\) and \(\mu = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \mu_i\). We bound \(P(\bar{X} - \mu \geq t)\).

Step 1 — Chernoff trick: For any \(s > 0\), by Markov on the non-negative r.v. \(e^{s(\bar{X}-\mu)}\):

$$P(\bar{X} - \mu \geq t) = P\!\left(e^{s(\bar{X}-\mu)} \geq e^{st}\right) \leq \frac{\mathbb{E}[e^{s(\bar{X}-\mu)}]}{e^{st}}$$

Step 2 — Factorize via independence:

$$\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{s(\bar{X}-\mu)}\right] = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\prod_{i=1}^n e^{\frac{s}{n}(X_i - \mu_i)}\right] = \prod_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\frac{s}{n}(X_i-\mu_i)}\right]$$

Step 3 — Apply Hoeffding's Lemma to each term. Since \(X_i - \mu_i \in [a_i-\mu_i,\, b_i-\mu_i]\) with mean 0:

$$\mathbb{E}\!\left[e^{\frac{s}{n}(X_i-\mu_i)}\right] \leq \exp\!\left(\frac{(s/n)^2(b_i-a_i)^2}{8}\right)$$

Step 4 — Combine:

$$P(\bar{X}-\mu \geq t) \leq e^{-st}\prod_{i=1}^n \exp\!\left(\frac{s^2(b_i-a_i)^2}{8n^2}\right) = \exp\!\left(-st + \frac{s^2}{8n^2}\sum_i(b_i-a_i)^2\right)$$

Step 5 — Optimize over \(s\): The exponent \(f(s) = -st + \frac{s^2 C}{8n^2}\) where \(C = \sum_i(b_i-a_i)^2\) is minimized at \(s^* = \frac{4n^2 t}{C}\). Substituting:

$$P(\bar{X}-\mu \geq t) \leq \exp\!\left(\frac{-2n^2 t^2}{\sum_i(b_i-a_i)^2}\right)$$

Step 6 — Two-sided bound by union bound (apply once for upper, once for lower tail):

$$\boxed{P(|\bar{X}-\mu| \geq t) \leq 2\exp\!\left(\frac{-2n^2 t^2}{\sum_i(b_i-a_i)^2}\right)}$$

For i.i.d. case with all \(X_i \in [a,b]\): \(\sum_i(b_i-a_i)^2 = n(b-a)^2\), giving \(2\exp(-2nt^2/(b-a)^2)\).

Sample Complexity Formula

Invert Hoeffding to ask: how many samples \(n\) guarantee \(P(|\bar{X}-\mu| \geq t) \leq \delta\)?

$$n \geq \frac{(b-a)^2\ln(2/\delta)}{2t^2}$$

Example: to estimate a \([0,1]\)-bounded quantity within \(t = 0.1\) with probability \(\geq 0.95\) (i.e., \(\delta = 0.05\)): \(n \geq \ln(40)/(0.02) \approx 184\) samples. Numerically exact.

⚠️ Common Misconceptions

1. "Chernoff requires Bernoulli variables." False — the basic Chernoff technique works for any distribution with a finite MGF. The Bernoulli version is just the most widely taught form.

2. "Hoeffding is always tighter than Chebyshev." Not if the variables are nearly unbounded: Hoeffding uses \((b-a)^2\), which may be huge. Bernstein's inequality sometimes offers a better trade-off by incorporating the variance.

3. "The bound is tight." Hoeffding is tight for worst-case distributions (two-point distributions on \(\{a,b\}\)) but often very conservative for smooth distributions like Gaussians.

Key Takeaways & When to Use Each Bound
▼

Choose the bound that matches what you know:

  • Only know the mean? → Markov (non-negative variables only)
  • Know mean + variance, unlimited range? → Chebyshev or Bernstein
  • Know variables are bounded, need PAC-learning guarantees? → Hoeffding
  • Working with Bernoulli / Poisson sums (coins, counts)? → Chernoff (multiplicative form)
🎯 Machine Learning Connection

Hoeffding's inequality underpins the PAC learning framework: a classifier with training error \(\hat{R}\) has generalization error \(R\) satisfying \(P(|R - \hat{R}| \geq \epsilon) \leq 2\exp(-2n\epsilon^2)\) for any fixed hypothesis. For a finite hypothesis class of size \(|\mathcal{H}|\), union-bounding over all hypotheses gives \(n \geq \ln(2|\mathcal{H}|/\delta)/(2\epsilon^2)\). This is the textbook sample complexity of supervised learning.
