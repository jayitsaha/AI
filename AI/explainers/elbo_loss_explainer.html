1
Understanding the ELBO
▼

What is it? To train a generative model like a VAE, we'd love to maximize \(\log p(x)\) — the probability the model assigns to real data. But \(p(x) = \int p(x,z)\,dz\) requires integrating over every possible latent cause \(z\), which is intractable. The ELBO is a computable stand-in: a guaranteed lower bound on \(\log p(x)\) that we can actually maximize with gradient descent.

Analogy: You can't measure a mountain's exact height through the fog (that's \(\log p(x)\)), but you can plant a flag at your best current estimate and prove "the peak is at least this high." As you explore more carefully — bringing your guess \(q(z|x)\) closer to the true shape of the terrain \(p(z|x)\) — your flag climbs higher and higher, squeezing closer to the real summit.

💡 If you remember one thing

$$\log p(x) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q(z|x)}[\log p(x|z)] - D_{KL}\big(q(z|x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}_{\text{ELBO}(x)} \;+\; \underbrace{D_{KL}\big(q(z|x)\,\|\,p(z|x)\big)}_{\text{the gap} \;\ge\, 0}$$ Maximizing the ELBO simultaneously fits the data and pulls \(q\) toward the true posterior — closing the gap.

2
Deriving the Bound from Jensen's Inequality
▼

Step 1 — Insert an arbitrary distribution

Pick any distribution \(q(z|x)\) over the latent variable (it doesn't have to be good yet). Multiply and divide the integrand by it:

$$\log p(x) = \log \int p(x,z)\,dz = \log \int q(z|x)\,\frac{p(x,z)}{q(z|x)}\,dz = \log \mathbb{E}_{q(z|x)}\!\left[\frac{p(x,z)}{q(z|x)}\right]$$

Step 2 — Apply Jensen's inequality

\(\log(\cdot)\) is concave, so for any random variable \(Y\), Jensen's inequality gives \(\log \mathbb{E}[Y] \ge \mathbb{E}[\log Y]\) — pulling the log inside the expectation can only shrink the value. Applying this with \(Y = p(x,z)/q(z|x)\):

$$\log p(x) \;\ge\; \mathbb{E}_{q(z|x)}\!\left[\log \frac{p(x,z)}{q(z|x)}\right] \;=\; \text{ELBO}(x)$$

Equality holds exactly when \(Y\) is constant almost surely — i.e. when \(p(x,z)/q(z|x)\) doesn't depend on \(z\), which happens precisely when \(q(z|x) = p(z|x)\), the true posterior. That single condition is the whole story of variational inference: the tighter \(q\) matches the true posterior, the tighter the bound.

Design decision: why not just estimate \(\log p(x)\) directly?

Monte-Carlo estimating \(p(x) = \mathbb{E}_{p(z)}[p(x|z)]\) by sampling from the prior needs an astronomical number of samples, because for most \(z \sim p(z)\), \(p(x|z) \approx 0\) — the prior rarely lands near the region of \(z\) that actually explains \(x\). Sampling instead from a learned \(q(z|x)\) that concentrates near the posterior makes the estimate tractable — this is importance sampling in disguise, and it's exactly what the ELBO exploits.

🔑 Key Property

The ELBO is a lower bound for any choice of \(q(z|x)\), even a terrible one. Optimizing over \(q\) tightens the bound (variational inference); optimizing over the generative parameters \(\theta\) of \(p_\theta(x|z)\) raises the bound itself (learning). VAEs do both jointly.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

The exact gap: ELBO + KL = evidence

Start from the definition and expand \(p(x,z) = p(z|x)p(x)\):

$$\text{ELBO}(x) = \mathbb{E}_q\!\left[\log\frac{p(z|x)p(x)}{q(z|x)}\right] = \log p(x) - \mathbb{E}_q\!\left[\log\frac{q(z|x)}{p(z|x)}\right] = \log p(x) - D_{KL}\big(q(z|x)\,\|\,p(z|x)\big)$$

Rearranged, this is an exact identity (not an approximation):

$$\log p(x) = \text{ELBO}(x) + D_{KL}\big(q(z|x)\,\|\,p(z|x)\big)$$

Since KL divergence is always \(\ge 0\) (Gibbs' inequality), this instantly re-derives the bound — and tells us the gap is literally the KL divergence between our approximation and the true posterior.

The trainable decomposition: reconstruction − regularizer

Expand \(p(x,z) = p(x|z)p(z)\) instead, and the ELBO splits into two pieces you can compute without ever knowing \(p(z|x)\):

$$\text{ELBO}(x) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q(z|x)}[\log p(x|z)]}_{\text{reconstruction}} \;-\; \underbrace{D_{KL}\big(q(z|x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)}_{\text{regularizer}}$$

The reconstruction term rewards \(z\)'s sampled from \(q\) that let the decoder \(p(x|z)\) reconstruct \(x\) well. The KL term penalizes \(q(z|x)\) for straying from the prior \(p(z)\), keeping the latent space well-behaved and sample-able. This is precisely the (negative) VAE loss.

β-VAE: reweighting the trade-off

$$\mathcal{L}_\beta(x) = \mathbb{E}_{q(z|x)}[\log p(x|z)] - \beta \cdot D_{KL}\big(q(z|x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)$$

Setting \(\beta = 1\) recovers the true ELBO. \(\beta > 1\) over-penalizes the KL term, pressuring latent dimensions to be independent and disentangled at the cost of reconstruction sharpness (and \(\mathcal{L}_\beta\) is no longer a valid lower bound on \(\log p(x)\)). \(\beta < 1\) prioritizes reconstruction fidelity over a clean prior-matching latent space.

Reparameterization trick

To backpropagate through the sampling step \(z \sim q(z|x)\), rewrite it as a deterministic function of noise: \(z = \mu_q + \sigma_q \odot \epsilon\), \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\). Gradients flow through \(\mu_q, \sigma_q\) directly; the randomness is isolated in \(\epsilon\), which needs no gradient.

⚠️ Common Misconception

"Maximizing the ELBO maximizes \(\log p(x)\)." Not quite — it maximizes a lower bound, and the achievable value of \(\log p(x)\) via the ELBO is capped by how expressive \(q\) is. If \(q\) can never equal the true posterior (e.g. a diagonal Gaussian approximating a multimodal posterior), the gap \(D_{KL}(q\|p(z|x))\) stays strictly positive no matter how well you optimize.

Key Takeaways
▼

Where the ELBO shows up:

  • Variational Autoencoders (VAE) — the negative ELBO, averaged over reparameterized samples, is the training loss
  • Variational Bayes / mean-field inference for any latent-variable model (LDA, Bayesian mixtures, state-space models)
  • β-VAE, VQ-VAE priors, and diffusion model training bounds (which are themselves a chain of ELBOs across timesteps)

When the bound is loose (be careful):

  • Mismatched variational family — e.g. a unimodal Gaussian \(q\) trying to cover a multimodal true posterior
  • Posterior collapse — a powerful decoder makes the KL term collapse \(q(z|x) \to p(z)\), ignoring \(z\) entirely (common in text VAEs)
  • High-dimensional \(z\) — reconstruction and KL terms can be wildly different scales, needing careful weighting (β) or free-bits tricks
💡 One-line summary

The ELBO trades an intractable integral for two tractable expectations — reconstruction and KL-to-prior — and its distance from the true log-evidence is exactly the KL divergence between your approximate and true posterior.

Code Trace — ELBO for a Gaussian VAE
▼
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
def elbo_loss(x, x_hat, mu_q, logvar_q, beta=1.0):
# -- Reconstruction term: E_q[log p(x|z)] --
# Gaussian decoder w/ fixed variance -> negative log-lik = MSE (up to const)
recon = -F.mse_loss(x_hat, x, reduction='sum')
# -- KL regularizer: D_KL(q(z|x) || p(z)) for p(z)=N(0,I) --
# closed form: 0.5 * sum(sigma^2 + mu^2 - 1 - log sigma^2)
kl = 0.5 * torch.sum(logvar_q.exp() + mu_q.pow(2) - 1 - logvar_q)
elbo = recon - beta * kl # beta=1 -> true ELBO
return -elbo # minimize the negative ELBO
def reparameterize(mu_q, logvar_q):
# z = mu + sigma * eps, eps ~ N(0, I) -- gradients flow through mu, logvar
std = torch.exp(0.5 * logvar_q)
eps = torch.randn_like(std)
return mu_q + eps * std
# Training step
mu_q, logvar_q = encoder(x)
z = reparameterize(mu_q, logvar_q)
x_hat = decoder(z)
loss = elbo_loss(x, x_hat, mu_q, logvar_q, beta=1.0)
loss.backward()
