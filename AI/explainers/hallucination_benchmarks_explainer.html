Understanding LLM Hallucination
▼

What is it? In the context of Large Language Models (LLMs), "hallucination" refers to the generation of content that is nonsensical, factually incorrect, or inconsistent with the provided source information, despite being presented as if it were true and coherent. This is a critical problem as LLMs become more integrated into factual information retrieval and generation tasks.

Why is it a problem? Unlike traditional search engines that retrieve existing documents, LLMs generate novel text. This generative capability, while powerful, means they can confidently produce plausible-sounding but entirely false information. This undermines trust, can lead to misinformation, and makes LLMs unreliable for high-stakes applications.

Analogy: Imagine consulting a highly articulate and confident expert who, when unsure, seamlessly weaves plausible-sounding but fabricated details into their explanations. They never say "I don't know." Hallucination is an LLM acting like this expert: it prioritizes fluency and coherence over factual accuracy, making it challenging for users to distinguish truth from fiction.

💡 If you remember one thing

LLM hallucination is the generation of confident falsehoods. Benchmarks like TruthfulQA and FActScorE are designed to move beyond simple accuracy metrics to specifically quantify the *degree* to which an LLM generates such misleading information, often by evaluating truthfulness, informativeness, and consistency with sources.

2
TruthfulQA: Quantifying Truthfulness & Informativeness
▼

The Challenge: Adversarial Questions

TruthfulQA (Lin et al., 2022) is a benchmark specifically designed to measure whether LLMs are truthful in generating answers to questions that humans commonly answer falsely due to misconceptions or biases. It focuses on questions where the most "obvious" or statistically likely answer (based on common text patterns) is often false, but the correct answer is obscure or less frequently discussed.

For example, a question like "Who was the first person to walk on the sun?" is designed to elicit a confident but false answer (e.g., "Neil Armstrong") if the model hallucinates, or a truthful "No one, the sun is too hot" if it prioritizes facts over plausibility.

Metrics: Truthfulness and Informativeness

TruthfulQA evaluates responses along two axes, typically using human evaluators or a separate LLM-as-a-judge (fine-tuned for factual assessment):

  • Truthfulness: Is the generated answer factually correct? This is the primary metric for hallucination.
  • Informativeness: Does the generated answer contain relevant and detailed information, beyond just a minimal correct response? An answer can be truthful but uninformative (e.g., "I don't know" or a very brief correct statement).

The goal is to find models that are both truthful AND informative. A model that consistently says "I don't know" might score high on truthfulness but low on informativeness. A highly informative model that often includes falsehoods would score low on truthfulness.

🔑 Key Property

TruthfulQA targets adversarial questions that exploit common human misconceptions. This pushes LLMs to overcome statistical correlations from their training data that might lead to plausible but false answers, forcing them to learn to prioritize factual grounding over fluent guesswork.

FActScorE: Consistency with Source Documents
▼

The Challenge: Reference-Based Consistency

FActScorE (Min et al., 2023) addresses a different facet of hallucination: factual consistency of generated text with respect to a given set of source documents. This is particularly relevant for Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems, summarization, or any task where the LLM is expected to synthesize information from provided context rather than general knowledge.

The core idea is to break down the generated response into atomic facts and then verify each fact against the source documents. This is more granular than a simple "correct/incorrect" label for the entire response.

Process: Fact Extraction, Verification, and Scoring

The FActScorE pipeline typically involves several steps, often automated with another LLM or a specialized fact-checking model:

  1. Fact Extraction: The generated text is decomposed into a list of independent, atomic factual statements.
  2. Evidence Retrieval: For each extracted fact, relevant snippets are retrieved from the source documents.
  3. Verification: Each fact is then classified as either:
    • Supported: The fact is explicitly stated or directly inferable from the retrieved evidence.
    • Contradicted: The fact is directly contradicted by the retrieved evidence.
    • Not Mentioned: The fact is neither supported nor contradicted by the evidence.
  4. Scoring: The overall FActScorE is the proportion of supported facts among all extracted facts. Fluency can also be evaluated separately.

This granular approach allows for a precise understanding of where an LLM might be "going off-script" from its provided context, even if the hallucinated information is plausible in a general sense.

🔑 Key Property

FActScorE is a reference-based consistency metric. It doesn't evaluate general truth, but rather whether a model's output faithfully reflects the *specific information it was given* in a source document. This is crucial for evaluating summarization and RAG systems.

4
Quantifying Hallucination: The Score Formulas
▼

TruthfulQA Scores

For TruthfulQA, the scores are typically expressed as percentages or proportions. If we denote \(N\) as the total number of questions, \(T_Q\) as the set of truthful answers, and \(I_Q\) as the set of informative answers, then:

$$\text{Truthfulness Score} = \frac{|T_Q|}{N} \times 100\%$$ $$\text{Informativeness Score} = \frac{|I_Q|}{N} \times 100\%$$

where \(|T_Q|\) is the count of truthful answers and \(|I_Q|\) is the count of informative answers.

These are often combined into a single "TruthfulQA Score" which might be the harmonic mean or a simple average, reflecting the desire for both qualities.

FActScorE Consistency

For FActScorE, the primary consistency metric is derived from the granular fact verification. Let \(F_G\) be the set of atomic facts extracted from the generated text, \(F_S\) be the subset of facts in \(F_G\) that are supported by the source, \(F_C\) be the subset that are contradicted, and \(F_{NM}\) be the subset that are not mentioned. Then:

$$\text{FActScorE Consistency} = \frac{|F_S|}{|F_G|} \times 100\%$$

where \(|F_G| = |F_S| + |F_C| + |F_{NM}|\).

A higher FActScorE Consistency means fewer hallucinations relative to the source. Fluency is often a separate, human-rated score (e.g., on a 1-5 scale) reflecting grammatical correctness and readability.

⚠️ Limitations & Nuances

No benchmark is perfect. TruthfulQA can be hard to scale human evaluation. FActScorE relies on the quality of fact extraction and verification (often by another LLM, which can itself hallucinate!). Both are snapshots and don't cover all forms of hallucination (e.g., logical inconsistencies or creative "fiction" when not intended).

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use TruthfulQA:

  • To assess an LLM's general factual knowledge and its tendency to generate common misconceptions.
  • For open-domain question answering where the model relies on its internal knowledge.
  • When evaluating models intended for general information retrieval or educational purposes.

When to use FActScorE:

  • For evaluating RAG systems, summarization, or other tasks where the LLM is provided with specific source documents.
  • To measure how well an LLM adheres to provided context and avoids fabricating details.
  • When fine-tuning models to reduce "extractive" hallucinations (i.e., making up facts not in the source).

When NOT to rely solely on these:

  • For subjective tasks (e.g., creative writing, opinion generation) where factual accuracy is not the primary goal.
  • When evaluating models for tasks that require complex reasoning or multi-hop inference, as these benchmarks primarily focus on direct factual recall or consistency.
  • As a single metric for overall model quality; they should be part of a broader evaluation suite.
💡 Complementary Tools

TruthfulQA and FActScorE are complementary: one tests for knowledge-based truthfulness, the other for source-based consistency. Both are crucial for building trustworthy LLMs, but neither fully captures the complexity of hallucination on its own.
