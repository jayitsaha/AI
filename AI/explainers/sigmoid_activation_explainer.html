Sigmoid: Properties & the Vanishing Gradient Problem

Why the S-curve that made backpropagation possible also quietly strangles gradients in deep networks.

Foundational Deep Learning Foundations σ'(x) ≤ 0.25 Vanishing Gradient
1
Understanding the Concept
▾

The sigmoid (logistic) function squashes any real number into the open interval (0,1):

$$\sigma(x) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}$$

Large positive inputs push the output toward 1, large negative inputs push it toward 0, and \(x=0\) lands exactly at 0.5. It was the first widely-used smooth stand-in for the biological neuron's all-or-nothing firing threshold — smooth enough to differentiate, which is exactly what backpropagation needs.

Analogy

Picture an S-shaped dimmer switch. Near the middle, small turns change the brightness a lot — highly responsive. But once the room is nearly pitch black or nearly full brightness, more turning barely changes anything. That flat, unresponsive zone at both ends is saturation, and it's the direct cause of the vanishing gradient problem explored below.

Historically, sigmoid was the default hidden-layer activation throughout the 1980s–2000s. Today it survives almost exclusively as an output-layer activation (binary classification probabilities) and inside gating mechanisms (LSTM/GRU gates, Swish/SiLU) — anywhere a bounded (0,1) "how open is this gate" value is exactly what's wanted.

How It Works Internally
▾

At layer \(l\), a neuron computes a pre-activation \(z = w^\top x + b\), then applies \(a=\sigma(z)\). During the forward pass, each sigmoid neuron independently squashes its input into (0,1). The interesting behavior shows up in the backward pass.

Backpropagation needs \(\partial L/\partial z\) at every layer, computed via the chain rule. Each sigmoid layer contributes a local multiplicative factor:

$$\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\big(1-\sigma(z)\big) \le 0.25$$

To reach an early layer, the gradient must be multiplied by this factor once per layer it passes through. Chain five layers together and — even in the best possible case where every neuron sits exactly at \(z=0\) (the single point where the derivative peaks at 0.25) — the gradient is attenuated by \(0.25^5 \approx 0.00098\), a thousand-fold shrinkage. Push any of those neurons into saturation (\(|z|\gtrsim 5\)) and the attenuation gets astronomically worse, as the interactive panel below shows numerically.

Design Decision

Why was sigmoid replaced by ReLU instead of "fixed" with a better learning rate or initialization? ReLU's derivative is exactly 1 for all positive inputs — it doesn't shrink gradients by construction. Sigmoid's shrinkage is baked into its S-shape; no amount of tuning removes it, only careful initialization (Xavier/Glorot) and shallow depth can keep it manageable.

3
Why It Works — The Math Guarantee
▾

Differentiate \(\sigma(x) = (1+e^{-x})^{-1}\) with the chain rule:

$$\sigma'(x) = \frac{e^{-x}}{(1+e^{-x})^2} = \sigma(x)\big(1-\sigma(x)\big)$$

Write \(s=\sigma(x)\in(0,1)\). The derivative is \(f(s)=s(1-s) = -(s-0.5)^2 + 0.25\) — a downward parabola with maximum exactly \(0.25\) at \(s=0.5\) (i.e. \(x=0\)), decaying to 0 as \(s\to 0\) or \(s\to 1\) (i.e. \(x\to\mp\infty\)). This single inequality, \(\sigma'(x)\le 0.25\) for all \(x\), is the entire mathematical root of the vanishing gradient problem.

Key Guarantee

\(\sigma'\) is self-referential — it only needs the already-computed forward value \(\sigma(x)\), never the raw \(x\) or a fresh \(\exp\) call. This made sigmoid cheap to backpropagate through, which is part of why it was the default choice for decades despite its saturation problem.

Common Misconception

"Vanishing gradients only happen when inputs are extreme." False — even at the ideal \(x=0\) for every neuron, \(0.25 < 1\), so the product across \(L\) layers still decays geometrically. Saturation makes it catastrophically worse, but the multiplicative shrinkage is structural, not just a saturation artifact.

Key Takeaways
▾
  • Formula: \(\sigma(x)=1/(1+e^{-x})\), range (0,1), derivative \(\sigma(1-\sigma)\le 0.25\).
  • Saturation: for \(|x|\gtrsim 5\), \(\sigma'(x)\approx 0\) — gradients through that neuron effectively stop.
  • Vanishing gradient: backprop multiplies \(\sigma'\) factors across layers; even at best, \(0.25^{L-1}\) shrinks exponentially with depth \(L\).
  • Non-zero-centered: always-positive outputs bias downstream weight gradients to a single sign, causing inefficient zig-zag updates.
  • Modern usage: avoid in hidden layers of deep nets — use ReLU/GELU/SiLU instead. Keep sigmoid for binary-classification outputs and gating units (LSTM/GRU, Swish) where saturation toward 0/1 is the desired behavior.
  • Related: Tanh (zero-centered but still saturates), ReLU family (no multiplicative shrinkage), Xavier/Glorot init (mitigates early saturation), BCEWithLogitsLoss (numerically stable pairing).
