Understanding Continuous Distributions
▼

What is a probability distribution? A continuous probability distribution is a mathematical recipe that tells you how likely different outcomes are for a continuous random variable — one that can take any real value in some range, not just counting numbers. Think of rainfall in millimetres, a person's height, or a neural network weight: they live on a continuous number line.

Every distribution is characterised by a probability density function (PDF) \(f(x)\). The PDF itself is not a probability — it's a density. Probability lives in area: \(P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx\). The total area under any valid PDF equals 1.

💡 If you remember one thing

Different distributions exist because real-world phenomena have different shapes. The Gaussian models symmetric noise; the Exponential models waiting times with "memoryless" forgetting; the Beta models probabilities themselves; the Cauchy models thick-tailed phenomena with no finite mean. Choosing the right distribution is half of statistical modelling.

The Four Distribution Families

Gaussian Family
Normal, Multivariate Normal, Log-normal. Central to ML via CLT. Stable under addition.
Gamma Family
Gamma, Exponential (Gamma with α=1), Chi-squared (Gamma with α=k/2, β=½). Models positive quantities.
Beta / Dirichlet
Beta on [0,1], Dirichlet on simplex. Conjugate priors for Bernoulli/Categorical. Models probabilities.
Heavy-Tail Family
Cauchy, Student's t, Pareto, Weibull. Tails heavier than Gaussian — extremes matter.
Other
Uniform (maximum entropy on [a,b]), Laplace (double-Exponential, L1 prior), F (ratio of chi-squareds).

Each Distribution: PDF, Story & Key Relationships
▼

Uniform \(\mathrm{Unif}(a,b)\)

Maximum-entropy distribution on a bounded interval — all values equally likely. Used for random number generation and non-informative priors.

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{b-a}, \quad x \in [a,b]; \qquad \mu = \frac{a+b}{2}, \quad \sigma^2 = \frac{(b-a)^2}{12}$$

Gaussian (Normal) \(\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)\)

The queen of distributions. Arises via the Central Limit Theorem: sums of independent random variables converge to a Gaussian. Unique among continuous distributions in being fully characterised by its first two moments (mean and variance).

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), \quad x \in \mathbb{R}; \qquad \mu,\;\sigma^2$$

Key properties: (1) \(\mathcal{N}(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2) + \mathcal{N}(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_1+\mu_2, \sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)\) (stable); (2) MLE estimates are the sample mean and variance; (3) the standard normal \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) gives the 68-95-99.7 rule; (4) it is self-conjugate — Gaussian likelihood + Gaussian prior = Gaussian posterior.

Multivariate Normal \(\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\Sigma)\)

Extends Gaussian to \(d\) dimensions. Fully characterised by mean vector \(\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^d\) and positive-definite covariance matrix \(\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\). Marginals and conditionals are also Gaussian (closure under linear transforms).

$$f(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}|\Sigma|^{1/2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\right)$$

Exponential \(\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda)\) — special case of Gamma

Models waiting time until the next event in a Poisson process. The unique memoryless continuous distribution: \(P(X > s+t \mid X > s) = P(X > t)\).

$$f(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}, \quad x \ge 0; \qquad \mu = \frac{1}{\lambda}, \quad \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{\lambda^2}$$

Gamma \(\Gamma(\alpha,\beta)\) — rate parameterisation

Models the waiting time for the \(\alpha\)-th event in a Poisson process (sum of \(\alpha\) independent Exponentials). The Gamma function \(\Gamma(\alpha)=\int_0^\infty t^{\alpha-1}e^{-t}\,dt\) generalises the factorial: \(\Gamma(n)=(n-1)!\).

$$f(x) = \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} x^{\alpha-1} e^{-\beta x}, \quad x>0; \qquad \mu = \frac{\alpha}{\beta}, \quad \sigma^2 = \frac{\alpha}{\beta^2}$$

Key special cases: \(\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda) = \Gamma(1,\lambda)\); \(\chi^2_k = \Gamma(k/2, 1/2)\).

Beta \(\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)\)

Defined on \([0,1]\) — models probabilities or proportions. Conjugate prior for the Bernoulli/Binomial likelihood: updating a Beta prior with Bernoulli data gives another Beta posterior. Shape: \(\alpha=\beta=1\) is Uniform; large equal \(\alpha,\beta\) concentrates near 0.5.

$$f(x) = \frac{x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1}}{B(\alpha,\beta)}, \; x\in[0,1]; \quad B(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}; \quad \mu = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}, \; \sigma^2 = \frac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^2(\alpha+\beta+1)}$$

Chi-squared \(\chi^2_k\) — special Gamma

If \(Z_1,\dots,Z_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) iid, then \(Z_1^2+\cdots+Z_k^2 \sim \chi^2_k\). Foundation of hypothesis testing and confidence intervals in regression.

$$\chi^2_k = \Gamma\!\left(\tfrac{k}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}\right); \qquad \mu = k, \quad \sigma^2 = 2k$$

Student's \(t_\nu\)

If \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) and \(V \sim \chi^2_\nu\) independently, then \(T = Z/\sqrt{V/\nu} \sim t_\nu\). Arises when estimating a Gaussian mean with unknown variance — the extra uncertainty inflates the tails. As \(\nu\to\infty\), \(t_\nu \to \mathcal{N}(0,1)\). At \(\nu=1\), the \(t\) distribution is the Cauchy.

$$f(x) = \frac{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu+1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\nu\pi}\,\Gamma\!\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\right)} \left(1+\frac{x^2}{\nu}\right)^{-(\nu+1)/2}; \quad \mu=0\;(\nu>1),\quad \sigma^2=\frac{\nu}{\nu-2}\;(\nu>2)$$

F-distribution \(F(d_1,d_2)\)

Ratio of two independent chi-squared variables (each scaled by their df). Used in ANOVA and comparing variances.

$$F(d_1,d_2) = \frac{\chi^2_{d_1}/d_1}{\chi^2_{d_2}/d_2}; \qquad \mu = \frac{d_2}{d_2-2}\;(d_2>2), \quad \sigma^2 = \frac{2d_2^2(d_1+d_2-2)}{d_1(d_2-2)^2(d_2-4)}\;(d_2>4)$$

Cauchy \(\mathrm{Cauchy}(\mu, \gamma)\)

The canonical example of a distribution with no finite moments. Heavy polynomial tails (\(\sim x^{-2}\)) mean the mean, variance, and all higher moments are undefined. The ratio of two independent standard normals follows a standard Cauchy. Physicists know it as the Lorentz/Breit-Wigner distribution.

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\pi\gamma\left[1+\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\gamma}\right)^2\right]}; \qquad \text{mean, variance: undefined}$$

Laplace \(\mathrm{Laplace}(\mu, b)\)

Double-sided Exponential: \(f(x) \propto \exp(-|x-\mu|/b)\). The MLE for the Laplace is the median — making it the probabilistic equivalent of L1 regression. Placing a Laplace prior on neural network weights is equivalent to L1 regularisation (LASSO).

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{2b}\exp\!\left(-\frac{|x-\mu|}{b}\right); \qquad \mu,\quad \sigma^2=2b^2$$

Log-normal \(\mathrm{LogNormal}(\mu,\sigma^2)\)

If \(X = e^Y\) where \(Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)\), then \(X\) is log-normal. Applies to multiplicative processes: stock prices, city sizes, biological concentrations. Right-skewed with a long right tail.

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{x\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(\ln x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), \; x>0; \quad \mathbb{E}[X]=e^{\mu+\sigma^2/2}, \quad \operatorname{Var}(X)=(e^{\sigma^2}-1)e^{2\mu+\sigma^2}$$

Weibull \(\mathrm{Weibull}(k,\lambda)\)

Generalises the Exponential. Shape parameter \(k\) controls the hazard rate: \(k<1\) (decreasing hazard = infant mortality), \(k=1\) (constant hazard = Exponential), \(k>1\) (increasing hazard = wear-out failures). Ubiquitous in reliability engineering and survival analysis.

$$f(x) = \frac{k}{\lambda}\!\left(\frac{x}{\lambda}\right)^{k-1}\!e^{-(x/\lambda)^k},\;x\ge 0;\quad \mu=\lambda\,\Gamma\!\left(1+\tfrac{1}{k}\right),\quad \sigma^2=\lambda^2\!\left[\Gamma\!\left(1+\tfrac{2}{k}\right)-\Gamma\!\left(1+\tfrac{1}{k}\right)^2\right]$$

Pareto \(\mathrm{Pareto}(\alpha, x_m)\)

Power-law tail: the 80/20 rule lives here. Models wealth, city sizes, internet traffic. Heavy tails — mean exists only if \(\alpha>1\), variance only if \(\alpha>2\). Support \(x \ge x_m\).

$$f(x) = \frac{\alpha x_m^\alpha}{x^{\alpha+1}},\;x\ge x_m;\quad \mu=\frac{\alpha x_m}{\alpha-1}\;(\alpha>1),\quad \sigma^2=\frac{x_m^2\alpha}{(\alpha-1)^2(\alpha-2)}\;(\alpha>2)$$

Dirichlet \(\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\)

The multivariate generalisation of Beta — lives on the probability simplex \(\{\mathbf{p}: p_i\ge 0, \sum p_i=1\}\). Conjugate prior for the Categorical/Multinomial. Parameter \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_K)\) controls concentration; \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\mathbf{1}\) is the Uniform distribution over the simplex.

$$f(\mathbf{p}) = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha_0)}{\prod_{k=1}^K \Gamma(\alpha_k)} \prod_{k=1}^K p_k^{\alpha_k-1}, \quad \alpha_0 = \sum_k \alpha_k; \quad \mathbb{E}[p_k]=\frac{\alpha_k}{\alpha_0}$$
Key Relationships at a Glance

\(\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda) = \Gamma(1,\lambda)\)  |  \(\chi^2_k = \Gamma(k/2,\tfrac{1}{2})\)  |  \(t_1 = \mathrm{Cauchy}\)  |  \(t_\infty = \mathcal{N}(0,1)\)  |  \(\mathrm{Beta}(1,1) = \mathrm{Unif}(0,1)\)  |  \(e^{\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^2)} = \mathrm{LogNormal}(\mu,\sigma^2)\)

The Gaussian in Depth — Why It Dominates ML
▼

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

If \(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n\) are iid with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2 < \infty\), then the standardised sample mean converges in distribution to \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\):

$$\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty$$

This is why Gaussian noise appears everywhere: any sum of many small independent contributions (measurement errors, thermal noise, biological variation) is approximately Gaussian regardless of the original distribution's shape.

Maximum Entropy Characterisation

Among all distributions on \(\mathbb{R}\) with fixed mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), the Gaussian has the maximum entropy: \(H[\mathcal{N}] = \frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi e\sigma^2)\). This makes it the "least informative" distribution given second-order constraints — justifying its use as a prior when you only know the variance.

The 68–95–99.7 Rule (3-sigma rule)

$$P(\mu - \sigma \le X \le \mu + \sigma) \approx 0.6827$$
$$P(\mu - 2\sigma \le X \le \mu + 2\sigma) \approx 0.9545$$
$$P(\mu - 3\sigma \le X \le \mu + 3\sigma) \approx 0.9973$$

Gaussian vs Heavy-Tailed Distributions

The Gaussian PDF decays as \(e^{-x^2}\) (super-exponentially fast) while the Cauchy decays as \(x^{-2}\) and the Pareto as \(x^{-\alpha-1}\). In practice, many real phenomena have heavier tails than the Gaussian — financial returns, internet traffic, earthquake magnitudes — where Student's \(t\) or Pareto better models extreme events.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

The CLT gives Gaussian sample means, not Gaussian raw data. Many practitioners wrongly assume all data is Gaussian. Always check tail behaviour with QQ-plots. Financial return data, for instance, has tails 4–10× heavier than Gaussian.

Multivariate Gaussian — Geometric Intuition

The level sets of the MVN density are ellipsoids determined by \(\Sigma\). The Mahalanobis distance \(d_M(\mathbf{x}) = \sqrt{(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\top \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})}\) is the "standardised" Euclidean distance accounting for correlation. Gaussian mixtures (GMMs) approximate any smooth density — the universal approximation theorem for distributions.

$$\mathbf{x} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma) \implies A\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b} \sim \mathcal{N}(A\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbf{b},\; A\Sigma A^\top)$$

💡 Decision Guide

Symmetric, bounded variance → Gaussian (CLT sums, neural net weights) · Positive, waiting times → Exponential/Gamma (inter-arrival times) · Values in [0,1] → Beta (probabilities, click-through rates) · Heavy tails, outliers → t, Cauchy, Laplace (robust models) · Power-law phenomena → Pareto, Log-normal (wealth, city sizes) · Reliability / failure → Weibull (product lifetimes)

Code Reference — Sampling & PDF in Python
▼
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
# ── Gaussian ──────────────────────────────────────────────
mu, sigma = 0.0, 1.0
x = stats.norm.rvs(loc=mu, scale=sigma, size=1000) # sample
p = stats.norm.pdf(x, loc=mu, scale=sigma) # density
# ── Exponential ───────────────────────────────────────────
lam = 2.0 # rate parameter
x_exp = stats.expon.rvs(scale=1/lam, size=1000) # scipy uses scale=1/lambda
# ── Gamma ─────────────────────────────────────────────────
alpha, beta = 3, 2 # shape, rate
x_gam = stats.gamma.rvs(a=alpha, scale=1/beta, size=1000) # scale=1/rate
# ── Beta ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
x_beta = stats.beta.rvs(a=2, b=5, size=1000)
# ── Student's t ───────────────────────────────────────────
x_t = stats.t.rvs(df=5, size=1000) # fatter tails than N(0,1) for small df
# ── Pareto ────────────────────────────────────────────────
alpha_p = 3.0; xm = 1.0
x_par = stats.pareto.rvs(b=alpha_p, scale=xm, size=1000)
# ── Dirichlet ─────────────────────────────────────────────
alpha_dir = [2, 3, 5] # K=3 categories
p_dir = np.random.dirichlet(alpha_dir, size=10) # samples live on simplex
# ── Multivariate Normal ───────────────────────────────────
mu_mv = np.array([0, 0])
Sigma = np.array([[1, 0.8], [0.8, 1]]) # correlation=0.8
x_mv = np.random.multivariate_normal(mu_mv, Sigma, size=500)
