How transformations stretch or shrink infinitesimal areas/volumes in multivariable integrals
Foundational Calculus Linear Algebra O(d²) determinant
1
Understanding the Change of Variables Formula
▼

What is it? The change of variables formula allows us to transform an integral from one coordinate system to another. This is incredibly useful when the region of integration or the integrand itself becomes simpler in a different coordinate system (e.g., switching from Cartesian to polar coordinates for circular regions). The core idea is that when you change coordinates, the "infinitesimal area" or "volume" element (\(dx\,dy\) or \(dx\,dy\,dz\)) also changes, and this change must be accounted for by a scaling factor: the Jacobian determinant.

Analogy: Imagine you have a map of a city drawn on a rubber sheet. You want to calculate the total population density across a complex, irregularly shaped neighborhood. If you stretch and distort the rubber sheet, the neighborhood might become a simple square, making calculations easier. However, the density values themselves would change, and the "area" of each tiny patch of the neighborhood would also change. The Jacobian determinant is like a local "stretch factor" that tells you how much a tiny square on the original map has been stretched or squished into its new shape and size on the distorted map.

💡 If you remember one thing

The Jacobian determinant is the crucial scaling factor that accounts for how infinitesimal area (or volume) elements change when you transform from one coordinate system to another. Without it, your integral will be wrong because you're adding up "pieces" of the wrong size.

2
How It Works Internally
▼

The Transformation Function

We start with a transformation \(T\) that maps points from one coordinate system (say, \(uv\)-plane) to another (the \(xy\)-plane). This function can be written as \(x = x(u,v)\) and \(y = y(u,v)\). For example, in polar coordinates, \(x = r \cos \theta\) and \(y = r \sin \theta\), where \((u,v)\) would be \((r,\theta)\).

When we integrate, we're summing up tiny pieces of area. In the \(uv\)-plane, an infinitesimal rectangle with sides \(du\) and \(dv\) has area \(du\,dv\). When this rectangle is mapped by \(T\) to the \(xy\)-plane, it generally becomes a small parallelogram. The key is to find the area of this transformed parallelogram.

Linear Approximation and Partial Derivatives

For a very small region around a point \((u_0, v_0)\), the transformation \(T\) can be approximated by a linear transformation. This linear approximation is given by the matrix of partial derivatives, known as the Jacobian matrix. The columns of the Jacobian matrix are the vectors representing how a small change in \(u\) (holding \(v\) constant) and a small change in \(v\) (holding \(u\) constant) affect \(x\) and \(y\). These are the partial derivative vectors:

  • \(\frac{\partial \mathbf{T}}{\partial u} = \left\langle \frac{\partial x}{\partial u}, \frac{\partial y}{\partial u} \right\rangle\)
  • \(\frac{\partial \mathbf{T}}{\partial v} = \left\langle \frac{\partial x}{\partial v}, \frac{\partial y}{\partial v} \right\rangle\)

If we consider a tiny vector \(\langle du, 0 \rangle\) in the \(uv\)-plane, it transforms approximately to \(\left\langle \frac{\partial x}{\partial u} du, \frac{\partial y}{\partial u} du \right\rangle\) in the \(xy\)-plane. Similarly, \(\langle 0, dv \rangle\) transforms to \(\left\langle \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} dv, \frac{\partial y}{\partial v} dv \right\rangle\).

These two transformed vectors form the sides of the parallelogram in the \(xy\)-plane. The area of a parallelogram formed by two vectors \(\mathbf{a} = \langle a_1, a_2 \rangle\) and \(\mathbf{b} = \langle b_1, b_2 \rangle\) is given by the absolute value of the determinant of the matrix formed by these vectors: \(|a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1|\).

🔑 Key Property

The Jacobian matrix is essentially the "derivative" of a multivariable transformation. Its determinant locally measures how much the transformation stretches or compresses space, providing the necessary scaling factor for integrals.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

The Change of Variables Formula

For a double integral over a region \(R\) in the \(xy\)-plane, transformed from a region \(S\) in the \(uv\)-plane by \(x=x(u,v)\) and \(y=y(u,v)\), the formula is:

$$\iint_R f(x,y) \,dx\,dy = \iint_S f(x(u,v), y(u,v)) \left| \det \left( \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} \right) \right| \,du\,dv$$

Here, \(f(x(u,v), y(u,v))\) is the integrand expressed in the new coordinates, and the term \(\left| \det \left( \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} \right) \right|\) is the absolute value of the Jacobian determinant, which accounts for the area scaling.

The Jacobian Matrix and its Determinant

The Jacobian matrix for a transformation \(T(u,v) = (x(u,v), y(u,v))\) is a \(2 \times 2\) matrix of partial derivatives:

$$J = \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\partial x}{\partial u} & \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} \\ \frac{\partial y}{\partial u} & \frac{\partial y}{\partial v} \end{pmatrix}$$

The determinant of this matrix is then:

$$\det(J) = \frac{\partial x}{\partial u} \frac{\partial y}{\partial v} - \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} \frac{\partial y}{\partial u}$$

This determinant, when taken as its absolute value, is the local scaling factor for area: \(dA_{xy} = |\det(J)| \,dA_{uv}\). For example, for polar coordinates \(x = r \cos \theta, y = r \sin \theta\), the Jacobian determinant is \(r\), which is why we use \(r\,dr\,d\theta\) in polar integrals.

⚠️ Important Detail

The absolute value of the Jacobian determinant is crucial. The determinant itself can be negative, indicating a change in orientation (e.g., a flip in the coordinate system). However, area and volume are always positive, so we always take the absolute value for the scaling factor.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use the Change of Variables Formula:

  • Simplifying Integration Regions: When the region of integration is complex in Cartesian coordinates (e.g., circles, ellipses, sectors), transforming to polar, cylindrical, or spherical coordinates simplifies the bounds.
  • Simplifying Integrands: If the function being integrated becomes much simpler in a different coordinate system.
  • Probability Density Function Transformations: In statistics, when transforming random variables, the Jacobian determinant is used to scale the probability density function to ensure the total probability remains 1.
  • Neural Networks (Invertible Flows): In generative models, invertible neural networks use the Jacobian determinant to compute likelihoods, as they explicitly model a change of variables.

When to be careful:

  • Non-Invertible Transformations: The transformation \(T\) must be one-to-one (invertible) over the region of integration. If \(T\) maps multiple points from the \(uv\)-plane to a single point in the \(xy\)-plane, the formula doesn't directly apply, or the determinant might be zero.
  • Non-Differentiable Transformations: The partial derivatives must exist and be continuous over the region.
  • Orientation Changes: A negative Jacobian determinant means the transformation flips the orientation of the coordinate system. While the absolute value is used for area/volume, understanding the sign can be important in other contexts (e.g., vector calculus).
💡 Big Picture

The Jacobian determinant is a fundamental concept in multivariable calculus, linear algebra, and advanced mathematics. It generalizes the idea of a derivative to multiple dimensions, quantifying local scaling effects. It's the multi-dimensional equivalent of the simple substitution rule \(\int f(g(x))g'(x) dx = \int f(u) du\), where \(g'(x)\) is the 1D scaling factor.

Code Trace (Python/NumPy)

import numpy as np
# Define a transformation function T(u, v) -> (x, y)
def transformation(u, v):
# Example: A non-linear transformation
x = u + 0.5 * v
y = v + 0.2 * u * v
return x, y
# Define the partial derivatives for the Jacobian matrix
def partial_derivatives(u, v):
# dx/du, dx/dv, dy/du, dy/dv
dx_du = 1.0
dx_dv = 0.5
dy_du = 0.2 * v
dy_dv = 1.0 + 0.2 * u
return dx_du, dx_dv, dy_du, dy_dv
# Function to compute the Jacobian matrix and its determinant
def compute_jacobian(u, v):
dx_du, dx_dv, dy_du, dy_dv = partial_derivatives(u, v)
jacobian_matrix = np.array([[dx_du, dx_dv],
[dy_du, dy_dv]])
determinant = np.linalg.det(jacobian_matrix)
return jacobian_matrix, determinant
# Example usage at a specific point (u_0, v_0)
u_0, v_0 = 1.0, 0.5
J, det_J = compute_jacobian(u_0, v_0)
print(f"Jacobian Matrix at ({u_0}, {v_0}):")
print(J)
print(f"Determinant of Jacobian: {det_J}")
print(f"Absolute Determinant (Area Scale Factor): {abs(det_J)}")
