Understanding the Problem
▼

What is it? By the time you reach "Training" in an ML system design interview, the architecture is usually settled — the hard, high-leverage decisions left are (1) how you turn raw interaction logs into a supervised training set (which events are positive labels, how negatives get manufactured, how imbalance gets corrected) and (2) which loss function you optimize. These two choices decide what the model actually learns far more than swapping one architecture for another does.

Analogy: Training a sommelier by only showing wines they said "I loved this" teaches nothing — there is no contrast to calibrate against. What you show as the "not loved" examples matters enormously: random bottles from the cellar teach "does this even resemble wine I like," while bottles that looked similar but were passed over teach "what is the fine-grained thing that actually makes the difference." Negative construction defines the question the model is being asked to answer.

💡 If you remember one thing

The label-construction and negative-sampling strategy silently redefine what the loss function is estimating. The same pointwise binary cross-entropy loss, $$\mathcal{L}_{BCE} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_i\big[y_i\log \hat p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat p_i)\big]$$ trained on random negatives learns "does this look clickable at all," while trained on hard negatives it learns "is this clickable versus its closest competitors." Same formula, different model.

How It Works Internally
▼

From logs to labels

Raw logs record impressions: a user was shown an item, and either clicked/converted or did not. A positive label is usually defined as click, add-to-cart, purchase, or long dwell time — but implicit feedback is noisy: a click can be misleading (clickbait), and a non-click doesn't mean "irrelevant," it can mean "never truly seen" (position bias — items shown lower get fewer clicks regardless of quality).

Negative sampling strategies

Un-clicked impressions are not automatically good negatives — how you pick them changes the difficulty of the learning problem:

  • Random negatives — sample uniformly from un-clicked items. Cheap, but usually too easy: the model reaches high accuracy quickly by learning coarse popularity cues without learning fine-grained relevance.
  • Hard negatives — items the current model scores highly but the user did not click. These sit near the decision boundary and keep gradient signal alive long after random negatives have stopped contributing much. Overused, they risk promoting false negatives (items the user would have liked but never saw).
  • In-batch negatives — reuse other examples' positive items within the same minibatch as free negatives for every other row. For a batch of size B this turns B labeled positives into B(B−1) negative pairs at zero extra labeling cost — the standard trick for large-scale retrieval / two-tower models.

Class-imbalance reweighting

Click-through rates are typically low, so positives are a small minority. Left uncorrected, a loss dominated by negatives pushes predicted probabilities toward zero for everything. Inverse-frequency class weights ($$w_{pos}=\tfrac{N}{2N_{pos}},\ w_{neg}=\tfrac{N}{2N_{neg}}$$), oversampling positives, or focal loss all rebalance the gradient contribution of each class.

Choosing the loss family

Pointwise (BCE) treats every example independently and produces a calibrated probability, but ignores relative ordering between candidates. Pairwise (BPR / hinge) directly optimizes "is the positive ranked above this negative," matching ranking metrics better but yielding uncalibrated scores. Listwise (softmax cross-entropy / ListNet / LambdaRank) optimizes the ordering of an entire candidate list at once — closest to metrics like NDCG, but the most expensive per training instance.

🔑 Design decision: where in the funnel?

Retrieval / matching stages (millions of candidates) favor in-batch negatives + pairwise or listwise contrastive loss — cheap negatives at scale matter more than calibration. Ranking stages (a few hundred already-relevant candidates) favor pointwise or listwise loss with hard or human-labeled negatives — precision on a small set matters more than throughput.

Why It Works — The Math
▼

Pointwise: Binary Cross-Entropy

$$\mathcal{L}_{BCE} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} w_{y_i}\big[y_i\log \hat p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-\hat p_i)\big]$$

The gradient with respect to the logit is \(\hat p_i - y_i\) — its magnitude shrinks to near zero once an example is confidently and correctly classified. This is exactly why easy random negatives stop teaching anything after a few epochs: their gradient vanishes, while hard negatives (where \(\hat p_i\) stays near 0.5) keep contributing signal.

Pairwise: Bayesian Personalized Ranking (BPR)

BPR assumes the model believes the positive item \(i^+\) is preferred over a sampled negative \(i^-\) with probability \(\sigma(s_{i^+}-s_{i^-})\). Maximizing the likelihood of the observed preference gives:

$$\mathcal{L}_{BPR} = -\log \sigma(s_{i^+} - s_{i^-})$$

Minimizing this directly maximizes the pairwise AUC of the ranking — it never needs a calibrated probability, only the correct relative order of scores.

Listwise: Softmax Cross-Entropy (Plackett–Luce)

$$\mathcal{L}_{list} = -\log \frac{e^{s_{i^+}}}{\sum_{j \in \text{list}} e^{s_j}}$$

This is the negative log-likelihood of the Plackett–Luce ranking model restricted to "who is first." Its gradient pushes the positive's score up relative to every other candidate in the list simultaneously — a strictly stronger signal per step than a single pairwise comparison.

⚠️ Common pitfall

In-batch negatives are not i.i.d. samples from the true negative distribution — their difficulty depends entirely on batch composition, and popular items appear as negatives disproportionately often, biasing the model against popular-but-relevant items. Production systems correct this with a log-frequency term inside the softmax (the "sampled softmax correction" used in YouTube's two-tower retrieval model).

Key Takeaways
▼

Use pointwise BCE when:

  • You need a calibrated probability (e.g. for pacing, pricing, or thresholding downstream)
  • Labels are abundant and roughly balanced after reweighting
  • Simplicity and independent per-example computation matter (easy to shard, cache, debug)

Use pairwise BPR when:

  • Only relative order matters (top-K ranking), not calibrated scores
  • You can cheaply sample (positive, negative) pairs, e.g. from implicit feedback
  • You want a loss more robust to overall base-rate shifts than BCE

Use listwise loss when:

  • You have the full candidate list per query/user (typical at the ranking stage, not retrieval)
  • Your offline metric is list-based (NDCG, MRR) — listwise loss correlates with it far better than pointwise
  • You can afford the extra compute of a softmax over the whole list per training step
💡 Interview one-liner

"Positives come from clicks/conversions with position-bias correction; negatives are random early on and hard-mined or in-batch as the model improves; the loss is pointwise for calibration at the ranking stage and pairwise/listwise with in-batch negatives for the retrieval stage — and class weights keep the rare positive class from being drowned out." That sentence covers Step 6 end-to-end.

# 5a. Pointwise BCE
def bce(p, y, w):
return -w * (y*np.log(p) + (1-y)*np.log(1-p))
# 5b. Pairwise BPR
def bpr(s_pos, s_neg):
return -np.log(sigmoid(s_pos - s_neg))
# 5c. Listwise softmax cross-entropy
def listwise(scores, true_idx):
probs = softmax(scores)
return -np.log(probs[true_idx])
