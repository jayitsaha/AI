Understanding Multivariate EDA
▼

What is it? Univariate EDA looks at one variable at a time (a histogram); bivariate EDA looks at two (a scatter plot with one correlation number). Real datasets have dozens of variables that interact — a variable can look harmless alone yet be almost a duplicate of another, or two variables can flip their relationship once you split by a hidden group. Multivariate EDA is the set of tools that let you see many variables — and their joint structure — at once, before you fit a single model.

Analogy: Reading one variable at a time is like judging a orchestra by listening to each instrument alone in a soundproof room. You will miss the harmony, the clashes, and the moments where two instruments are basically playing the same note. Pair plots, heatmaps, and parallel coordinates let you hear the whole orchestra together.

💡 If you remember one thing

A pair plot shows shape (linear vs curved vs clustered), a correlation heatmap shows magnitude and direction for every pair at a glance, and parallel coordinates show how whole observations move across every variable together. $$r_{XY}=\frac{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}{\sqrt{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2}}$$ is the single number a heatmap cell encodes — but it can hide as much as it reveals.

How Multivariate EDA Works Internally
▼

Pair Plots (Scatter-Plot Matrices)

For \(p\) numeric variables, a pair plot builds a \(p\times p\) grid. The diagonal shows each variable's own distribution (a histogram or density). Every off-diagonal cell \((i,j)\) is a scatter of variable \(j\) against variable \(i\). Colouring points by a categorical column (a cluster or class label) turns the grid into a cheap, brute-force way to spot separable groups, nonlinear curves, and outliers before any modelling.

Correlation Heatmaps: Pearson vs Spearman

A heatmap replaces every scatter cell with one number and one colour. Pearson \(r\) measures linear association — it is sensitive to outliers and blind to curvature. Spearman \(\rho\) first converts each variable to ranks, then computes Pearson correlation on the ranks — this makes it a measure of monotonic association, robust to outliers and immune to nonlinear-but-monotonic curvature. When \(\rho\) is noticeably larger than \(r\) for the same pair, the relationship is real but bent, not weak.

Parallel Coordinates

Instead of one axis per pair, parallel coordinates give each of the \(p\) variables its own vertical axis, evenly spaced, each independently min-max scaled. Every observation becomes a single polyline connecting its value on axis 1 to its value on axis 2, and so on. Groups of similarly coloured, near-parallel lines reveal a cluster that behaves consistently across all variables simultaneously — something no single 2-D plot can show.

🔑 Key Property

Every one of these tools operates on the same underlying \(n\times p\) data matrix; they are just different projections of it — pairwise (heatmap, pair plot), per-observation (parallel coordinates), or into a rotated low-dimensional basis (PCA).

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Pearson vs Spearman, Formally

$$r_{XY}=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y} \qquad \rho_{XY}=r_{\operatorname{rank}(X),\,\operatorname{rank}(Y)}$$

Spearman is literally Pearson applied after replacing each value with its rank — this single substitution is what makes it invariant to any monotonic (rank-preserving) transformation of either variable.

Multicollinearity via Variance Inflation Factor

$$VIF_j=\frac{1}{1-R_j^2}$$

where \(R_j^2\) comes from regressing variable \(X_j\) on all remaining predictors. With just two collinear variables this collapses to their pairwise \(r^2\). \(VIF_j > 5\)–\(10\) is a common rule-of-thumb warning that \(X_j\) is nearly a linear combination of the others.

PCA: Diagonalizing the Covariance Structure

PCA finds directions \(v\) that maximize variance by solving the eigenproblem on the covariance matrix \(\Sigma\):

$$\Sigma v = \lambda v \qquad \text{explained variance ratio of component } k = \frac{\lambda_k}{\sum_i \lambda_i}$$

The eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue \(\lambda_1\) is the direction of maximum spread (PC1); the next, orthogonal to it, is PC2, and so on. When two variables are highly correlated, PCA rotates onto the shared direction they both point along, compressing near-redundant columns into fewer effectively independent ones.

⚠️ Common Pitfall — Simpson's Paradox

A single aggregate correlation (one heatmap cell) can have the opposite sign of the correlation within every subgroup, if the subgroups differ enough in their means. Always colour scatter plots by a plausible grouping variable before trusting a heatmap number at face value.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use each technique:

  • Pair plot — first pass on a new dataset with roughly 8 or fewer numeric variables, to eyeball shape, spread, and clusters
  • Correlation heatmap (Pearson) — a fast numeric summary of every pairwise linear relationship at once
  • Correlation heatmap (Spearman) — same, but robust to monotonic-nonlinear relationships and outliers
  • Parallel coordinates — trace whole observations (not just pairs) across many variables, especially with a categorical hue
  • PCA overview — when variables are highly collinear and you want to compress correlated structure into a few uncorrelated directions

When NOT to rely on it blindly:

  • Pair plots with more than about 10-12 variables become an unreadable grid — select a subset or use PCA first
  • A single aggregate correlation number, without checking for Simpson's-paradox subgroups
  • Raw covariance heatmaps when variables are on very different scales — standardize first, or use correlation instead
💡 Hierarchy

Univariate EDA (one histogram) ⊂ Bivariate EDA (one scatter, one \(r\)) ⊂ Multivariate EDA (pair plot + heatmap + parallel coordinates + PCA). Each layer reveals interactions the previous layer structurally cannot show.
