Understanding A/B Testing for RecSys
▼

What is it? A/B testing for recommender systems means randomly splitting users into a control group (the current production ranker) and a treatment group (a candidate ranker), then measuring whether an engagement metric — click-through rate, watch time, session length — moves enough to be trusted as a real effect rather than noise. Two things make this harder than a typical web A/B test: recommendation effects are often tiny (a genuinely great model might move CTR by half a percent), and the metrics you can measure quickly are not always the metrics you actually care about.

Analogy: Classic A/B testing is like sending diners to two separate restaurants for a month and comparing how often they come back — informative, but you need a lot of diners and a lot of patience to see a real difference. Interleaving is like handing every single diner both chefs' dishes on one plate at the same time and watching which one they actually eat more of — a paired, within-user comparison that is dramatically more sensitive per observation. And long-term effects are the discovery, months later, that the "better" dish from the interleaving test was secretly loaded with sugar: diners ate more of it in the moment but stopped coming back for it as often.

💡 If you remember one thing

A short-term engagement win is not proof of a real improvement. RecSys experimentation stacks three tools: (1) randomized A/B tests for unbiased causal estimates, (2) interleaving for cheap, high-sensitivity ranker comparisons, and (3) long-running holdout groups to catch effects — like novelty or clickbait fatigue — that only appear after weeks.

2
How It Works Internally
▼

Absolute A/B testing mechanics

Users are bucketed by a deterministic hash of their user ID into control or treatment, so each user sees a consistent experience across sessions. Before launch, teams pre-register a primary metric and run a power analysis to decide the sample size \(n\) needed to detect a minimum detectable effect (MDE) \(\delta\) at significance level \(\alpha\) and power \(1-\beta\). The test runs for full weekly cycles to average out day-of-week effects.

Interleaving mechanics

Instead of showing two separate rankings to two separate users, Team-Draft Interleaving (TDI) merges ranking A and ranking B into a single list shown to one user. At each position, whichever team is currently behind (or a coin flip if tied) contributes its next unused top item; every slot is labeled with its owning team. Because both rankers are compared inside the same user's single session, all the between-user variance that drowns out absolute metrics cancels out — this is a paired design, and pairing is what makes it so much more sensitive.

Long-term effects mechanics

A small slice of traffic — a holdout group — is kept on the old system (or, in reverse-holdout designs, kept on the new one) for months, decoupled from the main launch decision. Teams track downstream outcomes: weekly retention, session frequency, satisfaction surveys. Because novelty wears off and fatigue accumulates slowly, the holdout is the only reliable way to see whether a short-term metric win survives contact with reality.

🔑 Key Property

Interleaving answers "which ranker do users prefer, right now, holding everything else fixed?" — a preference question. Long-term holdouts answer "does that preference translate into durable value?" — a very different, slower question. You need both.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Two-proportion z-test for absolute A/B

With click counts \(y_C, y_T\) out of \(n_C, n_T\) impressions, the pooled proportion and test statistic are:

$$\hat p = \frac{y_C+y_T}{n_C+n_T}, \qquad z = \frac{\hat p_T - \hat p_C}{\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)\left(\frac{1}{n_C}+\frac{1}{n_T}\right)}}$$

The required per-arm sample size to detect a true difference \(\delta\) at significance \(\alpha\) and power \(1-\beta\) is:

$$n = \frac{2\,p(1-p)\,(z_{\alpha/2}+z_{\beta})^2}{\delta^2}$$

Because \(\delta\) for ranking changes is often under 1% of a baseline CTR near 5%, \(n\) can run into the tens of thousands per arm — weeks of full traffic.

Interleaving's sensitivity edge

Interleaving reduces the question to a binomial sign test: across many sessions, does team B win more often than team A? With \(w_B\) sessions won by B out of \(N\) decisive sessions:

$$\hat w = \frac{w_B}{N}, \qquad SE(\hat w) = \sqrt{\frac{\hat w(1-\hat w)}{N}}$$

Because each session directly compares both rankers under identical user/context conditions, the effect size on \(\hat w\) (deviation from 0.5) is typically much larger relative to its variance than the deviation in raw CTR — so far fewer sessions are needed to reach significance.

Modeling the novelty-to-fatigue drift

Short-term lift often decays from an initial novelty bump toward a lower steady state; long-term harm often builds up slowly as trust erodes:

$$\Delta_{short}(t) = \delta_\infty + \beta\, e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad \Delta_{long}(t) = \delta_\infty^{(L)}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau'}\right)$$
⚠️ Common Pitfall

Shipping on a 1–2 week absolute A/B or a single interleaving win captures \(\Delta_{short}(t)\) near its peak, right when \(\beta e^{-t/\tau}\) is largest. The steady-state effect \(\delta_\infty\) — and the slow-building long-term effect \(\delta_\infty^{(L)}\) — can be smaller, or even the opposite sign.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to reach for each tool:

  • Use absolute A/B tests for the final, decision-grade estimate of business-metric impact — they give unbiased, interpretable numbers, but need large traffic and time.
  • Use interleaving for rapid ranker iteration — comparing two candidate rankers many times a week with a fraction of the traffic an absolute test would need.
  • Use long-term holdouts whenever a change could plausibly trade short-term engagement for long-term trust (clickbait, outrage, over-personalization, filter bubbles).

Common failure modes:

  • Shipping on interleaving wins alone — a preference signal is not the same as a validated business outcome.
  • Stopping an absolute A/B test as soon as it crosses significance ("peeking") inflates false-positive rate.
  • Ignoring novelty decay — measuring only in week 1 systematically overstates steady-state lift.
💡 The stacking principle

Interleaving tells you fast which ranker users prefer today. Absolute A/B tells you, with statistical rigor, how much a metric moved. Long-term holdouts tell you whether that preference was actually good for the user and the business. None of the three alone is sufficient.

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm
# --- Absolute A/B: two-proportion z-test ---
def ab_test(yC, nC, yT, nT):
pC, pT = yC/nC, yT/nT
pooled = (yC+yT)/(nC+nT)
se = np.sqrt(pooled*(1-pooled)*(1/nC+1/nT))
z = (pT - pC) / se
p_value = 2*(1 - norm.cdf(abs(z)))
return z, p_value
# --- Team-Draft Interleaving ---
def team_draft(rank_a, rank_b, rng):
merged, team_of, used = [], [], set()
countA = countB = ai = bi = 0
while len(merged) < len(rank_a):
take_a = countA < countB or (countA == countB and rng.random() < 0.5)
if take_a:
while rank_a[ai] in used: ai += 1
merged.append(rank_a[ai]); team_of.append('A'); used.add(rank_a[ai]); countA += 1
else:
while rank_b[bi] in used: bi += 1
merged.append(rank_b[bi]); team_of.append('B'); used.add(rank_b[bi]); countB += 1
return merged, team_of
# --- Credit a click to a team, aggregate over sessions ---
def score_click(team_of, clicked_pos, wins):
wins[team_of[clicked_pos]] += 1
# --- Long-term novelty-decay model fit on weekly deltas ---
def short_term(t, delta_inf, beta, tau):
return delta_inf + beta * np.exp(-t/tau)
