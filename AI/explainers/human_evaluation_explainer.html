Understanding Human Evaluation
▼

What is it? Human evaluation is the process of using human judgment to assess the quality or characteristics of data, systems, or models. In machine learning, it's often used to establish ground truth for subjective tasks (e.g., sentiment, creativity, helpfulness), validate model outputs, or compare different models where objective metrics fall short.

Analogy: Imagine you're judging a figure skating competition. There's no single "correct" score; judges (annotators) use their expertise and a rubric (guidelines) to assign scores (ratings). The goal is to ensure the judges' scores are consistent with each other (inter-annotator agreement) and reflect the true quality of the performance, even if subjective. Without human judgment, highly subjective tasks like evaluating a poem or a chatbot's personality would be impossible.

💡 If you remember one thing

Human evaluation is the gold standard for subjective tasks where no objective metric exists. However, human judgments are inherently noisy and prone to bias, necessitating rigorous methods for data collection and, crucially, for measuring inter-annotator agreement (IAA).

2
Methods for Collecting Human Judgments
▼

1. Likert Scales (Rating Scales)

Mechanism: Present an item (e.g., an AI response, an image) and ask annotators to rate it on an ordinal scale, typically 3-point, 5-point, or 7-point (e.g., "Strongly Disagree" to "Strongly Agree," or "Very Bad" to "Excellent"). Scores are then often averaged or summed.

Pros: Intuitive, easy to implement, provides a granular assessment of quality, efficient for evaluating many items.

Cons: Subjectivity (what "good" means varies), central tendency bias (annotators avoid extremes), cultural/language bias, scale interpretation differences (one person's "4" is another's "3"). Statistical analysis can be tricky as the data is ordinal, not truly interval.

2. Pairwise Preference

Mechanism: Present two items (A and B) and ask the annotator to choose which one is "better" according to a specific criterion, or if they are "equal." For \(N\) items, this requires \(\binom{N}{2}\) comparisons if all pairs are evaluated, or a subset if using techniques like Elo ratings or tournament-style evaluation.

Pros: Simpler cognitive task ("A or B?") reduces individual scale interpretation bias, often more robust to subtle differences, less prone to central tendency bias. Can be used to build a robust ranking.

Cons: Can be very time-consuming for many items, potential for intransitivity (A>B, B>C, but C>A), doesn't provide absolute scores, only relative rankings.

Common Challenges in Human Evaluation

  • Ambiguity: Poorly defined guidelines lead to inconsistent judgments.
  • Bias: Annotator background, expectations, or fatigue can skew results.
  • Fatigue: Long evaluation sessions can lead to rushed or less thoughtful judgments.
  • Cost: Human annotation is expensive and time-consuming.
🔑 Key Property

No matter the collection method, if multiple annotators are involved, measuring Inter-Annotator Agreement (IAA) is critical. IAA quantifies the reliability of the human data, indicating whether the "ground truth" is truly consistent or just noisy.

3
Quantifying Agreement: Kappa & Alpha
▼

Observed Agreement vs. Chance Agreement

The simplest measure, Observed Agreement (\(P_o\)), is just the percentage of times annotators agree. But this is insufficient: two annotators might agree often purely by chance, especially if one category is very common. We need to correct for this.

$$P_o = \frac{\text{Number of items where all annotators agree}}{\text{Total number of items}}$$

Chance Agreement (\(P_e\)) is the proportion of agreement expected if annotators were making their choices randomly, based on their individual marginal distributions. For two annotators \(A_1, A_2\) and categories \(k\):

$$P_e = \sum_{k} P(A_1=k) P(A_2=k)$$

where \(P(A_x=k)\) is the probability of annotator \(x\) choosing category \(k\). This is derived from the outer product of their marginal probabilities.

Cohen's Kappa (\(\kappa\))

Cohen's Kappa (Cohen, 1960) adjusts observed agreement by subtracting the agreement due to chance, then dividing by the maximum possible agreement beyond chance. It's suitable for two annotators rating items on a categorical (nominal or ordinal) scale.

$$\kappa = \frac{P_o - P_e}{1 - P_e}$$

Interpretation:

  • \(\kappa = 1\): Perfect agreement.
  • \(\kappa = 0\): Agreement is exactly what would be expected by chance.
  • \(\kappa < 0\): Agreement is worse than chance (rare, suggests systematic disagreement).
Typical ranges for "good" agreement are \(\kappa > 0.6\) or \(\kappa > 0.8\), depending on the domain.

⚠️ Kappa Paradox

Kappa can be misleading when categories are highly skewed (e.g., almost all items are rated "Good"). In such cases, \(P_e\) can be very high, leading to a low \(\kappa\) even if \(P_o\) is high, suggesting low agreement when annotators actually agree on the dominant category.

Krippendorff's Alpha (\(\alpha\))

Krippendorff's Alpha (Krippendorff, 1970) is a more general and robust agreement measure. It can handle:

  • Any number of annotators (2 or more).
  • Any number of categories.
  • Missing data (some items not rated by all annotators).
  • Various levels of measurement: nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio scales.
The general formula is:
$$\alpha = 1 - \frac{D_o}{D_e}$$

where \(D_o\) is the observed disagreement and \(D_e\) is the expected disagreement (what would be seen by chance). The calculation of \(D_o\) and \(D_e\) depends on the chosen metric for "disagreement" (e.g., simple mismatch for nominal, squared difference for interval). This flexibility makes Alpha the preferred choice for complex annotation setups, though its calculation is more involved.

🔑 Key Property

Both Kappa and Alpha correct for chance agreement, providing a more meaningful measure of consensus than simple percent agreement. They are essential for establishing the reliability and validity of human-annotated datasets.

Key Takeaways
▼

Choosing the Right Evaluation Method:

  • Likert Scales: Good for quick, granular feedback on many items. Best when the rating criteria are clear and annotators are well-calibrated. Often used for overall quality, helpfulness, or sentiment.
  • Pairwise Preference: Ideal for subtle comparisons or when absolute scoring is difficult. Reduces cognitive load and often yields more robust relative rankings. Useful for A/B testing or ranking models.

Choosing the Right Agreement Metric:

  • Cohen's Kappa (\(\kappa\)): The go-to for measuring agreement between two annotators on categorical data (nominal or ordinal). Simple to calculate and widely understood.
  • Krippendorff's Alpha (\(\alpha\)): The more robust and versatile choice for multiple annotators, any number of categories, and any measurement level (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio). Handles missing data gracefully.
  • Simple Percent Agreement: Avoid as a standalone metric, as it doesn't account for chance agreement, leading to inflated perceptions of reliability.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid:

  • Poor Guidelines: Ambiguous instructions are the #1 killer of IAA. Invest in clear, detailed rubrics.
  • Ignoring Chance: Relying solely on raw percent agreement overestimates true consensus.
  • Uncalibrated Annotators: Even with good guidelines, humans need training and calibration rounds to align their interpretations.
  • Scale Misuse: Treating ordinal Likert data as true interval data for statistical tests can be problematic.
💡 Big Picture

High inter-annotator agreement is a prerequisite for a trustworthy human-labeled dataset. If humans can't consistently agree on the labels, then even a perfect machine learning model won't be able to learn the underlying patterns reliably, and its performance will be capped by the human noise.

Code Trace: Calculating Cohen's Kappa
▼
import numpy as np
from collections import Counter
def cohen_kappa(annotator1_ratings, annotator2_ratings, num_categories):
# Ensure ratings are aligned and same length
assert len(annotator1_ratings) == len(annotator2_ratings)
N = len(annotator1_ratings) # Total number of items
# 1. Build the agreement matrix (confusion matrix)
# Matrix[i][j] = count where A1 rated i and A2 rated j
agreement_matrix = np.zeros((num_categories, num_categories))
for r1, r2 in zip(annotator1_ratings, annotator2_ratings):
# Adjust for 0-indexed categories if ratings are 1-indexed
agreement_matrix[r1-1][r2-1] += 1
# 2. Calculate Observed Agreement (Po)
# Sum of diagonal elements / Total N
Po = np.trace(agreement_matrix) / N
# 3. Calculate Expected Agreement (Pe)
# Marginal probabilities for each annotator and category
marginal_A1 = np.sum(agreement_matrix, axis=1) / N # P(A1=k)
marginal_A2 = np.sum(agreement_matrix, axis=0) / N # P(A2=k)
Pe = np.sum(marginal_A1 * marginal_A2) # Sum(P(A1=k) * P(A2=k))
# 4. Calculate Kappa
if Pe == 1: # Avoid division by zero if perfect chance agreement
return 1.0 if Po == 1 else 0.0
kappa = (Po - Pe) / (1 - Pe)
return kappa
