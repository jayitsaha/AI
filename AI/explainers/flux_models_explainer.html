1
Understanding FLUX Models
▼

What is it? FLUX models, often known as Normalizing Flows, are a class of generative models that explicitly learn a sequence of invertible transformations to map a simple probability distribution (like a Gaussian noise) to a complex data distribution. Unlike VAEs or GANs, they can compute the exact likelihood of a data point, making them powerful for density estimation.

Analogy: Imagine you have a blob of play-doh (representing a simple, known probability distribution). A FLUX model is like a series of precise, reversible "stretching" and "squishing" operations that transform this play-doh blob into a complex, recognizable sculpture (your target data distribution). Because each operation is reversible, you can always go back from the sculpture to the original blob, and you know exactly how much volume each stretch/squish changed, allowing you to calculate the density of any part of the sculpture.

💡 If you remember one thing

FLUX models define a transformation \(x = f(z)\) where \(f\) is invertible and differentiable. This allows calculating the exact probability density \(p_X(x)\) of a data point \(x\) using the change of variables formula: \(p_X(x) = p_Z(f^{-1}(x)) \left| \det \left(\frac{\partial f^{-1}(x)}{\partial x}\right) \right|\). This property is crucial for tasks like anomaly detection and exact likelihood training.

2
How FLUX Models Work Internally
▼

The Flow of Transformations

A FLUX model constructs a complex mapping \(f\) by chaining together many simple, invertible, and differentiable transformations. Starting with a latent variable \(z_0 \sim p_Z(z_0)\) (typically a standard Gaussian or uniform distribution), we apply a sequence of \(K\) transformations:

$$z_1 = f_1(z_0) \\ z_2 = f_2(z_1) \\ \vdots \\ x = z_K = f_K(z_{K-1})$$

The overall transformation is \(x = f(z_0) = f_K \circ \dots \circ f_1(z_0)\). Each individual transformation \(f_k\) is designed to be simple enough that its inverse \(f_k^{-1}\) and the determinant of its Jacobian matrix \(\left| \det \left(\frac{\partial f_k}{\partial z_{k-1}}\right) \right|\) are easy to compute. Common choices for \(f_k\) include coupling layers (e.g., Real NVP, Glow) or autoregressive transformations (e.g., MAF).

Design Decision: Why Invertible and Differentiable?

The invertibility of \(f\) is key for two reasons:

  1. Generation: To generate new data \(x\), we simply sample \(z_0\) from the simple base distribution and push it forward through the flow: \(x = f(z_0)\).
  2. Density Estimation / Encoding: To evaluate the likelihood of a data point \(x\), we need to find its corresponding latent \(z_0 = f^{-1}(x)\) and apply the change of variables formula. The differentiability is necessary for computing the Jacobian determinant and for training via gradient descent.

The ability to compute \(f^{-1}(x)\) means we can map any data point \(x\) back to its latent representation \(z_0\), which is useful for tasks like anomaly detection (data points with low likelihood, or far from the origin in latent space, are anomalous).

🔑 Key Property

The transformations are typically designed such that the Jacobian determinant \(\left| \det \left(\frac{\partial f_k}{\partial z_{k-1}}\right) \right|\) is either triangular (for autoregressive flows) or block-triangular (for coupling flows), making its determinant calculation efficient (product of diagonal elements).

3
Why It Works — The Change of Variables Formula
▼

The Change of Variables Formula for Probability Densities

Let \(z \sim p_Z(z)\) be a random variable with known density, and let \(x = f(z)\) be a transformation such that \(f\) is invertible and differentiable. The probability density of \(x\), denoted \(p_X(x)\), is given by:

$$p_X(x) = p_Z(f^{-1}(x)) \left| \det \left(\frac{\partial f^{-1}(x)}{\partial x}\right) \right|$$

This formula relates the density in the data space to the density in the latent space, scaled by the absolute determinant of the Jacobian of the inverse transformation. Alternatively, using the Jacobian of the forward transformation, we have:

$$p_X(x) = p_Z(z) \left| \det \left(\frac{\partial f(z)}{\partial z}\right)^{-1} \right|_{z=f^{-1}(x)} = p_Z(f^{-1}(x)) \left| \det \left(\frac{\partial f(z)}{\partial z}\right) \right|_{z=f^{-1}(x)}^{-1}$$

The term \(\left| \det \left(\frac{\partial f(z)}{\partial z}\right) \right|\) measures how much the volume of an infinitesimal region around \(z\) changes after the transformation \(f\). A larger determinant means the volume expands, so the density must decrease proportionally to conserve probability mass.

Log-Likelihood for Training

For a chained sequence of transformations \(x = f_K \circ \dots \circ f_1(z_0)\), the log-likelihood of \(x\) is given by summing the log-densities and log-Jacobian determinants:

$$\log p_X(x) = \log p_Z(z_0) - \sum_{k=1}^K \log \left| \det \left(\frac{\partial f_k(z_{k-1})}{\partial z_{k-1}}\right) \right|$$

where \(z_0 = f^{-1}(x) = f_1^{-1} \circ \dots \circ f_K^{-1}(x)\). Training a FLUX model involves maximizing this log-likelihood, which means learning the parameters of each \(f_k\) such that the transformed noise distribution closely matches the data distribution.

⚠️ Computational Cost

The main computational bottleneck for normalizing flows is often the calculation of the Jacobian determinant. For a general transformation \(f: \mathbb{R}^D \to \mathbb{R}^D\), the Jacobian is a \(D \times D\) matrix, and its determinant costs \(O(D^3)\). This is why practical flow architectures employ specific designs (like coupling layers or autoregressive structures) that make the Jacobian matrix triangular or block-triangular, reducing the determinant computation to \(O(D)\) or \(O(D^2)\).

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use FLUX models (Normalizing Flows):

  • Exact Likelihood Estimation: When you need to compute the precise probability density \(p(x)\) of data points, for example, in anomaly detection or for rigorous comparison of generative models.
  • High-Quality Sample Generation: Flows can generate samples by simply passing latent variables through the forward mapping \(f(z)\), leading to high-quality and diverse outputs.
  • Invertibility: The ability to map data to latent space (\(f^{-1}(x)\)) and back is valuable for tasks like data compression or disentangled representation learning.
  • Data Transformation: When you want to learn a reversible mapping between two distributions.

When NOT to use:

  • Very High-Dimensional Data: While advancements like Glow address this, the computational cost of Jacobian determinants can still be a bottleneck in extremely high dimensions if not carefully designed.
  • Highly Complex Transformations: Designing individual \(f_k\) layers that are both expressive and have an efficiently computable Jacobian determinant can be challenging.
  • Slow Inference: If the number of layers \(K\) is very large, both forward (generation) and inverse (density) passes can be slow, as they involve chaining all transformations.
💡 Big Picture

FLUX models bridge the gap between generative modeling and density estimation, offering a unique combination of exact likelihoods, high-quality generation, and invertible mappings. They are a powerful tool for understanding and manipulating complex data distributions.
