R² & Adjusted R²

Coefficient of Determination — Regression Evaluation Metric
Intermediate Classical ML Regression Metrics Part III — Evaluation
1
Understanding the Concept

What is R²?

Imagine you are trying to predict house prices. The simplest possible prediction is "just guess the average price every time." R² tells you: how much better is your model than that naive average baseline?

An R² of 0.85 means your model explains 85% of the variance in the target — it is 85% of the way from "always predict the mean" to "predict perfectly."

💡 The One Thing to Remember

R² = 1 − (error your model makes) / (error the mean makes). It is a relative measure against the do-nothing baseline. On test data it can go negative — meaning your model is worse than just predicting the mean.

Everyday Analogy

Think of variance in house prices as a target's total wobble. If you just threw darts blindly (predicting the mean), your residual wobble is SStot. Your trained model reduces that wobble to SSres. R² = 1 − (wobble left / original wobble). Perfect prediction → zero wobble left → R² = 1.

Why Adjusted R²?

R² never decreases when you add more features — even noise features give a tiny free boost. Adjusted R² applies a penalty for each extra predictor, so it only improves if the new feature earns its keep statistically.

⚠️ Critical Warning

R² can be negative on test/validation sets. This is not a bug — it means your model generalises so poorly that predicting the training mean would have been better. A high train-R² with negative test-R² is a severe overfitting signal.

2
How It Works — The Math

The Three Sums of Squares

$$SS_{tot} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \bar{y})^2 \quad \text{(total variance in }y\text{)}$$
$$SS_{res} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 \quad \text{(unexplained residual variance)}$$
$$SS_{reg} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})^2 \quad \text{(explained variance)}$$

For OLS regression: \(SS_{tot} = SS_{res} + SS_{reg}\) (not guaranteed for other models).

R² Definition

$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{SS_{res}}{SS_{tot}}$$

Range: \((-\infty, 1]\). On training data with an intercept term, OLS guarantees \(0 \le R^2 \le 1\). On held-out data, or for models without an intercept, \(R^2 < 0\) is possible.

Adjusted R² Definition

$$\bar{R}^2 = 1 - \frac{SS_{res}/(n-p-1)}{SS_{tot}/(n-1)} = 1 - (1 - R^2)\frac{n-1}{n-p-1}$$

where \(n\) = number of observations and \(p\) = number of predictors (excluding intercept). The ratio \((n-1)/(n-p-1) > 1\), so \(\bar{R}^2 \le R^2\) always. Adding a useless predictor increases \(p\), inflating the penalty factor.

📐 Numerical Example

Suppose \(n=10\), \(R^2 = 0.80\), and we compare \(p=1\) vs \(p=5\) predictors:

\(\bar{R}^2_{p=1} = 1 - 0.20 \cdot \tfrac{9}{8} = 0.775\)

\(\bar{R}^2_{p=5} = 1 - 0.20 \cdot \tfrac{9}{4} = 0.550\)

Same raw R² but adjusted R² drops from 0.775 to 0.550 — the extra 4 predictors are penalised heavily with only 10 samples.

3
Properties, Pitfalls & When R² < 0

✅ Valid interpretations

  • Proportion of variance explained by the model
  • Relative improvement over constant-mean predictor
  • Comparable across nested models on the same dataset
  • Adjusted R² useful for feature selection (penalises complexity)

❌ Common misuses

  • Comparing R² across different datasets (it depends on SStot)
  • Using R² for non-linear relationships without caution
  • Ignoring residual plots — high R² can coexist with model misspecification
  • Treating R² as an absolute goodness-of-fit for forecasting

Why R² Can Be Negative on Test Data

During training (OLS, with intercept), the optimisation guarantees \(\hat{y}\) is at least as good as \(\bar{y}_{train}\). On a new test set, three things can make \(SS_{res} > SS_{tot}\):

  1. Distribution shift: test \(\bar{y}_{test} \ne \bar{y}_{train}\), so predictions based on training patterns miss the test mean.
  2. Overfitting: model memorised noise; predictions are wild on unseen points.
  3. No-intercept model: even on training data, forced-zero intercept can yield R² < 0.
🔴 Negative R² Signals

Test R² = −0.4 means \(SS_{res}\) is 40% larger than \(SS_{tot}\). Your model's predictions are further from truth than the test mean is — a concrete sign of severe overfitting or distribution mismatch. Always report test R², not just train R².

R² vs Alternatives — When to Use What
Metric Formula Range Sensitive to scale? Best used when
R² \(1 - SS_{res}/SS_{tot}\) \((-\infty, 1]\) No (dimensionless) Comparing models on same dataset; checking relative fit
Adjusted R² \(1 - (1-R^2)\frac{n-1}{n-p-1}\) \((-\infty, 1]\) No Feature selection; nested model comparison
RMSE \(\sqrt{SS_{res}/n}\) \([0, \infty)\) Yes (same units as y) Penalising large errors; same units interpretation
MAE \(\frac{1}{n}\sum|y_i - \hat{y}_i|\) \([0, \infty)\) Yes Robust to outliers; median-like interpretation
MAPE \(\frac{1}{n}\sum\frac{|y_i - \hat{y}_i|}{|y_i|}\) \([0, \infty)\) No (percentage) Relative error; comparing across datasets
🎯 Decision Rule

Use R² when you want a scale-free measure of explained variance relative to the mean baseline. Use Adjusted R² when selecting among models with different numbers of features. Use RMSE/MAE when you need to communicate error in the original units (e.g., "±$8,000"). Never use R² alone — always pair with residual plots.
