Quasiconvex & Strongly Convex Functions

The three-tier hierarchy that governs optimization: quasiconvex ⊃ convex ⊃ strongly convex
Intermediate Calculus & Optimization Math Foundations Part I · Section 0.2
1
The Landscape of Convexity
▼

Why do these classes matter? Virtually every optimizer in machine learning — gradient descent, SGD, Adam, L-BFGS — exploits structure in the loss landscape. Knowing which class your objective belongs to tells you exactly what kind of guarantee you can obtain: will you find any minimizer, a global one, or will you converge at a linear rate?

The hierarchy is a strict inclusion: strongly convex ⊂ convex ⊂ quasiconvex. Each outer ring adds exotic functions that the inner ring cannot handle, while each inner ring buys you a sharper convergence theorem.

Quasiconvex: all sublevel sets convex
⊃
Convex: chord ≥ function
⊃
Strongly Convex: above quadratic bowl
💡 The One-Sentence Ladder

Quasiconvex guarantees no spurious local minima (every sublevel set is connected). Convex additionally guarantees every local minimum is global. Strongly convex further guarantees a unique global minimum and linear (geometric) convergence of gradient descent.

Analogy: mountain hiking

Imagine a mountain landscape. A quasiconvex height function means: if you draw any altitude contour (sublevel set = "below 1000 m"), it carves out a single connected valley — no isolated pockets. You can always walk downhill and find a basin. A convex landscape goes further: the terrain between any two points is always lower than the straight-line path in 3D — no ridges or bumps. A strongly convex landscape is like a perfect parabolic bowl: uniquely bowl-shaped, so gradient descent rolls to the unique bottom without hesitation.

Formal Definitions & Equivalent Characterizations
▼

Quasiconvexity

A function \(f : \mathcal{C} \to \mathbb{R}\) on a convex set \(\mathcal{C}\) is quasiconvex if for all \(x, y \in \mathcal{C}\) and \(t \in [0,1]\):

$$f\!\left(tx + (1-t)y\right) \;\le\; \max\!\left\{f(x),\, f(y)\right\}$$

Equivalently, all sublevel sets \(S_\alpha = \{x \in \mathcal{C} : f(x) \le \alpha\}\) are convex for every \(\alpha \in \mathbb{R}\). This is often the more useful characterization in practice.

📐 Sublevel Set Test

f is quasiconvex \(\iff\) for every \(\alpha\), the sublevel set \(\{x : f(x) \le \alpha\}\) is convex. Example: \(f(x) = -e^{-|x|}\) — each contour \(-e^{-|x|} \le c\) defines an interval \(|x| \le -\ln(-c)\) (convex), but the function itself is not convex (chord can fall below the function).

Convexity

A function \(f\) is convex if for all \(x, y\) and \(t \in [0,1]\):

$$f\!\left(tx + (1-t)y\right) \;\le\; t\,f(x) + (1-t)\,f(y)$$

The right-hand side is the value on the chord between \((x, f(x))\) and \((y, f(y))\). Convexity says the function lies on or below every chord. First-order equivalent (when differentiable): \(f(x) \ge f(y) + \nabla f(y)^\top(x-y)\) — the tangent hyperplane is a global underestimator. Second-order (when twice differentiable): \(\nabla^2 f(x) \succeq 0\) everywhere.

Strong Convexity

A function \(f\) is \(\mu\)-strongly convex (\(\mu > 0\)) if the function \(g(x) = f(x) - \frac{\mu}{2}\|x\|^2\) is convex, or equivalently, for all \(x, y\):

$$f(x) \;\ge\; f(y) + \nabla f(y)^\top(x - y) + \frac{\mu}{2}\|x - y\|^2$$

This is the first-order convexity inequality with a strictly positive quadratic gap. Strong convexity means \(f\) lies strictly above the tangent plane — it is bounded below by a paraboloid of curvature \(\mu\). Second-order equivalent: \(\nabla^2 f(x) \succeq \mu I\) (smallest eigenvalue \(\ge \mu\)).

🔑 Unique Minimizer Theorem

Every \(\mu\)-strongly convex function has a unique global minimizer \(x^*\). Proof: suppose \(x^*\) and \(y^*\) are both minimizers. By strong convexity applied at \(x = y^* + (1-t)(x^*-y^*)\), the gap \(\frac{\mu}{2}\|x^*-y^*\|^2 \le 0\), forcing \(x^* = y^*\).

PhD Deep-Dive: Strong Convexity & Convergence
▼

The Fundamental Inequality for Strongly Convex Functions

If \(f\) is \(\mu\)-strongly convex and \(L\)-smooth (i.e., \(\nabla f\) is \(L\)-Lipschitz, meaning \(\nabla^2 f \preceq LI\)), then for any \(x, y\):

$$\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-y\|^2 \;\le\; f(x) - f(y) - \nabla f(y)^\top(x-y) \;\le\; \frac{L}{2}\|x-y\|^2$$

The left inequality is strong convexity (excess above tangent is at least quadratic). The right is \(L\)-smoothness (excess is at most quadratic). Together they sandwich the curvature of \(f\) between \(\mu\) and \(L\).

Linear Convergence of Gradient Descent

Gradient descent with step \(\eta = 1/L\) on a \(\mu\)-strongly convex, \(L\)-smooth objective satisfies:

$$f(x_{k+1}) - f(x^*) \;\le\; \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\right)\!\left(f(x_k) - f(x^*)\right)$$

Since the contraction factor \(\rho = 1 - \mu/L \in [0,1)\), the gap shrinks geometrically each step:

$$f(x_k) - f(x^*) \;\le\; \rho^k \!\cdot\! \left(f(x_0) - f(x^*)\right)$$

The condition number \(\kappa = L/\mu\) governs the rate. When \(\kappa = 1\) (perfect bowl), convergence is instant. When \(\kappa \gg 1\) (ill-conditioned), the rate approaches 1 and convergence is slow — this is the motivation for preconditioning and adaptive methods like Adam.

📐 Proof Sketch: Descent Lemma

By \(L\)-smoothness: \(f(x_{k+1}) \le f(x_k) + \nabla f(x_k)^\top(x_{k+1}-x_k) + \frac{L}{2}\|x_{k+1}-x_k\|^2\). With \(x_{k+1} = x_k - \frac{1}{L}\nabla f(x_k)\), this becomes \(f(x_{k+1}) \le f(x_k) - \frac{1}{2L}\|\nabla f(x_k)\|^2\). Now use the PL inequality (implied by strong convexity): \(\|\nabla f(x)\|^2 \ge 2\mu(f(x)-f(x^*))\) to conclude.

Polyak-Łojasiewicz (PL) Inequality

Strong convexity implies the PL condition, but not vice versa — PL is strictly weaker. A function satisfies PL with parameter \(\mu\) if:

$$\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla f(x)\|^2 \;\ge\; \mu\!\left(f(x) - f^*\right) \quad \forall x$$

Gradient descent still achieves linear convergence under PL even when \(f\) is not strongly convex (e.g., overparameterized neural nets near global minima). This is why "linear convergence" is observed in practice even without strong convexity everywhere.

Quasiconvex Optimization

For quasiconvex \(f\), bisection (binary search) on sublevel sets yields an efficient optimizer. At each step, pick a candidate level \(\alpha\) and solve the feasibility problem: is \(S_\alpha = \{x : f(x) \le \alpha\}\) non-empty? Since \(S_\alpha\) is convex, this is a convex feasibility problem — solvable by projection. This gives an \(O(\log(1/\epsilon))\) oracle complexity for quasiconvex minimization, matching the convex rate for this simpler task.

⚠️ When Strong Convexity Fails in ML

Neural networks have non-convex (and rarely strongly convex) loss landscapes globally. However, near good local minima, the loss is often locally strongly convex — justifying local linear convergence. Regularization (\(\ell_2\) weight decay = \(\mu I\) shift) explicitly adds strong convexity, making the problem well-conditioned.

From-Scratch Code: Checking Convexity Class & GD Rates
▼

The code below implements three things: (1) a numerical sublevel-set convexity check for quasiconvexity, (2) the descent lemma check for convexity, and (3) gradient descent on a strongly convex objective, verifying the predicted linear rate.

import numpy as np
# ── 1. Quasiconvexity check (sublevel set sampling) ─────────────────────
def is_quasiconvex_sample(f, lo=-3, hi=3, N=2000, tol=1e-9):
"""Sample N pairs; quasiconvex iff f(tx+(1-t)y) <= max(f(x),f(y))."""
xs = np.random.uniform(lo, hi, (N, 2))
ts = np.random.uniform(0, 1, N)
mids = ts[:, None] * xs[:, [0]] + (1-ts[:, None]) * xs[:, [1]]
violated = np.sum(f(mids[:,0]) > np.maximum(f(xs[:,0]), f(xs[:,1])) + tol)
return violated == 0, violated
# ── 2. Functions under test ──────────────────────────────────────────────
fqcx = lambda x: -np.exp(-np.abs(x)) # quasiconvex, NOT convex
fcvx = lambda x: np.abs(x) # convex, NOT strongly convex
fscvx = lambda x: x**2 # strongly convex, mu=2
np.random.seed(42)
for name, f in [("quasiconvex", fqcx), ("convex", fcvx), ("s-convex", fscvx)]:
ok, v = is_quasiconvex_sample(f)
print(f"{name:12s}: quasiconvex={ok}, violations={v}")
# Output: all three pass (strongly convex ⊂ convex ⊂ quasiconvex)
# ── 3. Gradient descent on x^2 (strongly convex, mu=L=2) ───────────────
def grad_descent(f, grad_f, x0, lr, n_steps):
x = x0; history = [float(f(x))]
for _ in range(n_steps):
x = x - lr * grad_f(x)
history.append(float(f(x)))
return np.array(history)
mu, L = 2.0, 2.0 # f(x)=x^2: grad=2x, Hessian=2
rho = 1 - mu / L # = 0.0 (converges in 1 step!)
hist = grad_descent(lambda x: x**2, lambda x: 2*x, x0=3.0, lr=1/L, n_steps=10)
print("f(x_k):", np.round(hist, 6))
# [9.0, 0.0, 0.0, ...] — exact 1 step (rho=0)
# More interesting: f(x) = x^2 + 0.1*x^4 (strongly convex, larger L)
f2 = lambda x: x**2 + 0.1*x**4
grad_f2 = lambda x: 2*x + 0.4*x**3
# At x0=3: L ~ 2 + 1.2*9 = 12.8 (local Lipschitz of grad)
hist2 = grad_descent(f2, grad_f2, x0=3.0, lr=0.07, n_steps=30)
print("Gap (f - f*) after 30 steps:", hist2[-1] - f2(0))
# Should be near 0 — linear convergence guaranteed

Verifying the Rate Bound

For \(f(x) = x^2\): \(\mu = 2\), \(L = 2\), so \(\rho = 1 - \mu/L = 0\). Gradient descent converges in one step. This is exact for pure quadratics. For \(f(x) = x^2 + 0.1x^4\), the local condition number \(\kappa \approx 6\) at \(x_0=3\), giving \(\rho \approx 1 - 1/6 \approx 0.83\) — about 62 steps to reach \(10^{-6}\) accuracy. This matches np.log(1e6)/np.log(1/0.83) ≈ 62.

Interview Q&A & Common Gotchas
▼

Q1: What is the difference between convex and quasiconvex?

✅ Model Answer

A convex function satisfies the chord condition: \(f(tx+(1-t)y) \le tf(x)+(1-t)f(y)\). A quasiconvex function only satisfies the weaker condition \(f(tx+(1-t)y) \le \max(f(x),f(y))\). Every convex function is quasiconvex, but not vice versa. The canonical counterexample is \(f(x)=-e^{-|x|}\): its sublevel sets are all intervals (quasiconvex), but the chord between two distant points can fall below the function (not convex).

Q2: Why does strong convexity guarantee a unique minimizer?

✅ Model Answer

Suppose \(x^*\) and \(y^*\) are both minimizers. Apply the strong convexity condition at both points and add: \(0 \le -({\mu}/{2})\|x^*-y^*\|^2\), which is only satisfied when \(x^* = y^*\). Intuitively, the quadratic lower bowl around every point means no two distinct points can both sit at the global bottom.

Q3: What does the condition number \(\kappa = L/\mu\) mean intuitively?

✅ Model Answer

\(\kappa\) measures "how elliptical the bowl is." When \(\kappa=1\), the bowl is a perfect sphere — gradient descent points directly at the minimum and converges in 1 step. When \(\kappa=100\), the bowl is very elongated — gradient descent zig-zags slowly, taking \(O(\kappa \log 1/\epsilon)\) steps. Preconditioning (e.g., Newton's method, Adam) tries to reduce the effective \(\kappa\).

Q4: Is a sum of strongly convex functions strongly convex?

✅ Model Answer

Yes. If \(f\) is \(\mu_1\)-strongly convex and \(g\) is \(\mu_2\)-strongly convex, then \(f+g\) is \((\mu_1+\mu_2)\)-strongly convex. This is why adding \(\ell_2\) regularization \(\frac{\lambda}{2}\|w\|^2\) (which is \(\lambda\)-strongly convex) to any convex loss makes the total objective strongly convex with \(\mu \ge \lambda\).

Q5: Can a non-convex function have linear convergence for GD?

✅ Model Answer

Yes — if it satisfies the Polyak-Łojasiewicz (PL) condition \(\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla f(x)\|^2 \ge \mu(f(x)-f^*)\). The PL condition implies that every stationary point is a global minimum (no spurious local minima), even without convexity. It is strictly weaker than strong convexity. Overparameterized neural networks near global minima often satisfy PL locally, explaining observed linear convergence in practice.

⚠️ Common Mistake: "Unimodal = Quasiconvex"

Unimodal on \(\mathbb{R}\) (one peak/valley) is equivalent to quasiconvex only for real-valued functions on \(\mathbb{R}\). In higher dimensions, quasiconvexity is strictly about convex sublevel sets — a function can be unimodal along every line but still fail quasiconvexity in \(\mathbb{R}^n\). Always use the sublevel-set definition in \(n>1\).
