Huber Loss

Robustness to Outliers — the Best of MSE and MAE
Intermediate Classical ML Loss Functions Part III · Evaluation & Robustness
1
Understanding the Concept

When you train a regression model, you need a loss function — a way to measure how wrong your predictions are. The two classics are Mean Squared Error (MSE) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE). Each has a fatal flaw.

MSE squares every error, so a single large outlier (say, a typo that wrote 1 000 000 instead of 10) contributes one trillion times more to the loss than the correct data point. The model contorts itself to accommodate that single freak value, wrecking its predictions everywhere else.

MAE treats every error equally regardless of size — good for outlier resistance, but its gradient is constant (always ±1), which causes slow, noisy convergence near the optimum.

💡 Key Insight

Huber loss behaves like MSE for small errors (smooth gradient, fast convergence) and like MAE for large errors (bounded influence, outlier resistance). A single tunable parameter \(\delta\) sets the boundary.

Real-world analogy: Imagine you're scoring job applicants. For candidates near your benchmark, small differences in score matter a lot — you want fine-grained discrimination (MSE behaviour). But if one applicant clearly lies about their PhD, you cap your disappointment at a fixed level and move on — you don't let that one fraudster dominate your entire hiring process (MAE behaviour). Huber loss does exactly this: it caps how much any single data point can hurt you.

📌 One-Sentence Summary

Huber loss is a piecewise function that is quadratic for \(|r| \le \delta\) and linear for \(|r| > \delta\), giving MSE-like precision near zero and MAE-like robustness in the tails.

2
How It Works — Internals & Mathematics

Let the residual (prediction error) be \(r = y - \hat{y}\). The Huber loss is:

$$L_\delta(r) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{2}\,r^2 & \text{if } |r| \le \delta \\[8pt] \delta\bigl(|r| - \tfrac{1}{2}\delta\bigr) & \text{if } |r| > \delta \end{cases}$$

The two pieces join seamlessly: at \(|r| = \delta\), both expressions equal \(\tfrac{1}{2}\delta^2\), and both derivatives equal \(\pm\delta\) — the function is continuously differentiable (\(C^1\)).

The gradient (used in back-propagation / gradient descent) is:

$$\frac{\partial L_\delta}{\partial r} = \begin{cases} r & \text{if } |r| \le \delta \\ \delta \cdot \operatorname{sign}(r) & \text{if } |r| > \delta \end{cases}$$
📐 Why the Gradient Bound Matters

For MSE the gradient is \(\partial r^2/\partial r = 2r\), which grows without bound. An outlier with \(r=100\) pushes the parameters 100× harder than one with \(r=1\). For Huber loss, any residual beyond \(\delta\) contributes a gradient of at most \(\delta\) — the influence is capped.

The Influence Function — the Rigorous Story

In robust statistics, the influence function \(\text{IF}(r)\) of an estimator measures how much a single data point with residual \(r\) changes the fitted parameters. For a loss \(\rho(r)\):

$$\text{IF}(r) \propto \psi(r) = \rho'(r)$$

The three loss functions have radically different \(\psi\) (score) functions:

Loss\(\rho(r)\)\(\psi(r) = \rho'(r)\)Influence as \(r\to\infty\)
MSE\(\frac{1}{2}r^2\)\(r\)Unbounded — grows linearly
MAE\(|r|\)\(\operatorname{sign}(r)\)Bounded (±1) everywhere
Huberpiecewise\(r\) then \(\delta\cdot\operatorname{sign}(r)\)Bounded at \(\pm\delta\) for \(|r|>\delta\)

MSE's influence function is a straight line through the origin — no ceiling. A residual of 1 000 exerts 1 000× the influence of a residual of 1. Huber's influence function rises like MSE until it hits \(\pm\delta\) and then flatlines. Outliers beyond \(\delta\) all exert the same bounded nudge on parameters.

⚠️ Common Misconception

Huber loss does not ignore outliers — it still penalises them (linearly). It just prevents a single catastrophic residual from dominating the optimisation landscape. MAE is actually more outlier-resistant in one sense, but its non-differentiability at zero makes optimisation harder.

Choosing \(\delta\): A Practical Guide

The hyperparameter \(\delta\) controls the transition boundary:

  • Small \(\delta\) → most residuals treated as "outliers" → behaves like MAE → very robust but slower convergence
  • Large \(\delta\) → most residuals treated quadratically → behaves like MSE → fast convergence but outlier-sensitive
  • \(\delta \to 0\) → Huber → MAE
  • \(\delta \to \infty\) → Huber → MSE (scaled by \(1/\delta\))
💡 Rule of Thumb

Set \(\delta\) to the median absolute deviation (MAD) of the residuals from a preliminary OLS fit: \(\delta \approx 1.4826 \cdot \text{MAD}(\{r_i\})\). This is approximately the standard deviation of errors under a Gaussian model — so "normal" errors get MSE treatment and true outliers get MAE treatment.

Scikit-learn's HuberRegressor exposes this as the epsilon parameter (default 1.35, which corresponds to roughly 95% efficiency relative to OLS under a Gaussian).

⚠️ Do Not Treat δ as Sacred

Cross-validate \(\delta\) like any hyperparameter. The "right" value depends on your data's noise distribution and the severity of contamination you expect.

📌 Decision Rule

Clean Gaussian residuals → MSE. Heavy-tailed noise with many outliers → MAE. Most data is clean but occasional outliers exist → Huber (the common real-world case). Also consider Huber whenever MSE gives bizarre predictions near outlier regions.
