Understanding KKT Conditions
▼

What are KKT conditions? When you solve an optimization problem with inequality constraints — "minimize a function subject to some region being feasible" — the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions are a set of four mathematical tests that any optimal solution must pass. They are the generalization of "set the gradient to zero" for constrained problems.

Analogy: Imagine hiking to the lowest point in a valley (minimizing altitude) but you're fenced in by property boundaries. If the fence isn't blocking you, the lowest point is just the valley floor — gradient equals zero. But if the fence is blocking you, you're pinned against the fence and the gradient points into the fence, not zero. KKT conditions capture exactly this: they tell you which fences are active, how hard you're being pushed into them, and confirm you can't escape to a lower point.

Why do ML practitioners care? KKT conditions arise everywhere: the support vectors in an SVM are defined by complementary slackness; interior-point methods for training neural networks use KKT as the optimality certificate; and understanding duality requires knowing when KKT conditions are both necessary AND sufficient (when constraint qualification holds).

💡 If you remember one thing

At an optimum \(x^*\), the gradient of the objective must be cancelled by the active constraint gradients scaled by non-negative multipliers \(\mu_i\): $$\nabla f(x^*) + \sum_i \mu_i \nabla g_i(x^*) + \sum_j \nu_j \nabla h_j(x^*) = 0$$ and crucially, if a constraint is inactive (\(g_i(x^*) < 0\)), its multiplier must be zero — that is complementary slackness.

2
The Four KKT Conditions
▼

Consider the standard nonlinear program: minimize \(f(x)\) subject to \(g_i(x) \leq 0\) for \(i=1,\ldots,m\) and \(h_j(x)=0\) for \(j=1,\ldots,p\). The Lagrangian is:

$$\mathcal{L}(x, \mu, \nu) = f(x) + \sum_{i=1}^m \mu_i g_i(x) + \sum_{j=1}^p \nu_j h_j(x)$$

A point \(x^*\) with multipliers \((\mu^*, \nu^*)\) satisfies the KKT conditions if all four of the following hold:

① Stationarity — "the gradient balances"

$$\nabla_x \mathcal{L}(x^*, \mu^*, \nu^*) = \nabla f(x^*) + \sum_{i=1}^m \mu_i^* \nabla g_i(x^*) + \sum_{j=1}^p \nu_j^* \nabla h_j(x^*) = 0$$

The gradient of the objective is exactly counterbalanced by the constraint gradients weighted by the multipliers. Geometrically: the objective's gradient lies in the cone spanned by the active constraint gradients.

② Primal Feasibility — "the solution is legal"

$$g_i(x^*) \leq 0 \quad \forall i, \qquad h_j(x^*) = 0 \quad \forall j$$

The solution must satisfy all original constraints. Without this, \(x^*\) isn't even in the feasible set.

③ Dual Feasibility — "multipliers have correct sign"

$$\mu_i^* \geq 0 \quad \forall i$$

Inequality constraint multipliers must be non-negative. A negative \(\mu_i\) would mean the constraint is "pulling" the solution rather than "pushing" it, which contradicts the geometry of the feasible set. Equality constraint multipliers \(\nu_j\) are unrestricted in sign.

④ Complementary Slackness — "inactive constraints are free"

$$\mu_i^* \cdot g_i(x^*) = 0 \quad \forall i$$

For each inequality constraint, either the constraint is active (\(g_i(x^*)=0\)) or its multiplier is zero (\(\mu_i^*=0\)). Both can be zero simultaneously. This is the most distinctive condition: it says you only "pay" for constraints that are actually binding at the optimum.

🔑 Necessary vs Sufficient

KKT conditions are necessary for optimality when a constraint qualification (CQ) holds — e.g., LICQ (linear independence CQ) or Slater's condition (convex programs). They are sufficient when the problem is convex (f and g_i convex, h_j affine). In convex problems, any KKT point is a global minimum.

3
Complementary Slackness Geometry
▼

Complementary slackness \(\mu_i g_i(x^*)=0\) has a beautiful geometric interpretation. Consider the feasible region as a polygon in 2D. The optimum \(x^*\) either:

  • Lies strictly inside — no constraint is active, all \(\mu_i=0\), unconstrained optimum.
  • Lies on one constraint boundary — one \(g_i(x^*)=0\), that \(\mu_i>0\); others are inactive, \(\mu_j=0\).
  • Lies at a corner — multiple constraints active, multiple \(\mu_i>0\).

Complementary slackness tells us which constraints are "load-bearing" at the solution. For an SVM, the support vectors are exactly the training points where \(\mu_i > 0\) — i.e., the margin constraint is active. Points inside the margin have \(\mu_i = 0\) and don't influence the decision boundary at all.

Numerical Example (our problem)

Minimize \(f(x_1,x_2)=(x_1-3)^2+(x_2-3)^2\), subject to \(g_1: x_1+x_2 \leq 4\) and \(g_2: x_1 \leq 2.5\).

Optimal point: \(x^*=(2,2)\). Check:

  • \(g_1(x^*) = 2+2-4 = 0\) → active, so \(\mu_1^* > 0\). We find \(\mu_1^*=2\).
  • \(g_2(x^*) = 2-2.5 = -0.5 < 0\) → inactive, so complementary slackness forces \(\mu_2^*=0\).
$$\nabla f(x^*) + \mu_1^* \nabla g_1 + \mu_2^* \nabla g_2 = \begin{bmatrix}-2\\-2\end{bmatrix} + 2\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix} + 0\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\end{bmatrix} \checkmark$$
⚠️ Common Misconception

"Complementary slackness means the constraint is satisfied with equality." — Wrong. It means \(\mu_i g_i = 0\), a product. Either factor can be zero. An inactive constraint (\(g_i < 0\)) contributes nothing to the optimality conditions because its multiplier is forced to zero, not because you "set it to equality."

Active Set Strategy

The complementary slackness condition motivates the active set method: guess which constraints are active, solve equality-constrained subproblem, verify multiplier signs and primal feasibility, then update the active set. This is the basis of simplex method (linear programs) and active set quadratic programming used in SVM training.

From-Scratch KKT Checker (Python)
▼

This checker verifies all four KKT conditions for a given point and multipliers.

import numpy as np
def check_kkt(x, mus, grad_f, grad_gs, gs):
"""Verify all 4 KKT conditions.
x : candidate optimal point (n,)
mus : Lagrange multipliers for inequalities (m,)
grad_f : gradient of objective at x (n,)
grad_gs: list of constraint gradients at x, each (n,)
gs : constraint values g_i(x), shape (m,)
"""
# ① Stationarity: grad_f + sum(mu_i * grad_g_i) == 0
stationarity = grad_f + sum(mus[i] * grad_gs[i] for i in range(len(mus)))
stat_ok = np.allclose(stationarity, 0, atol=1e-8)
# ② Primal feasibility: g_i(x) <= 0 for all i
pf_ok = np.all(gs <= 1e-8)
# ③ Dual feasibility: mu_i >= 0 for all i
df_ok = np.all(mus >= -1e-8)
# ④ Complementary slackness: mu_i * g_i(x) == 0 for all i
cs_ok = np.allclose(mus * gs, 0, atol=1e-8)
return {"stationarity": stat_ok, "primal_feasibility": pf_ok,
"dual_feasibility": df_ok, "complementary_slackness": cs_ok,
"is_kkt_point": all([stat_ok, pf_ok, df_ok, cs_ok])}
# Example: minimize (x1-3)^2 + (x2-3)^2
# s.t. g1: x1+x2-4 <= 0 (active), g2: x1-2.5 <= 0 (inactive)
x_star = np.array([2.0, 2.0])
mus = np.array([2.0, 0.0]) # mu1=2 (active), mu2=0 (inactive)
grad_f = np.array([2*(x_star[0]-3), 2*(x_star[1]-3)]) # [-2, -2]
grad_g1 = np.array([1.0, 1.0])
grad_g2 = np.array([1.0, 0.0])
gs = np.array([x_star[0]+x_star[1]-4, x_star[0]-2.5]) # [0, -0.5]
result = check_kkt(x_star, mus, grad_f, [grad_g1, grad_g2], gs)
print(result)
# {'stationarity': True, 'primal_feasibility': True,
# 'dual_feasibility': True, 'complementary_slackness': True,
# 'is_kkt_point': True}

Key Takeaways & When KKT Applies
▼
  • KKT = necessary conditions for optimality when a constraint qualification holds. They are like the "fingerprint" of an optimal point.
  • Complementary slackness is the most distinctive condition: only binding constraints matter at the optimum. This is why SVMs have sparse support vectors.
  • Dual feasibility (\(\mu_i \geq 0\)) ensures multipliers "push inward" — they resist constraint violation, not enforce it.
  • Convex programs (convex f, convex g_i, affine h_j): KKT is also sufficient. Any KKT point is a global minimum. This is exploited by every convex optimizer.
  • Non-convex programs: KKT gives necessary conditions only. You may have KKT points that are saddle points or local minima, not global.

Where KKT Appears in ML

🔑 ML Applications

SVM: The dual SVM has \(\alpha_i \geq 0\) (dual feasibility) and complementary slackness ensures that non-support vectors have \(\alpha_i=0\), giving a sparse model. Lasso: Subgradient KKT conditions explain why Lasso produces exact zeros. Interior-point methods: Follow the "central path" of perturbed KKT systems until convergence to the true KKT point. RL: Constrained MDP policy optimization (CPO) uses KKT to balance reward and safety constraint multipliers.

When to Apply KKT

  • Use KKT to verify a candidate solution to any constrained optimization problem.
  • Use KKT to derive closed-form solutions (e.g., the SVM dual, LASSO solution path).
  • Do NOT rely on KKT sufficiency unless the problem is convex or the point is a global minimizer by other means.
