Non-Convex Loss Landscapes in Deep Learning

Why saddle points and plateaus — not poor local minima — are the real obstacle, and how SGD navigates them
Intermediate Optimization Deep Learning Review Track
1
The Core Insight
▼

What is this? When you train a neural network, you're searching for parameters that minimise a loss function. For simple models like linear regression, this loss is a smooth bowl — any descent path reaches the same bottom. For deep networks, the loss is a wild, high-dimensional terrain: ridges, valleys, plateaus, and strange flat regions called saddle points.

The puzzle: If the landscape is so complicated, why does training work at all? The surprising answer: it mostly works because of what's missing. Deep networks don't fail due to hopeless local minima — they fail (when they fail) due to saddle points and flat plateaus. And both of those are navigable.

💡 If you remember one thing

In high-dimensional non-convex landscapes, the dominant obstacle is saddle points, not poor local minima. Almost all low-loss critical points are global or near-global minima that are roughly equivalent in quality. Over-parameterization and SGD noise conspire to make training work despite non-convexity.

Convex vs Non-Convex: What Changes
▼

Convex Functions: The Easy Case

A function \(f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}\) is convex if for all \(x, y\) and \(\lambda \in [0,1]\):

$$f(\lambda x + (1-\lambda)y) \le \lambda f(x) + (1-\lambda)f(y)$$

Geometrically: the chord between any two points lies above the graph. Critically, every local minimum is a global minimum, and gradient descent is guaranteed to converge. Examples: mean squared error, logistic loss, SVMs, \(\ell_2\) regression.

Why Deep Networks Break Convexity

A neural network computes \(\hat{y} = \sigma_L(W_L \cdots \sigma_1(W_1 x))\). The loss \(\mathcal{L}(\theta)\) is a composition of non-linear functions of the parameters \(\theta = \{W_1, \ldots, W_L\}\). Even with convex individual activations (like ReLU), compositions of non-linear functions are generically non-convex. For example, with two layers and parameters \((w_1, w_2)\):

$$\mathcal{L}(w_1, w_2) = \ell\!\left(\sigma(w_2 \cdot \sigma(w_1 x))\right)$$

has multiple critical points, including saddles and (possibly) multiple local minima of different quality.

🔑 Key Structural Difference

Convex: \(\nabla f = 0 \Rightarrow\) global minimum. Any first-order method converges.
Non-convex: \(\nabla f = 0\) could mean local min, local max, saddle point, or flat plateau. Convergence to global min not guaranteed — but empirically happens anyway for deep nets.

Critical Points: Saddles, Plateaus, and Minima
▼

A critical point has \(\nabla f = 0\). In non-convex landscapes, these come in three flavours, distinguished by the Hessian \(H = \nabla^2 f\):

1. Local Minimum — All Hessian Eigenvalues Positive

All second derivatives are positive: the surface curves upward in every direction. Our toy function \(f(x,y) = x^4 - 2x^2 + 0.5y^2\) has minima at \((\pm 1, 0)\) with \(f = -1\). The Hessian there is \(\begin{bmatrix}8 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\) with eigenvalues \(8, 1 > 0\). Gradient descent converges to these.

2. Saddle Point — Mixed Hessian Eigenvalues (the Real Problem)

At the origin: gradient \(= [0,0]\) but the Hessian is \(\begin{bmatrix}-4 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{bmatrix}\) with eigenvalues \(\lambda_1 = -4 < 0\) (negative curvature along \(x\)) and \(\lambda_2 = 1 > 0\) (positive along \(y\)). This is a saddle — it looks like a min if you approach along \(y\), but is a max along \(x\). The Morse index (number of negative eigenvalues) determines how "saddly" it is.

$$\text{Morse index} = |\{i : \lambda_i(H) < 0\}| \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{local min iff index} = 0$$

3. Why Saddles Dominate in High Dimensions

In \(d\)-dimensional space, a random critical point near the global minimum has roughly equal probability of each eigenvalue being positive or negative. The probability of all eigenvalues being positive (a true local min) is \(\approx 2^{-d}\), exponentially small. The vast majority of critical points with loss above the global minimum are saddles. Key results:

  • Dauphin et al. (2014): Error at critical points grows monotonically with the Morse index. Low-error critical points are near-global minima.
  • Choromanska et al. (2015): Under spin-glass model assumptions, local minima cluster near the global minimum as network size grows.
  • Du et al. (2017): Strict saddle property — gradient descent with noise escapes strict saddles in polynomial time.
⚠️ Plateau vs. Saddle

Flat plateaus (gradient \(\approx 0\), Hessian \(\approx 0\)) are as dangerous as saddles — gradient is tiny so descent is slow. These appear early in training (vanishing gradients near initialisation) and in networks with dying ReLUs. BatchNorm and careful initialisation help navigate them.

Why Deep Nets Still Train: Three Saving Graces
▼

Despite non-convexity, deep networks train successfully in practice. Three interlocking reasons explain this:

Grace 1: Saddles Dominate, Not Poor Local Minima

At low loss levels, the Hessian at critical points has almost all positive eigenvalues — because to be stuck at a poor local minimum (high loss, all positive eigenvalues), the training loss would need to be simultaneously high and the curvature positive everywhere. Empirically, networks that get "stuck" are stuck at saddles or plateaus, not at genuinely bad local minima with loss far above the global.

Grace 2: SGD Noise Navigates Saddles

Gradient descent on the full dataset converges to saddles (it has no mechanism to escape them). But stochastic gradient descent computes noisy mini-batch gradients:

$$\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta_t; \mathcal{B}_t)$$

where \(\mathcal{B}_t\) is a random mini-batch. The noise term \(\nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta; \mathcal{B}_t) - \nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta)\) acts like thermal noise that kicks the optimizer off saddle points. Formally (Jin et al., 2017): under mild conditions, SGD with constant noise escapes strict saddles in \(\tilde{O}(1/\epsilon^4)\) steps — polynomial, not exponential.

Grace 3: Over-Parameterization Creates Equivalent Minima

Modern networks have vastly more parameters \(N\) than training samples \(M\) (e.g., GPT-4: \(N \sim 10^{12}\), \(M \sim 10^{12}\) — approximately matched, but layer-level overparameterization is extreme). When \(N \gg M\), the manifold of zero-training-loss solutions has dimension \(\approx N - M\). All points on this manifold have the same training loss, and they are connected. SGD finds a point on this manifold quickly:

$$\dim(\{\theta : \mathcal{L}(\theta) = 0\}) \approx N - M$$

Crucially, the point SGD finds depends on the learning rate and batch size. SGD with larger noise (larger learning rate or smaller batch) finds flatter minima — broader basins that generalize better (the "flat minima generalization" hypothesis of Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997; Keskar et al., 2017).

📐 The Loss Curvature — Sharpness

Flatness is measured by the largest Hessian eigenvalue \(\lambda_{\max}(H)\). Flatter minima have smaller \(\lambda_{\max}\), meaning the loss doesn't rise steeply as you move away. SAM (Sharpness-Aware Minimization, Foret et al. 2021) explicitly seeks flat minima: \(\min_\theta \max_{\|\epsilon\| \le \rho} \mathcal{L}(\theta + \epsilon)\), achieving better generalisation on benchmark tasks.

Key Takeaways
▼
  • Non-convex loss is the norm in deep learning — any composition of non-linear layers produces a non-convex objective.
  • The main obstacle is saddles and plateaus, not poor local minima. Low-loss critical points are almost all near-global.
  • SGD escapes saddles via mini-batch gradient noise — full-batch gradient descent gets stuck; SGD does not.
  • Over-parameterization (\(N \gg M\)) creates a high-dimensional solution manifold; SGD finds it efficiently.
  • Flat minima generalize better — the specific minimum SGD reaches depends on batch size and learning rate, with larger noise favouring broader basins.
💡 Practitioner Rule of Thumb

If your loss plateaus during training: not a local minimum — likely a saddle or plateau. Fix: reduce batch size (more SGD noise), use learning rate warmup, add gradient noise, or switch to Adam (which uses second-moment estimates to navigate flat regions). Learning rate schedules (cosine, cyclical) also help escape plateaus.
