Vanishing & Exploding Gradients

Why gradients shrink to zero or blow up as they flow backward through deep networks — and what breaks training when they do.

Intermediate Deep Learning · Optimization O(L) chain rule
1
Understanding the Concept
▾

What is this? When you train a deep network, the error signal has to travel backward from the output layer to the very first layer through backpropagation. At every layer that signal gets multiplied by a local factor (a weight times an activation derivative). Multiply a number slightly less than 1 by itself twenty times and it shrinks toward zero — that's a vanishing gradient. Multiply a number slightly greater than 1 twenty times and it explodes — that's an exploding gradient.

Analogy: Picture a game of "telephone" played through twenty rooms in a row, where each room either whispers the message a little quieter or shouts it a little louder before passing it on. By room twenty the message has either faded to silence or become a deafening, garbled roar. Early layers of a deep network are the last to hear the "correction" message from the loss function — and by the time it arrives, it may be inaudible or deafening.

Summary: Repeated multiplication of per-layer Jacobian factors during backprop causes gradient magnitude to decay or grow exponentially with network depth, stalling or destabilizing training of early layers.

If you remember one thing

The gradient at layer \(l\) is (roughly) the product of \(L-l\) per-layer factors \(w \cdot \sigma'(z)\); products of many numbers < 1 vanish, products of many numbers > 1 explode — it's exponential decay/growth in disguise.

2
How It Works Internally
▾

During backpropagation, the gradient of the loss \(\mathcal{L}\) with respect to an early hidden state \(h_l\) is obtained by the chain rule, walking backward through every intervening layer:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial h_l} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial h_L}\prod_{k=l+1}^{L} \frac{\partial h_k}{\partial h_{k-1}}, \qquad \frac{\partial h_k}{\partial h_{k-1}} = W_k^\top \operatorname{diag}\big(\sigma'(z_k)\big)$$

Each factor \(\frac{\partial h_k}{\partial h_{k-1}}\) is a Jacobian whose "size" is roughly \(\|W_k\| \cdot \max\sigma'(z_k)\). Sigmoid's derivative peaks at 0.25, tanh's at 1.0, and ReLU's is either exactly 0 or exactly 1. If typical weight magnitudes times this derivative bound are consistently below 1, the product shrinks geometrically with depth (vanishing). If they're consistently above 1, it grows geometrically (exploding). Recurrent networks are hit hardest because the same weight matrix is reused at every timestep, so its dominant eigenvalue directly controls whether \(\lambda^T\) vanishes or explodes as sequence length \(T\) grows.

Design decisions: This is exactly why saturating activations (sigmoid/tanh) were replaced by ReLU/GELU in deep feedforward nets — ReLU's derivative is 1 on the active region, removing one source of shrinkage. It's also why residual connections (\(h_k = h_{k-1} + F(h_{k-1})\)) add an identity path whose Jacobian is exactly \(I\), giving gradients a "highway" that bypasses multiplicative decay entirely, and why LSTMs use an additive cell-state update gated near 1 instead of a plain multiplicative recurrence.

3
Why It Works (The Math Guarantee — and When It Fails)
▾

Model each layer's contribution to the backward pass as a single scalar factor \(g\) (an idealization of \(\|W_k\|\cdot\sigma'(z_k)\)). After \(L\) layers the gradient reaching layer 1 scales like:

$$\left\| \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial h_1} \right\| \;\approx\; \left\| \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial h_L} \right\| \cdot g^{\,L-1}$$

This is a geometric sequence. Take logs: \(\log(\text{gradient}) \approx \log(\|\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial h_L\|) + (L-1)\log g\). If \(g < 1\), \(\log g < 0\) and the gradient decays linearly in log-space, i.e. exponentially in real space, as depth grows — layer 1's update becomes numerically indistinguishable from zero and effectively stops learning. If \(g > 1\), the gradient grows without bound, producing huge, unstable weight updates (or literal NaNs) that wreck training. Only \(g \approx 1\) keeps the signal roughly constant across depth — this is precisely the target that Xavier/He initialization and normalization layers are engineered to hit.

Common Misconception

"Vanishing gradients mean the gradient is exactly zero." In practice it's rarely exactly zero — it becomes so small (e.g. \(10^{-12}\)) that, combined with floating-point precision and the learning rate, the resulting weight update rounds to a no-op. The layer isn't mathematically frozen; it's practically frozen.

Key Takeaway

Every one of these techniques attacks the same root cause: keeping the per-layer backward factor \(g\) close to 1 so the product \(g^{L-1}\) neither collapses nor blows up, however deep the network gets.
