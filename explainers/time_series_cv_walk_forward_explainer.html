Walk-Forward Validation

Time-series cross-validation done right — never let a model train on the future.

Intermediate Time Series Model Evaluation
1
Understanding the Concept
▾

Walk-forward validation (a.k.a. rolling-origin evaluation) is how you correctly cross-validate a forecasting model. Instead of randomly shuffling rows into folds like ordinary k-fold CV, you keep the calendar intact: train on a block of the past, test on the block that comes immediately after it, then slide the "origin" forward in time and repeat.

Think of grading a weather forecaster by replaying history day by day — on March 1 they only know data through February 28, they forecast March, and only then do they get to see what actually happened. Letting them see April's actual temperatures while forecasting March would be cheating — yet that's exactly what shuffled k-fold CV does to a time series.

If you remember one thing

Every training point in a fold must occur strictly before every test point in that fold. That single ordering invariant is the entire difference between valid and invalid time-series CV.

2
How It Works Internally
▾

Given a series of length T, an initial training size n₀, a forecast horizon h, and a step size s, the split generator does this on repeat:

1) Train on everything up to the current origin t — either all history (expanding window) or just the last w points (sliding window). 2) Skip an optional gap of g points to "purge" any feature/label that peeks across the boundary (e.g. a 7-day moving average computed near the origin). 3) Test on the next h points. 4) Advance the origin by s and repeat until you run out of series.

Two window strategies trade off differently: an expanding window keeps growing and never throws away history — great when the process is stable. A sliding window keeps a fixed lookback and drops old data — better when the series drifts and stale data actively hurts predictions. A gap matters whenever engineered features or labels are computed over a window that could straddle the train/test boundary, silently leaking test-period information into the last few training rows.

Design decision

Why not just leave one contiguous block out and rotate through blocks in any order (blocked CV)? Because some rotations would still train on data that comes after the test block — walk-forward always tests on the genuine future relative to its own training set, mirroring exactly how the model will be used in production.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▾

Fold k has origin \(t_k = n_0 + (k-1)s\). Training and test sets are:

$$\text{Train}_k = \begin{cases} \{1,\dots,t_k\} & \text{expanding} \\ \{t_k-w+1,\dots,t_k\} & \text{sliding} \end{cases} \qquad \text{Test}_k = \{t_k+g+1,\dots,t_k+g+h\}$$

The number of folds that fit in a series of length \(T\) is:

$$K = \left\lfloor \frac{T - n_0 - g - h}{s} \right\rfloor + 1$$

Each fold's model \(\hat f_k\) is fit only on \(\text{Train}_k\) and scored on \(\text{Test}_k\). The overall CV score averages across folds:

$$\text{CV}(\hat f) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{1}{h}\sum_{t \in \text{Test}_k} L(y_t, \hat y_t)$$

Because consecutive folds reuse most of the same training data, fold errors \(E_k\) are positively correlated — unlike ordinary i.i.d. k-fold CV. That means the naive variance estimate \(\operatorname{Var}(E_k)/K\) understates the true uncertainty of the CV score.

Why random shuffling leaks

If the series is autocorrelated, \(\rho(y_t, y_{t+1}) \neq 0\), and a feature uses lag \(y_{t-1}\), then random k-fold puts \(t\) in test and \(t{-}1\) in train with high probability. The model effectively "sees" a value adjacent to the test point during training, biasing the CV error optimistically. Walk-forward removes this by construction — train indices are always strictly less than test indices.

Key Takeaways
▾
  • Never shuffle a time series for cross-validation — always split so training ends before testing begins.
  • Default to an expanding window for stable/seasonal series; switch to a sliding window when you observe concept drift.
  • Add a gap whenever features or labels are computed from a window that could straddle the train/test boundary (rolling averages, forward-looking returns).
  • Fold errors are correlated, not i.i.d. — don't over-trust tiny differences in mean CV score between two models.
  • In scikit-learn, always pass cv=TimeSeriesSplit(...) — the default cv=5 in GridSearchCV silently reverts to leaky random k-fold.

Code: The Split Generator
▾

The generator below is exactly what drives the visualization above — it produces the same train/test index arrays for every fold, in both expanding and sliding window modes:

def walk_forward_splits(n, n0, h, s=None, gap=0, window="expanding", w=None): s = s or h t = n0 while t + gap + h <= n: if window == "expanding": train_idx = range(0, t) else: train_idx = range(max(0, t - w), t) test_idx = range(t + gap, t + gap + h) yield train_idx, test_idx t += s
Gotcha

Fit any scaler / encoder / imputer only on each fold's train_idx slice — fitting on the full series before splitting leaks future statistics into every fold.
