Cramér-Rao Lower Bound & Fisher Information

The fundamental limit on estimation precision — and how curvature of the log-likelihood determines how much information data carries about a parameter.
Advanced Probability & Statistics Statistical Inference Must Know
1
Understanding the Concept
▼

Imagine you're trying to measure the weight of an object using a scale, but the scale has random noise. How accurately can any measurement procedure ever determine the true weight? The Cramér-Rao Lower Bound (CRLB) answers this question with mathematical precision: it sets a hard floor on the variance of any unbiased estimator.

The key player is Fisher information \(I(\theta)\) — a number that quantifies how much information a random variable \(X\) carries about the unknown parameter \(\theta\). Intuitively, it measures the sharpness of the log-likelihood: a peaked log-likelihood means the data is informative (high \(I(\theta)\)), while a flat one means the data is nearly useless (low \(I(\theta)\)).

Core Insight

Fisher information = curvature of the log-likelihood. The more curved (peaked) the log-likelihood at the true \(\theta\), the more information the data carries, and the lower the achievable estimation variance. Sharp peak = high info = tight bound.

The CRLB then says: for any unbiased estimator \(\hat\theta\) of \(\theta\) from \(n\) i.i.d. observations,

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta) \geq \frac{1}{n\,I(\theta)}$$

Estimators that achieve this bound are called efficient. Under mild regularity conditions, the MLE is asymptotically efficient — it achieves the CRLB as \(n \to \infty\).

If You Remember One Thing

Fisher information \(I(\theta)\) is the negative expected second derivative (curvature) of the log-likelihood. The CRLB is its reciprocal scaled by \(n\). More curvature = more information = tighter bound on variance.

2
How It Works — The Math
▼

Fisher Information — Three Equivalent Definitions

For a model \(p(x;\theta)\) with log-likelihood \(\ell(\theta; x) = \log p(x;\theta)\), the Fisher information is defined as:

$$I(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_\theta\!\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log p(X;\theta)\right)^{\!2}\right]$$

This is the variance of the score function \(s(\theta;X) = \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log p(X;\theta)\). Three equivalent forms (under regularity):

Three Faces of Fisher Information

1. Variance of the score: \(\displaystyle I(\theta) = \operatorname{Var}_\theta\!\left[\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta}\right]\)

2. Negative expected curvature: \(\displaystyle I(\theta) = -\,\mathbb{E}_\theta\!\left[\frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial \theta^2}\right]\)

3. KL divergence rate: \(\displaystyle I(\theta) = \lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{2\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(\cdot;\theta)\,\|\,p(\cdot;\theta+\epsilon))}{\epsilon^2}\)

Cramér-Rao Bound — Proof Sketch

Let \(\hat\theta(X_1,\ldots,X_n)\) be an unbiased estimator: \(\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta] = \theta\). Differentiate both sides w.r.t. \(\theta\):

$$\int \hat\theta(x)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}p(x;\theta)\,dx = 1$$

Rewrite using \(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log p = \frac{(\partial/\partial\theta)\,p}{p}\), so the left side becomes \(\mathbb{E}[\hat\theta \cdot s(\theta;X)] = 1\). Apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

$$1 = \bigl(\operatorname{Cov}(\hat\theta,\, s)\bigr)^2 \leq \operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)\cdot\operatorname{Var}(s) = \operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta)\cdot I(\theta)$$

Rearranging yields the bound for one observation. For \(n\) i.i.d. observations, the score sums and variance multiplies, giving:

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta) \geq \frac{1}{n\,I(\theta)}$$

Numerical Example — Normal Distribution

Let \(X_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, 1)\) with \(\sigma^2=1\) known, estimating \(\mu\).

Log-likelihood for one obs: \(\ell(\mu;x) = -\tfrac{1}{2}\log(2\pi) - \tfrac{1}{2}(x-\mu)^2\)

Score: \(\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\mu} = x - \mu\). Curvature: \(\frac{\partial^2\ell}{\partial\mu^2} = -1\).

Fisher info: \(I(\mu) = -\mathbb{E}[-1] = 1 = 1/\sigma^2\).

CRLB for \(n=10\): \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat\mu) \geq 1/(10 \times 1) = 0.10\).

The sample mean \(\bar X\) has \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X) = 1/10 = 0.10\). It achieves the bound. It is the UMVUE (uniformly minimum variance unbiased estimator).

Common Pitfall

The CRLB is a lower bound for unbiased estimators only. A biased estimator can have lower variance — but bias is a cost too. The full trade-off is captured by the MSE: \(\mathrm{MSE}(\hat\theta) = \operatorname{Var}(\hat\theta) + \mathrm{Bias}^2\).

Connections — Natural Gradient & Laplace Approximation
▼

Connection 1 — Natural Gradient Descent

In standard gradient descent, the parameter update is \(\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}\). But the Euclidean metric on parameter space is arbitrary — the natural metric for a statistical model is the Fisher information matrix:

$$F_{ij}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\partial \log p}{\partial\theta_i}\frac{\partial \log p}{\partial\theta_j}\right]$$

The natural gradient replaces the standard gradient with the Fisher-preconditioned version:

$$\tilde\nabla\mathcal{L} = F(\theta)^{-1}\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}$$

This makes the step invariant to reparametrisation of the model. Intuitively: if the Fisher information is high in some direction (the log-likelihood is sharply curved there), the natural gradient takes smaller steps — appropriately, because we already have precise knowledge in that direction. Natural gradient underlies KFAC, K-FAC approximate second-order methods, and the policy gradient algorithm TRPO/PPO.

Connection 2 — Laplace Approximation

The Laplace approximation approximates a posterior \(p(\theta|X)\) by a Gaussian centred at the MAP estimate \(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}}\):

$$p(\theta\mid X) \approx \mathcal{N}\!\left(\hat\theta_{\mathrm{MAP}},\; \bigl[-\nabla^2_\theta\log p(\theta\mid X)\bigr]^{-1}\right)$$

The curvature of the log-posterior at the MAP is precisely the observed Fisher information (plus the prior's Hessian). A steep log-posterior \(\Rightarrow\) tighter Gaussian posterior \(\Rightarrow\) more confident inference. This is the Bayesian dual of the CRLB: the posterior covariance (uncertainty) at convergence is bounded below by the inverse Fisher information.

Unified Picture

Fisher information sits at the intersection of classical statistics (CRLB), information geometry (natural gradient), and Bayesian inference (Laplace approximation). It measures curvature of the log-likelihood — the higher the curvature, the more information, the tighter the bound, the smaller the natural gradient step, and the sharper the Laplace posterior.

Code — From Scratch Implementation
▼

From-scratch Python implementations of Fisher information and CRLB verification.

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
def fisher_info_normal(sigma2: float) -> float:
"""Fisher information for Normal(mu, sigma^2), estimating mu."""
# score: d/dmu log f = (x-mu)/sigma^2
# Var(score) = Var(X)/sigma^4 = sigma^2/sigma^4 = 1/sigma^2
return 1.0 / sigma2
def fisher_info_bernoulli(p: float) -> float:
"""Fisher information for Bernoulli(p), estimating p."""
# score: d/dp log f = x/p - (1-x)/(1-p)
# E[score^2] = 1/(p*(1-p))
return 1.0 / (p * (1.0 - p))
def crlb(fisher_info: float, n: int) -> float:
"""Cramér-Rao lower bound for n i.i.d. observations."""
return 1.0 / (n * fisher_info)
def verify_mle_efficiency(n=1000, reps=50000):
"""Check that sample mean achieves CRLB for Normal(mu=1, sigma^2=1)."""
sigma2, mu = 1.0, 1.0
# Generate reps datasets of size n
data = np.random.normal(mu, np.sqrt(sigma2), size=(reps, n))
# MLE = sample mean
mle_estimates = data.mean(axis=1)
empirical_var = np.var(mle_estimates) # should ≈ 1/n
bound = crlb(fisher_info_normal(sigma2), n) # = 1/n
eff = bound / empirical_var # efficiency ≈ 1.0 for MLE
print(f"Empirical Var = {empirical_var:.5f}")
print(f"CRLB = {bound:.5f}")
print(f"Efficiency = {eff:.4f} (1.0 = efficient)")
return eff
# Example output (n=1000, reps=50000):
# Empirical Var = 0.00100 CRLB = 0.00100 Efficiency = 0.9997

Key Takeaways & When to Use
▼

When to use CRLB: Benchmarking estimators — compare any unbiased estimator's variance against the bound to measure efficiency. If your estimator's variance equals the CRLB, it's optimal among all unbiased estimators.

  • MLE is asymptotically efficient — it achieves the CRLB as \(n\to\infty\) under regularity.
  • Exponential family — sufficient statistics always achieve the CRLB (they are UMVUE by Lehmann-Scheffé).
  • Signal processing — radar, GPS, and sensor fusion use CRLB to benchmark location estimators.
  • Experiment design — maximising \(I(\theta)\) (D-optimal design) minimises the CRLB.
  • Neural networks — natural gradient (Fisher preconditioner) converges faster than SGD on curved loss surfaces.
Limitations

The CRLB requires the regularity conditions: the support must not depend on \(\theta\), and differentiation through the integral must be valid. For the Uniform(\(0,\theta\)) distribution, the MLE \(\hat\theta = \max X_i\) has \(\operatorname{Var} \propto 1/n^2\) — faster than \(1/n\) — because the regularity conditions fail. The CRLB does not apply here.
