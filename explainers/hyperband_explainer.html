Hyperband & Successive Halving

Early-stopping bandit-based hyperparameter optimisation — eliminate bad configs fast, spend budget on winners

Advanced Classical ML · HPO O(n log n) configs
1
Understanding the Concept

What is this? When you train a machine-learning model, you have many knobs to turn — learning rate, batch size, number of layers, etc. Trying every combination fully is brutally expensive. Successive Halving (SHA) is a smart shortcut: throw all your candidate configurations into a race, give each only a tiny budget (e.g., 1 epoch), cut the worst half, give survivors twice the budget, repeat. Hyperband runs several SHA races in parallel, each starting with a different trade-off between "how many configs" vs "how much budget per config", and takes the winner across all races.

💡 Analogy — Horse Race with Eliminations

Imagine a talent show. Round 1: all 81 acts perform for 1 minute each. The worst two-thirds are cut. Round 2: 27 acts get 3 minutes each. Round 3: 9 acts get 9 minutes. Final: 3 acts get 27 minutes each. You find the winner using only a fraction of the total time you'd need if everyone performed the full set. That's Successive Halving. Hyperband hedges by also running a parallel track where fewer acts start but each gets more time from the beginning — so you never mis-eliminate a slow-starter.

Key Insight

If you remember one thing: SHA converts a budget allocation problem into a sequence of "keep the top \(\frac{1}{\eta}\) fraction" decisions; Hyperband eliminates the need to choose \(n\) vs \(r_{\min}\) by running all sensible bracket configurations and taking the best result.

How It Works Internally

Successive Halving (SHA)

SHA takes three inputs: a maximum budget \(R\) (e.g., 81 epochs), a reduction factor \(\eta\) (typically 3), and a starting count \(n\). It produces a rung ladder:

$$r_k = R \cdot \eta^{k - s_{\max}}, \quad n_k = \left\lfloor \frac{n}{\eta^k} \right\rfloor \quad k = 0, 1, \ldots, s_{\max}$$

At each rung \(k\): train all surviving configurations for \(r_k\) total resources, evaluate their validation metric, keep the top \(\lfloor n_k / \eta \rfloor\).

Hyperband

SHA has a free parameter: how to split the total budget \(B = n \cdot R\) between breadth (many cheap configs) and depth (few expensive configs). Hyperband eliminates this by running \(s_{\max}+1\) brackets, indexed \(s = s_{\max}, s_{\max}-1, \ldots, 0\):

$$s_{\max} = \lfloor \log_\eta R \rfloor$$

Bracket \(s\) starts with \(n_s = \lceil \frac{s_{\max}+1}{s+1} \cdot \eta^s \rceil\) configs and minimum budget \(r_s = R \cdot \eta^{-s}\). Total budget per bracket is approximately constant \(\approx (s_{\max}+1) \cdot R\).

⚠ Design Decision — Why not just set n large?

Large \(n\) gives more exploration but wastes budget if the validation loss ordering at rung 0 (minimal budget) doesn't correlate with the final ordering. This correlation assumption — called the early-stopping assumption — is strong for well-behaved loss curves but breaks for architectures with long burn-in (e.g., Transformers with warmup). Hyperband hedges: bracket \(s=0\) runs only 1 config for the full budget (pure exploitation), bracket \(s=s_{\max}\) runs the maximum number of configs for the minimum budget (pure exploration).

The Math — Guarantees & Budget

Let \(R\) be the maximum resource per config, \(\eta \geq 2\) the reduction factor, \(s_{\max} = \lfloor \log_\eta R \rfloor\).

Bracket structure for \(R=81, \eta=3\)

$$s_{\max} = \lfloor \log_3 81 \rfloor = 4$$

Five brackets (\(s = 4, 3, 2, 1, 0\)) with budgets:

$$B_s = \sum_{k=0}^{s} n_{s,k} \cdot r_{s,k} \approx (s_{\max}+1) \cdot R = 5 \times 81 = 405 \text{ resource units per Hyperband round}$$

SHA budget cost

$$\text{Total SHA cost} = \sum_{k=0}^{s} n_k \cdot (r_{k+1} - r_k) \leq (s+1) \cdot R$$

Regret bound (Li et al., 2018)

Under the assumption that the best config is in the top fraction \(\frac{1}{n}\) of all sampled configs with high probability, Hyperband achieves expected simple regret:

$$\mathbb{E}[\text{regret}] = \tilde{O}\!\left(\frac{(\log R)^2}{\sqrt{T}}\right)$$

compared to random search's \(\tilde{O}(1/\sqrt{T})\). The \(\log^2 R\) factor pays for not knowing which bracket is best.

Numerical Trace — R=81, η=3, s_max=4

s=4 (max exploration): n=81, r_min=1 → rungs: 81@1, 27@3, 9@9, 3@27, 1@81
s=3: n=34, r_min=3 → rungs: 34@3, 11@9, 3@27, 1@81
s=2: n=15, r_min=9 → rungs: 15@9, 5@27, 1@81
s=1: n=8, r_min=27 → rungs: 8@27, 2@81
s=0 (pure exploit): n=5, r_min=81 → rungs: 5@81

Key Takeaways & When to Use

Use Successive Halving when

  • You have a clear budget proxy (epochs, data subsets, trees) that is monotonically correlated with final performance
  • You can afford to fix \(n\) yourself (you know the exploration/exploitation trade-off)
  • Training curves are smooth — no warm-up phases that delay convergence

Use Hyperband when

  • You are uncertain about how many configs to try vs how much budget to give each
  • You want a theoretically grounded, parameter-free algorithm that covers all bracket configs
  • Combined with Bayesian optimisation → BOHB (replace random sampling with TPE/GP-based proposals)
