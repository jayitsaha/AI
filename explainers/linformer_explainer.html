Linformer

Low-rank approximation of self-attention — from \(O(n^2)\) to \(O(nk)\)
Advanced Efficient Transformers NLP O(n·k) attention
1
Understanding Linformer
▼

What is it? Standard self-attention computes a full \(n \times n\) matrix of pairwise scores between every token and every other token — that's what makes Transformers slow and memory-hungry on long sequences (\(O(n^2)\) time and space). Linformer (Wang et al., 2020) observed that this huge attention matrix is almost always low-rank in practice — most of its "information" lives in just a handful of dominant directions. So instead of computing the full matrix, Linformer projects the keys and values down from length \(n\) to a small fixed length \(k\) using two learned linear maps \(E, F \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times k}\), and computes attention against those \(k\) "summarized" positions instead.

Analogy: Imagine polling every one of 10,000 voters individually about every other voter's opinion (10,000² comparisons) versus first clustering them into 256 representative archetypes and having everyone vote relative to those archetypes instead. You lose a little precision, but the cost drops from quadratic to linear in the number of voters — because opinions cluster; the "true rank" of the opinion matrix is far smaller than 10,000.

💡 If you remember one thing

Linformer replaces the \(n\times n\) attention matrix with a low-rank factorization through a \(k\)-dimensional bottleneck: project \(K,V\) (length \(n\)) down to length \(k\) with \(E,F\), then attend over just those \(k\) positions. Cost drops from \(O(n^2 d)\) to \(O(nkd)\), and for many tasks \(k\) can be a small constant, independent of \(n\).

2
How Linformer Works Internally
▼

Standard self-attention, step by step

Given input \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\), standard self-attention forms \(Q = XW_Q\), \(K = XW_K\), \(V = XW_V\) (all \(n\times d\)), then computes \(A = \operatorname{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d})\), an \(n\times n\) matrix, and output \(\operatorname{Attn} = AV\), an \(n\times d\) matrix. Forming \(A\) costs \(O(n^2 d)\) time and \(O(n^2)\) memory — for \(n=4096\) that's ~16.7M entries per head, per layer.

The Linformer fix: project the length dimension

Linformer adds two learned projection matrices \(E, F \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times k}\) that compress the sequence-length axis of \(K\) and \(V\) (not the feature axis):

$$\bar K = E^\top K \in \mathbb{R}^{k\times d}, \qquad \bar V = F^\top V \in \mathbb{R}^{k\times d}$$

Attention is then computed between the full-length queries and the compressed keys/values:

$$\operatorname{Attn}(Q,\bar K,\bar V) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q\bar K^\top}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\bar V, \qquad Q\bar K^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times k}$$

The intermediate score matrix is now \(n\times k\) instead of \(n\times n\) — computed in \(O(nkd)\) time. Since \(k \ll n\) can be a small constant (the paper uses \(k=128\) or \(256\) even for \(n=4096\)), this is effectively linear in sequence length.

Design decisions

  • Why project K,V and not Q? Queries must stay at full length \(n\) — every output position still needs its own context vector. Only the "pool of things to attend to" (keys/values) is compressed.
  • Why share E,F across heads (optionally layers)? Parameter sharing (headwise, or even layerwise) barely hurts accuracy in the paper's ablations and cuts extra parameter count essentially to zero versus the base Transformer.
  • Why not just truncate to the first k tokens? That would discard information. \(E,F\) are learned linear combinations — every original position contributes to every compressed row, so no token is silently dropped, it's a soft summary.
🔑 Key property

Because \(E,F\) act only on the length axis, \(\bar K = E^\top K\) and \(\bar V = F^\top V\) are literally a low-rank factorization applied before the softmax's nonlinearity — the effective attention output is mathematically equivalent to attending over a rank-\(\le k\) approximation of the interaction structure, at a fraction of the cost.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

The empirical low-rank claim

Linformer's justification starts from an experiment: take a trained Transformer's attention matrices \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) and compute their singular value spectra. Across layers and heads, the spectrum decays sharply — a large fraction of the spectrum's energy sits in the top handful of singular values. By the Eckart–Young theorem, the best rank-\(k\) approximation to \(A\) in Frobenius norm is exactly its truncated SVD:

$$A_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_i u_i v_i^\top = U_k \Sigma_k V_k^\top, \qquad \|A-A_k\|_F = \min_{\operatorname{rank}(B)\le k}\|A-B\|_F$$

If \(\|A-A_k\|_F\) is already small for modest \(k\), then a model that only ever gets to "see" a rank-\(k\) summary of the interaction pattern should lose little compared to full attention — which is exactly what Linformer forces via its \(E,F\) bottleneck.

A theoretical bound (Johnson–Lindenstrauss flavor)

The Linformer paper also gives a distributional argument: if \(P \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\) is a softmax attention row-distribution and \(R\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}\) is a random projection matrix with \(k = O(\log n/\varepsilon^2)\), then with high probability

$$\Pr\big[\, \|PR R^\top V - PV\|_2 \le \varepsilon\,\|PV\|_2 \,\big] \ge 1-o(1)$$

i.e. a random (or learned) length-\(k\) projection preserves the attention output up to bounded error, with \(k\) growing only logarithmically in \(n\) — this is the theoretical backbone for why compressing to a fixed, small \(k\) doesn't blow up error as sequences get longer.

⚠️ Common misconception

"Linformer computes the SVD of the attention matrix at inference time." It does not — computing an SVD would itself cost \(O(n^2)\) or more. \(E\) and \(F\) are ordinary learned linear layers, trained end-to-end by backprop; the SVD argument is only used to justify that such a low-rank bottleneck should work, not as part of the forward pass.

Key Takeaways & Comparisons
▼

Linformer is one of several "efficient attention" strategies that trade the exact \(O(n^2)\) attention matrix for a cheaper approximation. It's simplest when compared directly against the alternatives it's usually cited alongside:

MethodCore ideaComplexityApproximation quality
Full attentionExact \(n\times n\) softmax\(O(n^2 d)\)Exact
LinformerLow-rank projection of \(K,V\) length axis\(O(nkd)\), \(k\) fixedGood if attention is truly low-rank
PerformerKernel/random-feature approx of softmax\(O(nd^2)\)Unbiased estimator, no rank assumption
Longformer / BigBirdSparse local + global attention patterns\(O(n\cdot w)\), window \(w\)Exact within pattern, misses long sparse deps
ReformerLSH bucketing of similar queries/keys\(O(n\log n)\)Approximate nearest-neighbor attention

Use Linformer when: sequence length is large and roughly fixed at inference (projection matrices are sized for a specific \(n\)), and empirically the task's attention patterns are diffuse/global rather than sharply local — encoder-style tasks (classification, retrieval) tend to work well.

Avoid it when: you need variable/streaming sequence lengths (E,F are shaped for a fixed \(n\), though follow-ups handle this), or the task depends on precise sparse long-range dependencies that a low-rank summary would blur (e.g. exact copy/pointer tasks).

🎯 Bottom line

Linformer turns the attention bottleneck from quadratic to linear by exploiting one empirical fact: real attention matrices are approximately low-rank. It needs no architectural surgery beyond two extra linear layers, making it one of the simplest efficient-attention drop-in replacements.
