1
Understanding Random Projection
▼

What is it? Random projection is a technique for reducing the dimensionality of a dataset by multiplying it with a random matrix. Remarkably, this apparently careless operation provably preserves pairwise distances — not exactly, but within a controllable error factor \((1 \pm \varepsilon)\). The key insight is that high-dimensional space is mostly empty, and random directions are nearly orthogonal with high probability.

Analogy: Imagine you have a 3D sculpture, and you want to project its silhouette onto a 2D canvas. If you pick the canvas angle randomly, most silhouettes will capture the sculpture's relative proportions reasonably well — nearby parts of the sculpture appear close in the silhouette, and distant parts appear far. Random projection does this formally, in arbitrarily high dimensions, with a mathematical guarantee on the error.

Why does it matter? Many ML algorithms — k-NN, SVM, clustering, PCA — scale poorly with dimension. Random projection slashes dimensionality from \(d\) to \(k = O(\log n / \varepsilon^2)\) in \(O(n \cdot d \cdot k)\) time, independent of the input dimension structure. No eigenvectors needed. No PCA fitting. Just multiply by a random matrix.

💡 If you remember one thing

The Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma guarantees: for any \(n\) points in \(\mathbb{R}^d\), there exists a map \(f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^k\) with \(k = O(\log n / \varepsilon^2)\) such that for all pairs \(i, j\): $$(1 - \varepsilon)\|x_i - x_j\|^2 \leq \|f(x_i) - f(x_j)\|^2 \leq (1 + \varepsilon)\|x_i - x_j\|^2$$ A random Gaussian matrix achieves this with high probability — no data-dependent construction required.

2
The Johnson-Lindenstrauss Lemma
▼

Statement of the Lemma

Let \(\varepsilon \in (0, 1/2)\) and let \(X = \{x_1, \ldots, x_n\} \subset \mathbb{R}^d\). Set \(k = \lceil 4(\varepsilon^2/2 - \varepsilon^3/3)^{-1} \ln n \rceil\). Then a random linear map \(f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^k\) satisfies the distance preservation inequality for all pairs with probability at least \(1 - 1/n\).

$$k \geq \frac{4 \ln n}{\varepsilon^2 / 2 - \varepsilon^3 / 3} \approx \frac{8 \ln n}{\varepsilon^2} \quad \text{(simplified)}$$

Proof Sketch (Why Random Works)

The proof uses two ingredients: the concentration of measure phenomenon and the union bound.

Step 1 — Single pair, one dimension: If \(r \in \mathbb{R}^d\) is drawn from \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\) and \(u = x_i - x_j\) (a fixed unit vector after normalization), then \(\langle r, u \rangle \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\). The square of this inner product has expectation 1.

Step 2 — Average over k dimensions: The projected squared distance is \(\frac{1}{k}\|Ru\|^2 = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{l=1}^k \langle r_l, u \rangle^2\), a scaled chi-squared with \(k\) degrees of freedom. By the Chernoff bound for chi-squared:

$$\Pr\!\left[\left|\frac{1}{k}\|Ru\|^2 - \|u\|^2\right| > \varepsilon \|u\|^2\right] \leq 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{k(\varepsilon^2/2 - \varepsilon^3/3)}{2}\right)$$

Step 3 — Union bound over all pairs: There are \(\binom{n}{2} < n^2/2\) pairs. Choosing \(k\) large enough so that \(2\exp(-k(\varepsilon^2/2-\varepsilon^3/3)/2) < 1/n^2\), the union bound gives failure probability \(< 1/2\). With \(k = O(\log n / \varepsilon^2)\), all pairs are preserved simultaneously.

🔑 Key Property

The target dimension \(k = O(\log n / \varepsilon^2)\) depends only on the number of points \(n\) and the desired distortion \(\varepsilon\) — not on the original dimension \(d\). This means random projection is essentially free when \(d \gg \log n\).

Numerical Example

For \(n = 10{,}000\) points, \(\varepsilon = 0.3\): \(k \approx 8 \ln(10000) / 0.09 \approx 8 \times 9.21 / 0.09 \approx 819\) dimensions. If original \(d = 100{,}000\), this is a 122× compression with guaranteed \(\leq 30\%\) distance distortion on every pair.

3
Gaussian vs Sparse (Achlioptas) Projections
▼

Gaussian Random Projection

The standard construction: draw each entry of \(R \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times d}\) i.i.d. from \(\mathcal{N}(0, 1/k)\). The projected point is \(f(x) = Rx\). This matrix satisfies JL with the optimal \(k\).

$$R_{ij} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0, \frac{1}{k}\right), \quad f(x) = Rx \in \mathbb{R}^k$$

Cost: Generating and applying \(R\) takes \(O(dk)\) per point — feasible but memory-intensive for very large \(d\). Storage: \(O(dk)\) floats.

Sparse Random Projection (Achlioptas, 2003)

Achlioptas showed that sparse matrices work just as well. Each entry is drawn from a three-outcome distribution:

$$R_{ij} = \sqrt{3} \times \begin{cases} +1 & \text{with prob } 1/6 \\ 0 & \text{with prob } 2/3 \\ -1 & \text{with prob } 1/6 \end{cases}$$

Two-thirds of entries are zero, replaced by fast integer arithmetic. This gives a 3× speedup with the same JL guarantee. Even sparser variants (e.g., \(\pm\sqrt{d/k}\) with prob \(1/(2\sqrt{d/k})\)) can push sparsity further for very high-dimensional data.

⚠️ Common Misconception

The projection matrix \(R\) does NOT need to have orthonormal columns to satisfy JL. Orthogonal matrices preserve distances exactly, but JL only asks for approximate preservation — a weaker requirement that random matrices achieve automatically. You do NOT need to QR-decompose \(R\).

Comparison: Gaussian vs Sparse vs PCA

All three reduce dimension, but with very different trade-offs:

Key Takeaways & When to Use
▼

Use random projection when: Your data has very high dimension (\(d \gg \log n\)), you need preprocessing speed, or you want a data-oblivious method that requires no fitting. Excellent as a pre-step before k-NN, SVM, or LSH (locality-sensitive hashing).

Don't use when: You need interpretable features (projections are random linear combinations), the task requires capturing top-variance structure (use PCA), or \(n\) is very small (log n is negligible and the JL dimension may exceed d).

💡 The Free Lunch

For \(n\) fixed, as \(d \to \infty\), the JL target dimension \(k = O(\log n / \varepsilon^2)\) stays constant. Random projection achieves effectively unlimited dimensionality reduction with a fixed distortion budget — the compression ratio \(d/k \to \infty\) as \(d\) grows. That's why it's described as "near-free" dimensionality reduction.
