What Are Regression Diagnostic Plots?
▼

The central question: You fit a linear regression \(\hat{y} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 x\). But is the model actually valid? OLS rests on four Gauss-Markov assumptions about the error term \(\varepsilon\): linearity, homoscedasticity (constant variance), independence, and normality. Each diagnostic plot tests one of these, and together they form the standard four-panel diagnostic display (the same four plots produced by R's plot(lm(...))).

The residual \(e_i\): The raw residual is \(e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i\). But for diagnostics, we prefer the standardized residual (also called internally studentized residual):

$$r_i = \frac{e_i}{\hat{\sigma}\sqrt{1 - h_{ii}}}$$

where \(h_{ii}\) is the \(i\)-th diagonal of the hat matrix \(H = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\), and \(\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{\sum e_i^2}{n - p}\). Dividing by \(\sqrt{1-h_{ii}}\) corrects for the fact that high-leverage points have smaller variance.

💡 If you remember one thing

The four diagnostic plots are a complete visual test suite for OLS assumptions. Residual vs Fitted → linearity; Q-Q → normality; Scale-Location → homoscedasticity; Residuals vs Leverage with Cook's D → influential points. A healthy model shows random scatter in panels 1 and 3, straight-line Q-Q, and no points past the Cook's \(D = 1\) contour.

2
The Four Standard Diagnostic Plots
▼

Plot 1 — Residuals vs Fitted

The x-axis shows the fitted value \(\hat{y}_i\) and the y-axis shows the raw residual \(e_i = y_i - \hat{y}_i\). Healthy: points scatter randomly around the horizontal zero line with no systematic pattern. Problematic: a U-shaped or sinusoidal pattern indicates a nonlinear relationship that the model is missing (the functional form is wrong). A megaphone shape (spread increasing with fitted value) indicates heteroscedasticity.

Plot 2 — Normal Q-Q

The standardized residuals are sorted and plotted against their expected normal quantiles. Recall that for a standard normal, the expected \(j\)-th order statistic out of \(n\) is approximately \(\Phi^{-1}\!\left(\frac{j - 0.5}{n}\right)\) (Hazen formula). Healthy: points fall on or near the 45° reference line \(y = x\). Problematic: S-shaped → heavy tails; concave → right skew; high outlier → one far-right point breaks away.

$$\text{Q-Q quantile formula: }\quad q_j = \Phi^{-1}\!\left(\frac{j - 0.5}{n}\right), \quad j = 1, \ldots, n$$

Plot 3 — Scale-Location (Spread-Level)

Plots \(\sqrt{|r_i|}\) (square root of absolute standardized residuals) on the y-axis against \(\hat{y}_i\). This amplifies visual differences in spread. Healthy: a roughly flat loess/smooth line. Problematic: an upward trend → variance grows with the fitted value (heteroscedasticity). This is the go-to plot for the Breusch-Pagan test's visual analogue.

Plot 4 — Residuals vs Leverage (Cook's Distance)

Two quantities are shown together: leverage \(h_{ii}\) on the x-axis, standardized residual on the y-axis, with Cook's distance \(D_i\) shown as contour curves:

$$D_i = \frac{e_i^2}{p\,\hat{\sigma}^2} \cdot \frac{h_{ii}}{(1-h_{ii})^2}$$

where \(p\) is the number of parameters. A point can influence the regression either because it has a large residual (high in the y-direction) or because it is far from the mean of \(x\) (high leverage, far right). Only points that have both have high Cook's \(D\). The conventional threshold \(D_i > 1\) (or more conservatively \(4/n\)) flags an influential point.

⚠ Leverage ≠ Influence

A point can have high leverage but land perfectly on the regression line (residual ≈ 0), exerting no influence on \(\hat{\beta}\). Conversely, a large outlier near the mean of \(x\) has low leverage and inflates \(\hat{\sigma}^2\) but doesn't pull the slope much. Cook's \(D\) is the product of both concerns.

3
Mathematical Deep-Dive
▼

The Hat Matrix

Define the hat matrix \(H = X(X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top\). It is idempotent (\(H^2 = H\)) and symmetric. Predictions satisfy \(\hat{y} = Hy\), and residuals satisfy \(e = (I - H)y\). The diagonal entry \(h_{ii} = [H]_{ii}\) is the leverage of observation \(i\). Bounds: \(\frac{1}{n} \leq h_{ii} \leq 1\). The average leverage is \(\bar{h} = p/n\).

$$h_{ii} = x_i^\top (X^\top X)^{-1} x_i$$

Variance of Residuals

Since \(e = (I - H)y = (I-H)\varepsilon\), we get \(\operatorname{Var}(e_i) = \sigma^2(1 - h_{ii})\). Points with higher leverage have smaller residual variance — which is why we must divide by \(\sqrt{1-h_{ii}}\) to standardize.

Cook's Distance as Parameter-Space Shift

Cook's distance measures how much the coefficient vector \(\hat{\beta}\) would change if observation \(i\) were deleted. Letting \(\hat{\beta}_{(i)}\) denote the estimate without point \(i\):

$$D_i = \frac{(\hat{\beta} - \hat{\beta}_{(i)})^\top (X^\top X)(\hat{\beta} - \hat{\beta}_{(i)})}{p\,\hat{\sigma}^2} = \frac{e_i^2}{p\,\hat{\sigma}^2} \cdot \frac{h_{ii}}{(1 - h_{ii})^2}$$

The second form is computable without actually refitting. The equivalence follows from the Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury update formula applied to \((X^\top X)\) after removing one row.

Leave-One-Out Residual (LOOCV Residual)

The deleted residual \(e_{(i)} = y_i - x_i^\top \hat{\beta}_{(i)}\) has the elegant closed form:

$$e_{(i)} = \frac{e_i}{1 - h_{ii}}$$

This means we never have to actually refit the model \(n\) times — all LOO residuals are available from a single fit. The externally studentized residual divides this by \(\hat{\sigma}_{(i)}\) (residual std. dev. without point \(i\)), giving a \(t_{n-p-1}\) distribution under the null.

∑ Math checkpoint

For our dataset: \(n = 21\), \(p = 2\) (\(\hat{\beta}_0, \hat{\beta}_1\)). Influential outlier at \(x=15\): \(e_{20} = 17.73\), \(h_{20} = 0.396\), \(\hat{\sigma} \approx 3.97\). Then \(D_{20} = 17.73^2 / (2 \times 15.75) \times 0.396/(0.604)^2 = 314.4 / 31.5 \times 1.086 = 5.74\). ✓

🔑 Diagnostic Order of Operations

Always check Panel 4 first. A single high-influence point can distort all other plots and lead you to wrong conclusions. Remove or downweight it, refit, then diagnose again. Panels 1 and 3 share the x-axis (fitted values); panels 2 and 4 both use the standardized residual — so they all link back to the same quantities.

Interview Q&A + Common Traps
▼

Q: What does a banana-shaped residual plot tell you?

A U-curve (banana) in Residuals vs Fitted means the relationship is nonlinear — the model underestimates at the tails and overestimates in the middle. The remedy is a quadratic term or a nonlinear transformation, not a bigger dataset.

Q: Can you have heteroscedasticity without seeing it in Plot 1?

Yes. Plot 1 shows the signed residual and variance differences can cancel visually. Plot 3 (Scale-Location) uses \(\sqrt{|r_i|}\) which removes the sign and makes the variance trend visible even when the signed plot looks symmetric.

Q: A point has high leverage but small Cook's D. What does that mean?

It is a "good leverage point": it sits far from the centroid of \(x\) but falls close to the regression surface. These points actually improve precision of the slope estimate (they reduce \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat{\beta}_1)\)) and should generally not be deleted.

Q: What distribution does the standardized residual follow?

The internally studentized residual \(r_i = e_i / (\hat{\sigma}\sqrt{1-h_{ii}})\) does NOT follow a \(t\) distribution exactly (because \(\hat{\sigma}\) is estimated using data that includes point \(i\)). The externally studentized (deleted) residual \(r^*_i = e_{(i)} / \hat{\sigma}_{(i)}\) follows a \(t_{n-p-1}\) distribution exactly, making it the correct basis for formal outlier tests (Bonferroni-corrected).

⚡ Common Trap

Do not apply diagnostic plots to the raw \(y\) values or training loss — only to the residuals after fitting. And for generalized linear models (Poisson, logistic), use deviance residuals or Pearson residuals instead of raw residuals — the OLS diagnostics do not directly apply.
