Semantic Textual Similarity (STS)

Measuring how similar two pieces of text are using Sentence-BERT embeddings
Foundational NLP Deep Learning Intermediate
1
Understanding STS & Sentence-BERT
▼

What is Semantic Textual Similarity (STS)? STS is the task of measuring the degree to which two pieces of text (words, sentences, paragraphs) are semantically equivalent. Unlike simple keyword matching, STS aims to understand the underlying meaning. For example, "The cat sat on the mat" and "A feline was resting on the rug" are semantically very similar, despite having few words in common.

Why is it hard for computers? Human language is nuanced. Synonyms, paraphrases, negation, and different grammatical structures can all convey the same or very similar meanings. Traditional methods like TF-IDF or simple word embeddings struggle to capture sentence-level semantics effectively, often treating sentences as mere "bags of words."

The problem with vanilla BERT: Large language models like BERT are excellent at understanding context. However, directly using BERT's `[CLS]` token output (often considered a sentence representation) or averaging its word embeddings typically produces suboptimal sentence embeddings for similarity tasks. This is because BERT is pre-trained for tasks like masked language modeling and next sentence prediction, not for generating semantically meaningful sentence vectors that are directly comparable via cosine similarity.

Introducing Sentence-BERT (SBERT): SBERT is a modification of the pre-trained BERT network that uses siamese and triplet network structures to fine-tune BERT, producing semantically meaningful sentence embeddings. This fine-tuning ensures that sentences with similar meanings are mapped to nearby points in the vector space, while dissimilar sentences are mapped far apart. This makes SBERT highly effective for STS tasks.

💡 If you remember one thing

SBERT fine-tunes BERT using a siamese or triplet network architecture to generate dense sentence embeddings. These embeddings are then directly comparable using cosine similarity to determine semantic relatedness, making it vastly more efficient and accurate for STS than vanilla BERT.

2
How Sentence-BERT Works Internally
▼

The Siamese/Triplet Network Architecture

Instead of processing one sentence at a time, SBERT uses a siamese (two identical BERT networks) or triplet (three identical BERT networks) architecture. During training, two or three sentences are fed into separate, but weight-shared, BERT models. This setup allows the model to learn relationships between multiple sentences simultaneously.

For a siamese network, two sentences (\(A\) and \(B\)) are input. Their embeddings \(u = \text{BERT}(A)\) and \(v = \text{BERT}(B)\) are computed. A similarity score (e.g., cosine similarity) is then calculated, and the model is trained to push this score higher for similar pairs and lower for dissimilar pairs.

A triplet network takes three sentences: an anchor (\(A\)), a positive (\(P\), semantically similar to \(A\)), and a negative (\(N\), semantically dissimilar to \(A\)). The goal is to train the model so that the distance between the anchor and positive embedding is smaller than the distance between the anchor and negative embedding, by at least a certain margin.

Pooling Strategies

BERT produces a contextualized embedding for each token in a sentence. To get a single fixed-size vector for the entire sentence, a pooling strategy is applied:

  • Mean Pooling: The most common and often best-performing strategy. All token embeddings (excluding special tokens like `[CLS]` and `[SEP]`) are averaged to produce the sentence embedding.
  • CLS Token Pooling: Using the embedding of the `[CLS]` token directly. While common in vanilla BERT for classification, it's generally not as effective for STS with SBERT without further fine-tuning.
  • Max Pooling: Taking the maximum value across each dimension of the token embeddings.

Fine-tuning Objective

SBERT is fine-tuned on NLI (Natural Language Inference) datasets, which contain sentence pairs labeled with their relationship (entailment, contradiction, neutral). This explicit training objective teaches the model to produce embeddings that are semantically meaningful for comparison. The loss functions used are typically:

  • Contrastive Loss: For siamese networks, it minimizes the distance between positive pairs and maximizes it for negative pairs.
  • Triplet Loss: For triplet networks, it ensures \(d(A,P) + \text{margin} < d(A,N)\), where \(d\) is a distance metric (e.g., Euclidean distance or 1 - cosine similarity).
🔑 Key Property

SBERT transforms sentences into dense, fixed-size vectors (embeddings) where the geometric distance (e.g., cosine similarity) between vectors directly corresponds to their semantic similarity. This enables efficient retrieval, clustering, and comparison of sentences.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Cosine Similarity

The core mathematical tool for comparing sentence embeddings is cosine similarity. Given two sentence embeddings (vectors) \(u\) and \(v\), their cosine similarity is defined as:

$$\text{similarity}(u, v) = \frac{u \cdot v}{\|u\| \|v\|} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^k u_i v_i}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^k u_i^2} \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^k v_i^2}}$$

Where \(k\) is the dimensionality of the embedding vectors. Cosine similarity measures the cosine of the angle between two vectors. A value of 1 means the vectors are identical in direction (maximum similarity), 0 means they are orthogonal (no similarity), and -1 means they are opposite in direction (maximum dissimilarity). This metric is preferred over Euclidean distance for high-dimensional vectors because it focuses on direction rather than magnitude, which is often more relevant for semantic meaning.

Triplet Loss Function

Triplet loss is a common objective function used to train SBERT. For an anchor sentence \(A\), a positive sentence \(P\) (similar to \(A\)), and a negative sentence \(N\) (dissimilar to \(A\)), the goal is to minimize the distance between \(A\) and \(P\), and maximize the distance between \(A\) and \(N\). Let \(u_A, u_P, u_N\) be their respective embeddings. The triplet loss is:

$$L(u_i, u_j, y_{ij}) = y_{ij} \|u_i - u_j\|_2^2 + (1 - y_{ij}) \max(0, \text{margin} - \|u_i - u_j\|_2^2)$$

If \(y_{ij}=1\) (similar), the loss is \(\|u_i - u_j\|_2^2\), pushing similar embeddings closer. If \(y_{ij}=0\) (dissimilar), the loss is \(\max(0, \text{margin} - \|u_i - u_j\|_2^2)\), pushing dissimilar embeddings further apart until their distance exceeds the margin.

⚠️ Computational Cost

Calculating semantic similarity for a large corpus (N sentences) requires \(O(N^2)\) comparisons, which is computationally expensive. SBERT's strength lies in generating embeddings once, then using efficient similarity search methods (like FAISS for Approximate Nearest Neighbors) to find similar sentences in \(O(N \log N)\) or better time.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use SBERT for STS:

  • Semantic Search: Find documents or passages semantically related to a query, even if they don't share keywords.
  • Clustering: Group similar sentences or documents together.
  • Paraphrase Detection: Identify if two sentences convey the same meaning.
  • Duplicate Content Detection: Find redundant information in large datasets.
  • Recommendation Systems: Recommend items based on semantic similarity to user preferences or other items.
  • Question Answering: Match user questions to relevant answers or knowledge base entries.

When NOT to use / Limitations:

  • Very Long Documents: BERT-based models have a token limit (typically 512 tokens). For documents exceeding this, strategies like chunking or using hierarchical models are needed.
  • Highly Domain-Specific Language: If your text contains jargon or concepts not well-represented in the pre-training data, further domain-specific fine-tuning might be necessary for optimal performance.
  • Resource Constraints: While more efficient than vanilla BERT for STS, SBERT models are still large deep learning models requiring significant computational resources for inference, especially in real-time applications at scale.
  • Absence of Nuance: While excellent, SBERT can still struggle with extremely subtle semantic differences, sarcasm, or complex logical relationships that require deep world knowledge or reasoning.
💡 SBERT's Efficiency Advantage

For comparing \(N\) sentences, vanilla BERT requires \(N^2\) forward passes through the model (one for each pair). SBERT requires only \(N\) forward passes to generate all embeddings, after which \(N^2\) cosine similarity calculations are trivial. This makes SBERT orders of magnitude faster for large-scale \(N > 1\) tasks.

Code Trace
▼
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer, util
import torch
# 1. Load a pre-trained Sentence-BERT model
# 'all-MiniLM-L6-v2' is a popular, efficient model
model = SentenceTransformer('all-MiniLM-L6-v2')
# 2. Define the sentences to compare
sentence_a = "The cat sat on the mat."
sentence_b = "A feline was resting on the rug."
sentence_c = "The dog barked loudly."
# 3. Encode the sentences into embeddings
# This is the most computationally intensive step
embedding_a = model.encode(sentence_a, convert_to_tensor=True)
embedding_b = model.encode(sentence_b, convert_to_tensor=True)
embedding_c = model.encode(sentence_c, convert_to_tensor=True)
# 4. Calculate cosine similarity between embeddings
# This is a fast, vector operation
similarity_ab = util.cos_sim(embedding_a, embedding_b)
similarity_ac = util.cos_sim(embedding_a, embedding_c)
print(f"Similarity (A, B): {similarity_ab.item():.4f}")
print(f"Similarity (A, C): {similarity_ac.item():.4f}")
# Expected Output:
# Similarity (A, B): 0.8500 (High similarity)
# Similarity (A, C): 0.1500 (Low similarity)
