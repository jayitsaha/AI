Information Bottleneck Method

Finding the minimal sufficient statistic: compress X into T while preserving what matters about Y.
Advanced Information Theory Deep Learning Theory I(X;T) − βI(T;Y)
1
Understanding the Concept

What is this? Imagine you're studying for an exam. You have a thick textbook (X) and you want to write a cheat sheet (T) that fits on one page — but you still want to ace the exam (predict Y). The Information Bottleneck method is a principled theory for finding exactly that cheat sheet: the most compressed representation T that still retains the information needed to predict Y.

Real-World Analogy

A radio station compresses voice (X) into a broadcast signal (T) that can be decoded at the receiver (Y = reconstructed voice). Good compression squeezes bandwidth while minimising quality loss. IB asks: what is the minimal signal that preserves voice quality — and gives a precise mathematical answer.

One-sentence summary: The Information Bottleneck finds a stochastic mapping \(p(T|X)\) that minimises the mutual information \(I(X;T)\) (compression) while maximising \(I(T;Y)\) (relevance), controlled by a single Lagrange multiplier \(\beta\).

If you remember one thing

IB solves \(\min_{p(T|X)}\, I(X;T) - \beta\, I(T;Y)\). Small \(\beta\) → maximum compression; large \(\beta\) → maximum relevance. The full trade-off curve, the IB curve, is the Pareto frontier of compression vs. relevance.

2
How It Works — Mathematical Core

We have three random variables: input \(X\), label \(Y\), and compressed representation \(T\). The joint distribution \(p(X, Y)\) is given by the data. We search over all stochastic encoders \(p(T|X)\).

The IB Lagrangian (what we minimise):

$$\mathcal{L}[p(T|X)] = I(X;\,T) \;-\; \beta\; I(T;\,Y)$$

where \(\beta \geq 0\) is the trade-off parameter. Expanding using the data-processing inequality:

$$I(X;\,T) = \sum_{x,t} p(x)\, p(t|x)\,\log\frac{p(t|x)}{p(t)}$$
$$I(T;\,Y) = \sum_{t,y} p(t)\, p(y|t)\,\log\frac{p(y|t)}{p(y)}$$

The self-consistent IB equations (Tishby et al., 1999). Setting \(\delta\mathcal{L}/\delta p(T|X) = 0\) gives the Blahut-Arimoto-like fixed-point equations:

$$p(t|x) = \frac{p(t)}{Z(\beta,x)}\exp\!\left(-\beta\; D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left[p(Y|x)\,\big\|\,p(Y|t)\right]\right)$$
$$p(t) = \sum_x p(t|x)\, p(x)$$
$$p(y|t) = \sum_x p(y|x)\, p(x|t)$$

These three equations are iterated to convergence for each \(\beta\). The partition function \(Z(\beta, x)\) normalises \(p(t|x)\) to sum to 1 over \(t\).

The Markov Chain

A critical constraint: \(Y \to X \to T\) must form a Markov chain — \(T\) is computed only from \(X\), and may only know about \(Y\) through \(X\). This encodes the idea that T is a lossy compression of X, not a side channel to Y.

IB curve & phase transitions. As \(\beta\) increases from 0 to \(\infty\), the optimal \(T\) goes from a single point (completely compressed, \(I(X;T)=0\)) to \(T=X\) (no compression). The curve of achievable \((I(X;T),\, I(T;Y))\) pairs is the IB curve. It is concave and has discrete "phase transitions" where the optimal number of clusters in \(T\) jumps.

Common Confusion

\(\beta\) is not a compression ratio. It is a Lagrange multiplier: it controls the slope of the tangent to the IB curve. Different \(\beta\) values trace out different operating points on the Pareto frontier.

3
IB & Deep Learning — Tishby's Information Plane

In 2017, Tishby & Schwartz-Ziv proposed viewing each layer \(h_k\) of a deep neural network as a compressed representation of the input, and tracking \((I(X;h_k),\, I(h_k;Y))\) during training — the information plane.

The two-phase training hypothesis:

  • Fitting phase (early training): \(I(X;T)\) and \(I(T;Y)\) both increase — the network memorises training data.
  • Compression phase (later training): \(I(T;Y)\) stabilises while \(I(X;T)\) decreases — the network forgets irrelevant input details, compressing toward the IB optimal.
Key Insight

If a deep net implements the IB principle, each layer is closer to the IB curve than the previous one. The final layer representation ideally sits on the IB curve for the target \(\beta\).

Ongoing Debate

Saxe et al. (2019) showed the compression phase is artefact-dependent — it appears with saturating activations (tanh) but not ReLU. The IB interpretation of deep learning remains an active research area, not settled consensus.

Variational IB (VIB). For continuous \(X\) and neural networks, the IB is approximated as a variational bound (Alemi et al., 2017). The encoder outputs a Gaussian \(p(T|X) = \mathcal{N}(\mu(X), \Sigma(X))\) and the objective becomes:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VIB}} = \mathbb{E}_{x,y}\!\left[\mathbb{E}_{t\sim p(t|x)}\!\left[\log q(y|t)\right] - \beta\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left[p(t|x)\,\big\|\,r(t)\right]\right]$$

where \(r(t)\) is a marginal prior (often \(\mathcal{N}(0,I)\)). This is exactly a \(\beta\)-VAE with a classification head — connecting IB to variational autoencoders.

Key Takeaways & When to Use
  • Use IB when you need principled feature compression that provably retains task-relevant information.
  • Variational IB (VIB) is the practical neural-network version — a β-VAE with a classification head.
  • The information plane is a diagnostic tool: plotting layer coordinates during training reveals fitting vs. compression phases.
  • Phase transitions in the IB curve correspond to the minimum number of clusters needed to separate classes — a fundamental result.
  • Related: Rate-distortion theory (continuous X/Y), β-VAE, mutual information neural estimation (MINE), sufficient statistics.
Connection to Sufficiency

The IB optimal T is the minimal sufficient statistic of X for Y. When \(\beta \to \infty\), \(T\) is a sufficient statistic; for finite \(\beta\) it is an approximate (lossy) sufficient statistic — the "relevant" component of X.
