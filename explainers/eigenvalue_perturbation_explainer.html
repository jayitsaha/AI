Eigenvalue Perturbation Theory

How do eigenvalues move when a matrix is slightly changed?
Intermediate Linear Algebra Spectral Theory Weyl & Bauer-Fike
1
Understanding Eigenvalue Perturbation
▼

What is it? Every matrix \(A\) has eigenvalues — special scalars \(\lambda\) where \(Av = \lambda v\). Perturbation theory asks: if we add a small "noise matrix" \(\varepsilon E\) to \(A\), how much do the eigenvalues shift? This lets us bound errors in numerical computations and understand stability of physical systems.

Analogy: Imagine tuning a guitar string. The string's resonant frequencies are the "eigenvalues." If you slightly loosen a tuning peg (the perturbation), the frequency shifts a little. Perturbation theory tells you how much it shifts and whether it shifts smoothly or could jump erratically.

💡 If you remember one thing

For a symmetric matrix, Weyl's inequality gives \(|\lambda_k(A+E) - \lambda_k(A)| \leq \|E\|_2\). For a general diagonalizable matrix, the Bauer-Fike theorem gives \(\min_j|\mu - \lambda_j| \leq \kappa(X)\|E\|_2\), where \(\kappa(X) = \|X\|\|X^{-1}\|\) is the condition number of the eigenvector matrix. Ill-conditioned eigenvectors amplify sensitivity.

2
The Three Key Results
▼

Result 1: First-Order Perturbation (General)

Write \(\tilde{A} = A + \varepsilon E\). Expand eigenvalue and eigenvector in powers of \(\varepsilon\): \(\lambda(\varepsilon) = \lambda_0 + \varepsilon \lambda_1 + \varepsilon^2 \lambda_2 + \cdots\). Plugging into the eigenvalue equation and collecting \(O(\varepsilon)\) terms gives:

$$\delta\lambda = \lambda_1 = \frac{q^\top (E)\, p}{q^\top p}$$

where \(p\) is the right eigenvector of \(A\) and \(q\) is the left eigenvector (\(q^\top A = \lambda q^\top\)). For symmetric matrices, \(q = p\), so \(\delta\lambda = p^\top E\, p / (p^\top p)\).

Result 2: Weyl's Inequality (Symmetric Matrices)

When \(A\) and \(E\) are both symmetric (Hermitian), the eigenvalues are real and ordered: \(\lambda_1 \leq \lambda_2 \leq \cdots \leq \lambda_n\). Weyl's theorem gives an absolute, tight bound:

$$|\lambda_k(A + E) - \lambda_k(A)| \leq \|E\|_2 = \sigma_{\max}(E)$$

This is order-preserving: the \(k\)-th smallest eigenvalue of \(A+E\) is within \(\|E\|_2\) of the \(k\)-th smallest eigenvalue of \(A\).

Result 3: Bauer-Fike Theorem (General Diagonalizable)

If \(A = X \Lambda X^{-1}\) (diagonalizable), then for any eigenvalue \(\mu\) of \(A + E\):

$$\min_j |\mu - \lambda_j(A)| \leq \kappa_p(X) \cdot \|E\|_p$$

where \(\kappa_p(X) = \|X\|_p \|X^{-1}\|_p\) is the condition number of the eigenvector matrix \(X\). When \(A\) is normal (e.g., symmetric), \(X\) is unitary and \(\kappa(X)=1\), recovering Weyl-like sharpness.

🔑 Key Property

The condition number \(\kappa(X)\) measures eigenvector conditioning. When eigenvectors are nearly linearly dependent, small perturbations to \(A\) cause large eigenvalue movements — eigenvalue sensitivity is controlled by eigenvector geometry, not just the perturbation size.

⚠️ Common Misconception

Weyl's inequality does not apply to non-symmetric matrices. For asymmetric \(A\), eigenvalues are complex and the correct bound is Bauer-Fike, which involves \(\kappa(X)\) and can be much larger than \(\|E\|_2\) alone.

3
Mathematical Derivation
▼

First-Order Expansion (Symmetric Case)

Let \(A\) be symmetric with eigenvalue \(\lambda_0\) and unit eigenvector \(q\): \(Aq = \lambda_0 q\), \(\|q\|=1\). Consider \(\tilde{A}(\varepsilon) = A + \varepsilon E\). The eigenvalue equation is:

$$(A + \varepsilon E)\tilde{q}(\varepsilon) = \tilde{\lambda}(\varepsilon)\,\tilde{q}(\varepsilon)$$

Expand: \(\tilde{\lambda}(\varepsilon) = \lambda_0 + \varepsilon\lambda_1 + O(\varepsilon^2)\) and \(\tilde{q}(\varepsilon) = q + \varepsilon q_1 + O(\varepsilon^2)\). Substituting and collecting \(O(\varepsilon)\) terms:

$$Aq_1 + Eq = \lambda_0 q_1 + \lambda_1 q$$

Multiply on the left by \(q^\top\). Since \(q^\top A = \lambda_0 q^\top\) (symmetric), the \(q^\top A q_1\) and \(\lambda_0 q^\top q_1\) terms cancel:

$$q^\top E q = \lambda_1 q^\top q = \lambda_1 \implies \boxed{\delta\lambda = \lambda_1 = q^\top E q}$$

Proof Sketch: Weyl's Inequality

Use the min-max (Courant-Fischer) characterization: \(\lambda_k(A) = \min_{\dim S = k} \max_{x \in S, \|x\|=1} x^\top A x\). For \(\tilde{A} = A+E\):

$$x^\top \tilde{A} x = x^\top A x + x^\top E x \leq x^\top A x + \|E\|_2$$

Applying Courant-Fischer for both \(+E\) and \(-E\) directions gives the symmetric bound \(|\lambda_k(\tilde{A}) - \lambda_k(A)| \leq \|E\|_2\).

Proof Sketch: Bauer-Fike

Let \(\mu\) be an eigenvalue of \(\tilde{A} = A + E\) and \(v\) its eigenvector. Then \((A+E)v = \mu v\), so \(E v = (\mu I - A)v\). Since \(A = X\Lambda X^{-1}\):

$$X^{-1}E v = X^{-1}(\mu I - A)v = (\mu I - \Lambda)X^{-1}v$$

If \(\mu \neq \lambda_j\) for all \(j\), then \((\mu I - \Lambda)\) is invertible, and \(X^{-1}v = (\mu I - \Lambda)^{-1} X^{-1} E v\). Taking norms:

$$\|X^{-1}v\| \leq \|(\mu I - \Lambda)^{-1}\| \cdot \|X^{-1}\| \cdot \|E\| \cdot \|v\|$$

Also \(\|v\| \leq \|X\| \|X^{-1}v\|\), so dividing through yields \(1/\min_j|\mu-\lambda_j| \leq \kappa(X)\|E\|\)/\(\|v\|\cdot\|v\|/\|X\|\|X^{-1}v\|\), ultimately giving the Bauer-Fike bound.

📐 Summary of Bounds

First-order: \(\delta\lambda \approx q^\top(\delta A)q\) (exact to first order, simple dot product).
Weyl (symmetric): \(|\lambda_k(A+E)-\lambda_k(A)| \leq \|E\|_2\) (tight, order-preserving).
Bauer-Fike (general): \(\min_j|\mu-\lambda_j| \leq \kappa(X)\|E\|_2\) (amplified by condition number).

Code: From-Scratch Implementation
▼

Computing perturbation bounds from scratch in NumPy. Each line corresponds to a step in the theory.

import numpy as np
# Define base matrix A (symmetric 3x3) and perturbation E
A = np.array([[4,1,0],[1,3,1],[0,1,2]], dtype=float) # symmetric
E = np.array([[0.1,0.05,0],[0.05,0.1,0.05],[0,0.05,0.1]]) # also symmetric
eps = 0.3
# Compute eigenvalues and eigenvectors of A
lam0, Q = np.linalg.eigh(A) # eigh for symmetric: lam0 sorted ascending
# lam0 = [1.268, 3.000, 4.732] (exact: 3-sqrt(2), 3, 3+sqrt(2))
# First-order perturbation: delta_lambda_k = q_k^T E q_k
delta_lam_fo = np.array([Q[:,k] @ E @ Q[:,k] for k in range(3)])
# delta_lam_fo * eps = first-order shift for each eigenvalue
# Weyl bound (symmetric): |lam_k(A+eps*E) - lam_k(A)| <= eps * ||E||_2
weyl_bound = eps * np.linalg.norm(E, ord=2) # spectral norm = largest singular value
# Actual perturbed eigenvalues
lam_pert, _ = np.linalg.eigh(A + eps * E)
actual_shifts = np.abs(lam_pert - lam0)
# Bauer-Fike: for general (non-symmetric) A
A_gen = np.array([[3,2,0],[0,2,1],[0,0,1]], dtype=float) # non-symmetric
lam_gen, X = np.linalg.eig(A_gen) # general eigenvectors
kappa = np.linalg.cond(X) # condition number of eigenvector matrix
bf_bound = kappa * eps * np.linalg.norm(E, ord=2) # Bauer-Fike bound
# bf_bound >> weyl_bound when eigenvectors are ill-conditioned
💡 Numerical Trace

For the symmetric 3×3 above with \(\varepsilon=0.3\):
 • \(\|E\|_2 \approx 0.193\), so Weyl bound = \(0.3 \times 0.193 \approx 0.058\).
 • First-order estimates: \(\delta\lambda_1 \approx 0.3 \times 0.086 = 0.026\), \(\delta\lambda_2 \approx 0.3 \times 0.100 = 0.030\), \(\delta\lambda_3 \approx 0.3 \times 0.086 = 0.026\).
 • Actual shifts from exact diagonalization are within the Weyl bound (as expected).
 • For the non-symmetric 3×3, \(\kappa(X) \approx 6.5\), so Bauer-Fike bound \(\approx 0.058 \times 6.5 \approx 0.38\) — much looser.

Key Takeaways & Connections
▼

When to Use Each Bound

SituationBest BoundStrength
Symmetric/Hermitian \(A\), symmetric \(E\)Weyl's inequalityTight, \(\kappa(X)=1\)
General diagonalizable \(A\)Bauer-FikeExact order; may be loose if \(\kappa(X)\gg 1\)

Applications in ML/Computing

PCA stability: If you perturb the data matrix, Weyl bounds the shift in explained variance per principal component. PageRank: Perturbation of the link matrix shifts the dominant eigenvector and thus page ranks. Numerical stability: Floating-point errors in matrix entries perturb eigenvalues — condition numbers predict how badly.

🚨 Repeated / Degenerate Eigenvalues

When two eigenvalues are equal or nearly equal (degenerate), first-order perturbation theory breaks down and you must use degenerate perturbation theory. The perturbation \(E\) can split the degenerate eigenspace, and only its projection onto the eigenspace matters for first-order analysis.
