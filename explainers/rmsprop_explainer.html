Understanding RMSProp
▼

What is it? RMSProp (Root Mean Square Propagation) is an adaptive gradient optimizer that automatically adjusts the learning rate for each parameter. Parameters whose gradients have been consistently large get a smaller effective learning rate; parameters with small gradients get a larger one. The key insight is using a decaying average (EMA) of recent squared gradients instead of the full cumulative sum used by AdaGrad.

The problem it solves: AdaGrad accumulates all past squared gradients. In deep networks trained for many steps, this sum grows without bound, shrinking the effective learning rate toward zero — training stalls. RMSProp fixes this with a forgetting factor \(\rho\) that exponentially decays old gradient history.

If you remember one thing

RMSProp replaces AdaGrad's ever-growing sum with an EMA: \(v_t = \rho v_{t-1} + (1-\rho)\,g_t^2\). The parameter update is then \(w_t = w_{t-1} - \dfrac{\eta}{\sqrt{v_t + \varepsilon}}\,g_t\). The denominator normalises the gradient by recent magnitude — large recent gradients shrink the step; small recent gradients enlarge it.

Geoffrey Hinton introduced RMSProp in his Coursera lecture (2012, unpublished). It predates Adam, which later refined RMSProp by adding momentum. RMSProp remains the optimizer of choice for RNNs and is widely used in RL.

How RMSProp Works Internally
▼

The Three-Line Algorithm

For each parameter \(w\) at step \(t\):

$$v_t = \rho\, v_{t-1} + (1 - \rho)\, g_t^2$$
$$w_t = w_{t-1} - \frac{\eta}{\sqrt{v_t + \varepsilon}}\, g_t$$

where \(g_t = \nabla_w \mathcal{L}\) is the gradient, \(\rho \approx 0.9\) is the decay factor, \(\eta\) is the global learning rate, and \(\varepsilon \approx 10^{-8}\) prevents division by zero.

The EMA as a "Running Average"

\(v_t\) is an exponential moving average of \(g_t^2\). Unrolling the recurrence:

$$v_t = (1-\rho)\sum_{k=0}^{t} \rho^{t-k}\, g_k^2$$

The weight on gradient \(g_k^2\) decays as \(\rho^{t-k}\). With \(\rho=0.9\), gradients from 10 steps ago carry weight \(0.9^{10} \approx 0.35\); gradients from 50 steps ago carry \(0.9^{50} \approx 0.005\) — effectively forgotten. This is the "moving" in "moving average."

Why Divide by \(\sqrt{v_t}\)?

The denominator \(\sqrt{v_t}\) estimates the root-mean-square (RMS) of recent gradients. Dividing \(g_t\) by the RMS normalises the update to be dimensionless — regardless of the gradient's scale in that direction. A direction that has had large gradients (steep) gets a smaller normalised step; a direction with tiny gradients (flat) gets a proportionally larger step.

Key Invariant

The effective learning rate in direction \(i\) is \(\eta_i^{\text{eff}} = \eta / \sqrt{v_{t,i} + \varepsilon}\). When gradient magnitude in direction \(i\) is roughly constant, \(v_{t,i} \approx g_i^2\), so \(\eta_i^{\text{eff}} \approx \eta / |g_i|\) — the step size is approximately constant in all directions, equalising progress across steep and flat regions.

Common Pitfall: No Bias Correction

RMSProp initialises \(v_0 = 0\). At step 1, \(v_1 = (1-\rho)g_1^2\), which underestimates the true variance. Adam fixes this with a bias-correction term \(v_t/(1-\rho^t)\). RMSProp omits it, so early steps may have inflated effective learning rates. In practice, this rarely causes problems because most training uses warm-up schedules anyway.

Why It Works — The Math
▼

Diagonal Preconditioning View

RMSProp approximates second-order optimisation. Newton's method uses: \(w_t = w_{t-1} - H_t^{-1} g_t\), where \(H_t\) is the Hessian. The diagonal of the Fisher information matrix approximates the Hessian, and \(\mathbb{E}[g_t^2]\) approximates the diagonal Fisher. RMSProp estimates this diagonal online:

$$w_t = w_{t-1} - \underbrace{\eta \, \hat{F}_t^{-1/2}}_{\text{preconditioned LR}} \cdot g_t$$

where \(\hat{F}_t = \operatorname{diag}(\sqrt{v_t + \varepsilon})\) is a diagonal approximation of the square root of the Fisher matrix.

EMA vs Cumulative Sum: Why the Difference Matters

Define AdaGrad's accumulator \(G_t = \sum_{k=1}^{t} g_k^2\) (monotone increasing). The effective learning rate is \(\eta/\sqrt{G_t}\), which decreases at rate \(O(1/\sqrt{t})\) even if gradients are constant. For convex problems with finite horizon \(T\), this is fine — AdaGrad achieves \(O(\sqrt{T})\) regret. But for non-convex deep networks, gradients oscillate and the problem is effectively non-stationary. RMSProp's EMA \(v_t\) tracks a recent local estimate, adapting to the current gradient distribution rather than all history.

Steady-State Analysis

If gradient magnitudes are constant, \(g_t^2 \equiv c\), then \(v_t \to c\) as \(t \to \infty\) (fixed point of the EMA). The effective LR converges to \(\eta/\sqrt{c+\varepsilon}\). Compare AdaGrad: effective LR \(\to 0\). This is the fundamental advantage of EMA over cumulative sum.

Numerical Trace (2D Problem)

Consider \(f(w) = 50w_0^2 + 0.5w_1^2\) starting at \(w_0=0.5, w_1=2.0\), with \(\rho=0.9\), \(\eta=0.1\). At step 1:

  • \(g_0 = 100 \cdot 0.5 = 50,\quad g_1 = 1.0 \cdot 2.0 = 2.0\)
  • \(v_0 = 0.1 \cdot 50^2 = 250,\quad v_1 = 0.1 \cdot 2^2 = 0.4\)
  • \(\text{eff LR}_0 = 0.1/\sqrt{250} \approx 0.00632,\quad \text{eff LR}_1 = 0.1/\sqrt{0.4} \approx 0.158\)
  • Step 0: \(\Delta w_0 = 0.00632 \cdot 50 = 0.316\), Step 1: \(\Delta w_1 = 0.158 \cdot 2 = 0.316\)

Both parameters take equal-sized steps despite gradient magnitudes differing 25-fold. This is the isotropic-update property of RMSProp.

Key Takeaways & Comparisons
▼

RMSProp vs the Optimizer Family

Think of the optimizer family as a hierarchy of ideas:

  • SGD: Fixed LR \(\eta\), no per-parameter adaptation.
  • AdaGrad: Adapts LR using cumulative sum \(G_t = \sum g_k^2\). Great for sparse gradients, but LR decays to 0.
  • RMSProp: Replaces cumulative sum with EMA \(v_t = \rho v_{t-1} + (1-\rho)g_t^2\). Solves AdaGrad's decay problem.
  • Adam: RMSProp + momentum on gradients + bias correction. Usually the strongest all-around optimizer.
When to use RMSProp

RNNs / LSTMs: Non-stationary gradients from variable-length sequences. RMSProp's forgetting adapts better than AdaGrad. • Reinforcement Learning: Non-stationary reward signal means gradient distribution shifts over time. • When Adam is overkill: RMSProp has fewer hyperparameters and less memory overhead (no \(m_t\) momentum buffer).

Hyperparameter Guide

  • \(\rho = 0.9\): Standard. Higher (0.99) = more memory of the past. Lower (0.5) = more reactive to recent gradients.
  • \(\eta \in [10^{-4}, 10^{-2}]\): Start with \(10^{-3}\).
  • \(\varepsilon = 10^{-8}\): Rarely tuned; increase to \(10^{-5}\) if training is unstable.

import numpy as np
def rmsprop_step(w, v, grad, lr=0.1, rho=0.9, eps=1e-8):
# Step 1: update EMA of squared gradients
v_new = rho * v + (1 - rho) * grad**2
# Step 2: compute adapted update
w_new = w - lr * grad / (np.sqrt(v_new) + eps)
return w_new, v_new
# --- Numerical trace ---
w = np.array([0.5, 2.0]) # start: steep and flat directions
v = np.zeros(2) # EMA initialized to 0
for t in range(1, 9):
g = np.array([100*w[0], 1.0*w[1]]) # grad of 50w0²+0.5w1²
v = 0.9*v + 0.1*g**2 # EMA update
w = w - 0.1 * g / (np.sqrt(v) + 1e-8) # parameter step
eff_lr = 0.1 / (np.sqrt(v) + 1e-8) # effective LR
print(f"t={t}: w={w.round(4)}, eff_lr={eff_lr.round(4)}")
