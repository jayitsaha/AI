Understanding Two-Stream Networks
▼

What is it? A two-stream network recognizes actions in video ("running", "waving", "jumping jacks") by running two separate convolutional networks in parallel: one looks at a single RGB frame (appearance), the other looks at a stack of optical-flow fields computed between consecutive frames (motion). Each network independently outputs a class-probability guess, and the guesses are averaged to make the final prediction.

Analogy: Imagine two witnesses to an event. The first witness only sees one photograph — they can tell you the scene looks like a gym, and there's a person mid-stride, but not whether they're walking or sprinting. The second witness is blindfolded to color and shape but wears motion-tracking gloves that only feel velocity — they can't tell you it's a gym, but they can tell you exactly how fast and in what direction the limbs moved. Neither witness alone tells the full story; together, their testimony pins down the action.

Summary: Two-stream ConvNets decompose video into "appearance" (spatial stream, single RGB frame) and "motion" (temporal stream, stacked optical flow), train a CNN on each, and fuse their softmax predictions — because appearance and motion are statistically very different signals that are easier to learn separately than jointly.

💡 If you remember one thing

A single video frame alone is nearly blind to motion, and raw pixel differences alone are nearly blind to appearance/context. Two-stream networks solve this by never asking one network to do both jobs: $$p_{\text{fused}} = \alpha\, p_{\text{spatial}} + (1-\alpha)\, p_{\text{temporal}}$$ where \(p_{\text{spatial}}\) comes from a CNN on a still RGB frame and \(p_{\text{temporal}}\) comes from a CNN on stacked optical flow.

How It Works Internally
▼

The Two Streams

Spatial stream: a standard image-classification CNN (e.g. a VGG/AlexNet-style architecture, in the original Simonyan & Zisserman (2014) paper pretrained on ImageNet) that takes ONE RGB frame \(I_\tau \in \mathbb{R}^{H\times W \times 3}\) and predicts class scores. It captures scene context, objects, and body pose — everything you could infer from a single photo.

Temporal stream: a CNN with an almost identical architecture, but its input is a stack of optical flow fields across \(L\) consecutive frame pairs, giving a \(H \times W \times 2L\) volume (2 channels per pair: horizontal displacement \(d_x\) and vertical displacement \(d_y\)). This CNN never sees color or texture — only where pixels moved. It is trained from scratch (or with heavy multi-task regularization) because ImageNet has no motion statistics to transfer.

Optical Flow: The Bridge from Pixels to Motion

Optical flow estimates, for every pixel (or patch), a displacement vector \((d_x, d_y)\) describing where that pixel's content moved to in the next frame. Classical methods (Farneback, TV-L1) minimize a brightness-constancy + smoothness objective; our visualization below uses a simplified block-matching search (minimize sum-of-squared-differences over a small search window) — the same core idea, computed live.

Late Fusion: Combining Two Independent Opinions

At test time the two streams' softmax outputs are combined — originally by simple averaging, or by training a linear SVM on the two streams' L2-normalized scores. Crucially, in the original 2014 design there is no joint backpropagation through the fusion step: each stream is trained independently to convergence, and fusion happens only at inference. Later architectures (e.g. two-stream I3D, SlowFast) instead fuse features mid-network and train end-to-end.

🔑 Design Decision: Why Not One Network on Stacked RGB+Flow?

Simonyan & Zisserman explicitly tested "early fusion" — a single ConvNet trained directly on RGB frames stacked with flow. It underperformed two separately-trained, late-fused streams. Appearance filters (edges, colors, textures) and motion filters (displacement gradients) have very different statistics; forcing one set of convolutional filters to represent both wastes capacity and hurts each task. Splitting into two streams lets each network specialize.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Stacking Optical Flow into a Network Input

Let \(d_x^t(u,v)\) and \(d_y^t(u,v)\) be the horizontal/vertical flow at pixel \((u,v)\) between frames \(t\) and \(t+1\). For a clip starting at frame \(\tau\), the temporal stream's input volume \(I_\tau \in \mathbb{R}^{H\times W \times 2L}\) stacks \(L\) consecutive flow fields:

$$I_\tau(u,v,2k{-}1) = d_x^{\tau+k-1}(u,v), \qquad I_\tau(u,v,2k) = d_y^{\tau+k-1}(u,v), \qquad k = 1,\dots,L$$

The first convolutional layer's filters therefore learn to detect local motion patterns (e.g. "consistent downward flow" = falling, "oscillating left-right flow" = waving) rather than edges or colors.

Independent Softmax Losses

Each stream is trained with the standard cross-entropy loss on its own softmax output. For raw class scores (logits) \(z \in \mathbb{R}^C\):

$$p_c = \frac{e^{z_c}}{\sum_{j=1}^{C} e^{z_j}}, \qquad \mathcal{L} = -\sum_{c=1}^{C} y_c \log p_c$$

Because \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{spatial}}\) and \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{temporal}}\) are optimized separately, gradients never mix between streams during training in the original formulation — the only "interaction" is the test-time average.

Late Fusion as a Convex Combination

$$p_{\text{fused}} = \alpha\, p_{\text{spatial}} + (1-\alpha)\, p_{\text{temporal}}, \qquad \hat{y} = \operatorname*{argmax}_c\ p_{\text{fused},c}$$

Averaging softmax outputs (rather than logits) works because both are already calibrated to the probability simplex \(\Delta^{C-1}\); an equal-weight average (\(\alpha=0.5\)) is a strong default, and the paper's ablations show fused accuracy exceeds either stream alone on UCF-101 and HMDB-51.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

Optical flow is expensive to compute (classical TV-L1 flow can take longer than the CNN forward pass itself!) and is not differentiable end-to-end through the flow-estimation step in the classic pipeline. This is exactly why later 3D-ConvNet (C3D, I3D-RGB) and video-transformer models try to learn motion implicitly from raw RGB clips instead of relying on a hand-designed flow front-end.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use two-stream architectures:

  • Action/activity recognition where motion is diagnostic (e.g. distinguishing "wave" from "jumping jack" that may look similar in a single frame)
  • Limited labeled video but abundant labeled images — the spatial stream can transfer ImageNet features; the temporal stream can be pretrained with multi-task learning across video datasets
  • Offline / batch video analysis where the cost of computing optical flow is acceptable

When NOT to use:

  • Real-time / low-latency applications — classical optical flow (TV-L1) is a major bottleneck; consider 3D CNNs (C3D, I3D) or motion vectors decoded directly from compressed video instead
  • Very long-range temporal reasoning — stacking \(L\) flow frames only covers a short temporal window; use temporal pooling/segment networks (TSN) or transformers (TimeSformer, ViViT) for long clips
  • End-to-end differentiable pipelines — the classic hand-crafted flow front-end blocks joint gradient-based optimization; use learned motion representations if you need full differentiability
💡 Lineage

Two-Stream (2014) → Temporal Segment Networks (2016) (sparse sampling + segmental consensus) → I3D (2017) (inflated 3D convs, still two-stream RGB+flow) → SlowFast (2019) (two RGB pathways at different frame rates instead of RGB+flow) → Video Transformers (2021+) (learn spatio-temporal attention directly from RGB, no explicit optical flow).

0.5, 3, 15, 3, 5, 1.2, 0) # (H, W, 2): dx, dy
