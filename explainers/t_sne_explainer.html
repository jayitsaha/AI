Understanding t-SNE
▼

What is it? t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) is a non-linear dimensionality reduction technique primarily used for visualizing high-dimensional datasets. It maps data points from a high-dimensional space to a lower-dimensional space (typically 2D or 3D) in such a way that similar points in the high-dimensional space are mapped to nearby points in the low-dimensional space, and dissimilar points are mapped to distant points.

Analogy: Imagine you have a crumpled piece of paper covered in dots. Each dot represents a high-dimensional data point, and how close they are on the crumpled paper is their high-dimensional similarity. t-SNE's job is to carefully flatten this paper onto a table (the 2D low-dimensional space) without tearing or stretching apart dots that were close together on the crumpled paper. It prioritizes preserving the "local neighborhoods" — ensuring that if two dots were close on the crumpled paper, they remain close on the table.

💡 If you remember one thing

t-SNE defines probabilistic similarities between points in both high-dimensional and low-dimensional spaces, then uses gradient descent to minimize the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between these two distributions. It's designed to preserve local structure, often revealing clusters that linear methods like PCA might miss.

How t-SNE Works Internally
▼

1. High-Dimensional Probabilities (\(P_{ij}\))

For each data point \(x_i\), t-SNE constructs a Gaussian distribution centered at \(x_i\). The probability \(p_{j|i}\) that \(x_i\) would pick \(x_j\) as its neighbor is proportional to the Gaussian probability density at \(x_j\). Crucially, the variance \(\sigma_i^2\) of this Gaussian is set individually for each point such that the perplexity of \(p_{j|i}\) equals a user-defined value. This makes \(\sigma_i\) smaller in dense regions (fewer neighbors needed to reach target perplexity) and larger in sparse regions.

2. Low-Dimensional Probabilities (\(Q_{ij}\))

Similarly, in the low-dimensional embedding space, t-SNE models similarities between points \(y_i\) and \(y_j\) using a Student's t-distribution with 1 degree of freedom (which is equivalent to a Cauchy distribution). The probability \(q_{ij}\) is proportional to the inverse of \(1 + \|y_i - y_j\|^2\). The key difference from the high-dimensional Gaussian is the heavy tails of the t-distribution, which helps alleviate the "crowding problem."

3. The Crowding Problem & Heavy Tails

In high dimensions, a point can have many equidistant neighbors. When projected to 2D, these neighbors would be forced to occupy a small area, leading to "crowding." Gaussian distributions in low dimensions would struggle to separate these points without making distant points appear too close. The Student's t-distribution, with its heavy tails, allows moderately distant points in the low-dimensional map to represent large distances in the high-dimensional space without requiring excessive separation, thus mitigating crowding.

4. Optimization via KL Divergence

t-SNE uses gradient descent to minimize the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between the high-dimensional probability distribution \(P\) and the low-dimensional distribution \(Q\). The cost function encourages \(P_{ij}\) and \(Q_{ij}\) to be as similar as possible. The gradient drives attractive forces between points with high \(P_{ij}\) and \(Q_{ij}\) and repulsive forces between points with low \(P_{ij}\) and high \(Q_{ij}\).

🔑 Key Property: Perplexity

Perplexity is t-SNE's most critical hyperparameter. It can be thought of as a smooth measure of the effective number of neighbors each point has. A higher perplexity value means each point considers more neighbors, influencing the global structure more. Typical values are between 5 and 50. Too low, and local structure dominates; too high, and global structure might dominate, or points might merge.

Why It Works — The Math
▼

1. High-Dimensional Conditional Probabilities

The conditional probability \(p_{j|i}\) that point \(x_i\) would pick \(x_j\) as its neighbor is given by a Gaussian centered at \(x_i\):

$$p_{j|i} = \frac{\exp(-\|x_i - x_j\|^2 / 2\sigma_i^2)}{\sum_{k \neq i} \exp(-\|x_i - x_k\|^2 / 2\sigma_i^2)}$$

Here, \(\sigma_i\) is chosen such that the perplexity of the conditional distribution \(P_i\) matches a user-defined value. Perplexity is defined as \(2^{H(P_i)}\), where \(H(P_i)\) is the Shannon entropy of \(P_i\).

2. Symmetric High-Dimensional Probabilities

To simplify the gradient calculations, t-SNE uses a symmetric version of the high-dimensional probabilities:

$$P_{ij} = \frac{p_{j|i} + p_{i|j}}{2N}$$

This symmetric probability ensures that \(\sum_i \sum_j P_{ij} = 1\), making \(P\) a valid probability distribution over all pairs of points.

3. Low-Dimensional Probabilities (Student's t-distribution)

In the low-dimensional embedding space, the similarity between points \(y_i\) and \(y_j\) is modeled by a Student's t-distribution (with 1 degree of freedom):

$$Q_{ij} = \frac{(1 + \|y_i - y_j\|^2)^{-1}}{\sum_{k \neq l} (1 + \|y_k - y_l\|^2)^{-1}}$$

The heavy tails of this distribution allow points that are moderately far apart in the low-dimensional space to represent "true" neighbors from the high-dimensional space without incurring a high cost.

4. Cost Function and Gradient

The cost function is the KL divergence between \(P\) and \(Q\):

$$C = \sum_i \sum_j P_{ij} \log \frac{P_{ij}}{Q_{ij}}$$

Minimizing this cost function with respect to the low-dimensional coordinates \(y_i\) is done using gradient descent. The gradient is given by:

$$\frac{\partial C}{\partial y_i} = 4 \sum_j (P_{ij} - Q_{ij})(y_i - y_j)(1 + \|y_i - y_j\|^2)^{-1}$$

This gradient represents a sum of forces: attractive forces pull points \(y_i\) and \(y_j\) together if \(P_{ij} > Q_{ij}\), and repulsive forces push them apart if \(P_{ij} < Q_{ij}\). The \((1 + \|y_i - y_j\|^2)^{-1}\) term acts as a spring constant, ensuring that points that are already far apart exert weaker repulsive forces.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

t-SNE's global structure (distances between clusters) is often not meaningful. It's excellent for revealing clusters, but the exact spacing between clusters or the overall shape of the embedding should not be over-interpreted. Also, its \(O(N^2)\) or \(O(N \log N)\) complexity makes it slow for very large datasets, often requiring approximations (e.g., Barnes-Hut t-SNE).

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use t-SNE:

  • Visualization: Your primary goal is to visually explore the structure of high-dimensional data, identify clusters, or understand relationships between data points.
  • Non-linear relationships: When linear methods like PCA fail to reveal meaningful structure because relationships between variables are non-linear.
  • Local structure preservation: When preserving the "neighborhoods" of points is more important than preserving global distances.
  • Exploratory Data Analysis: To gain insights into complex datasets, such as gene expression data, image features, or text embeddings.

When NOT to use t-SNE:

  • Global structure interpretation: Do not interpret distances between clusters or the overall shape of the embedding as quantitative measures of similarity or density. t-SNE distorts global distances.
  • Computational efficiency: For very large datasets (millions of points), vanilla t-SNE is too slow (\(O(N^2)\) or \(O(N \log N)\) for optimized versions). Consider UMAP or other faster methods.
  • Dimensionality reduction for downstream tasks: The output of t-SNE is typically not suitable as input for other machine learning models, as it's optimized for visualization, not feature engineering.
  • Reproducibility: Due to its non-convex optimization and random initialization, t-SNE embeddings can vary between runs, requiring careful interpretation and multiple runs.
💡 t-SNE vs. PCA vs. UMAP

PCA (Principal Component Analysis) is linear, fast, preserves global variance, but often fails to separate non-linearly separable clusters. t-SNE is non-linear, slower, preserves local neighborhoods well, excels at cluster separation for visualization, but distorts global distances. UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) is a newer non-linear method, often faster than t-SNE, scales better, and tends to preserve more global structure while still revealing local clusters, making it a popular alternative.
