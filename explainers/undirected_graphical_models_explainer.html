Understanding Undirected Graphical Models
▼

What is it? A Markov Random Field (MRF) draws relationships between variables as plain lines with no arrows — there's no "cause" and "effect," only "these two tend to agree/disagree." Every small fully-connected cluster of nodes (a clique) gets a potential function: a non-negative number scoring how compatible a joint setting of that cluster is. Multiply all clique scores together, divide by one big normalizing constant \(Z\) (the partition function), and you get a valid joint probability.

Analogy: A group photo where friends cluster together. Nobody directs positions top-down (no arrows); each pair just has a compatibility score ("Alice and Bob like standing close"). The whole crowd's arrangement probability is the product of every pairwise compatibility, rescaled so all possible arrangements sum to 1.

💡 If you remember one thing

$$P(x) = \frac{1}{Z}\prod_{C \in \mathcal{C}(G)} \phi_C(x_C), \qquad Z = \sum_{x} \prod_{C} \phi_C(x_C)$$ Potentials are compatibility scores, not probabilities — only the whole normalized product is a probability.

2
How MRFs Work Internally
▼

Cliques Define the Local Factors

Given graph \(G=(V,E)\), a clique is a fully-connected subset of nodes; a maximal clique can't be extended further. The Hammersley-Clifford theorem says every strictly positive distribution consistent with \(G\)'s Markov structure factorizes as a product over exactly the maximal cliques — no more, no fewer.

Why Undirected Instead of Directed?

A directed graph (Bayesian Network) needs an acyclic ordering to define "parents," so its local conditional probability tables are automatically normalized. Undirected graphs can be cyclic (e.g. a 4-node ring A–B–C–D–A) with no such ordering — there's no consistent way to carve that into normalized local conditionals. The price of dropping direction is a single global normalizer \(Z\) instead of automatic local normalization.

Design Decision: Why Potentials, Not Probabilities?

Forcing every clique factor to be a valid probability distribution over its variables would over-constrain the model — there's no acyclic order to anchor "conditional on what." Allowing arbitrary non-negative potentials (even values > 1) is strictly more flexible, at the cost of needing to compute \(Z\) globally.

🔑 Key Property

Graph separation ⟺ conditional independence. If node set \(S\) separates \(A\) from \(B\) in \(G\) (every path from \(A\) to \(B\) passes through \(S\)), then \(X_A \perp X_B \mid X_S\) — the global Markov property, proven equivalent to clique-factorization by Hammersley-Clifford.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

The Gibbs / Energy Form

Potentials are usually written in exponential form to guarantee positivity and numerical stability:

$$\phi_C(x_C) = \exp\big(-E_C(x_C)\big) \implies P(x) = \frac{1}{Z}\exp\Big(-\sum_C E_C(x_C)\Big)$$

This is exactly the Boltzmann distribution — MRFs inherited this form directly from the Ising model in statistical physics.

Hammersley-Clifford Theorem

For a strictly positive \(P(x) > 0\), these are equivalent: (1) \(P\) satisfies the Markov properties of \(G\); (2) \(P\) factorizes over \(G\)'s maximal cliques:

$$P(x) = \frac{1}{Z}\prod_{C \in \mathcal{C}(G)} \phi_C(x_C)$$

Proof sketch (factorization ⟹ Markov): fix node \(i\), split the rest into neighbors \(N(i)\) and the remainder \(R\). Every clique either contains \(i\) (so its other members are all in \(N(i)\), by definition of the graph) or excludes \(i\) entirely. Grouping the product into "touches \(i\)" vs "doesn't touch \(i\)" shows the term depending on \(X_i\) separates multiplicatively from \(X_R\) given \(X_{N(i)}\) — exactly \(X_i \perp X_R \mid X_{N(i)}\). The converse direction is the hard part of the original proof (Möbius inversion over clique subsets).

Training Gradient

For log-linear potentials \(\phi_C(x_C) = \exp(\theta_C^\top f_C(x_C))\):

$$\frac{\partial \log P(x;\theta)}{\partial \theta_C} = f_C(x_C) - \mathbb{E}_{P(x';\theta)}[f_C(x'_C)]$$

"Observed minus expected" — the expectation needs inference under the model at every gradient step, which is why MRF/CRF training is costlier than a DAG's closed-form MLE.

⚠️ Common Misconception

"Potentials are just unnormalized probabilities." Not quite — a potential can exceed 1 and is only meaningful up to a per-clique rescaling; only the full product divided by \(Z\) must be a valid probability. Also, Hammersley-Clifford requires \(P(x)>0\) everywhere — the Moussouris (1974) counterexample shows the equivalence can fail without positivity.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use MRFs / CRFs:

  • Relationships are symmetric/non-causal — pixel neighborhoods, spatial correlation, social homophily
  • Structure has cycles that a DAG can't represent without spurious extra edges
  • Structured prediction over sequences/grids where you want rich overlapping features (CRFs for NER, POS tagging)

When NOT to use:

  • You have genuine causal/generative structure and want cheap ancestral sampling — use a Bayesian Network instead
  • The graph is dense and exact inference (computing \(Z\)) is required — falls back to approximate inference (loopy BP, MCMC)
💡 The Core Trade

Directed models buy automatic normalization by committing to an acyclic causal order. Undirected models buy freedom from that ordering (and native cycle support) at the cost of a single global partition function \(Z\) that is generally intractable to compute exactly.

import numpy as np, itertools
# Pairwise potentials for a 4-cycle MRF: A-B-C-D-A
phi_AB = np.array([[2.,1.],[1.,2.]]) # rewards A==B
phi_BC = np.array([[1.,2.],[2.,1.]]) # rewards B!=C
phi_CD = np.array([[2.,1.],[1.,2.]]) # rewards C==D
phi_DA = np.array([[1.,1.],[1.,2.]]) # slight D==A==1 pref
def unnorm(a,b,c,d):
return phi_AB[a,b]*phi_BC[b,c]*phi_CD[c,d]*phi_DA[d,a]
# Brute-force Z: sum over all 2^4 = 16 states
Z = 0.0; table = {}
for a,b,c,d in itertools.product([0,1],repeat=4):
w = unnorm(a,b,c,d)
table[(a,b,c,d)] = w
Z += w
P = {s: w/Z for s,w in table.items()}
print(Z) # 67.0
print(P[(1,0,1,0)]) # 0.0299
