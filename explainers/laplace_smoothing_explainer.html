Laplace Smoothing

Additive smoothing that eliminates zero-probability events in Naive Bayes and language models
Foundational Classical ML Naive Bayes O(V) per class
1
The Zero-Probability Problem
▼

The fatal flaw of maximum-likelihood estimation for discrete events: if a word never appeared in training documents of class SPAM, its estimated probability is 0. Multiply that zero into a Naive Bayes product — and the entire posterior for SPAM collapses to zero, regardless of every other strong signal.

This is not a minor numerical quirk. A spam filter that has never seen the word "hello" in training spam will always classify "buy hello cheap" as not-spam, even though "buy" and "cheap" are overwhelmingly spammy.

💀 The Zero-Count Catastrophe

Naive Bayes computes \(\hat{y} = \arg\max_c \; P(c) \prod_{i} P(w_i \mid c)\). One unseen word forces \(P(w_i \mid c) = 0\), which multiplies the entire product to zero. The chain rule of probability turns a single unseen count into total certainty of the wrong class.

Where does this come from? The Maximum Likelihood Estimate (MLE) for a word \(w\) in class \(c\) with vocabulary \(\mathcal{V}\) is:

$$\hat{P}_{\text{MLE}}(w \mid c) = \frac{\text{count}(w, c)}{\sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} \text{count}(v, c)} = \frac{N_{wc}}{N_c}$$

If \(N_{wc} = 0\), the probability is 0. Laplace smoothing pretends each word was observed \(\alpha\) extra times — effectively adding virtual pseudocounts to every cell of the count table.

2
How Laplace (Additive) Smoothing Works
▼

The fix is elegant: instead of counting only what you observed, pretend you observed each possible outcome an additional \(\alpha\) times. This single change removes every zero probability while barely affecting cells with large counts.

$$\hat{P}_{\alpha}(w \mid c) = \frac{N_{wc} + \alpha}{N_c + \alpha \lvert\mathcal{V}\rvert}$$

where \(N_{wc}\) is the raw count of word \(w\) in class \(c\), \(N_c = \sum_v N_{vc}\) is the total words in class \(c\), and \(\lvert\mathcal{V}\rvert\) is the vocabulary size. The denominator grows by \(\alpha|\mathcal{V}|\) to keep all probabilities summing to 1.

The \(\alpha\) parameter controls the interpolation

  • \(\alpha \to 0\): recovers MLE — zeros survive, probabilities are pure data-driven.
  • \(\alpha = 1\): classic Laplace smoothing (also called add-one smoothing). Each outcome gets one pseudocount.
  • \(\alpha \to \infty\): all probabilities collapse toward the uniform distribution \(1/|\mathcal{V}|\).
  • Any \(\alpha > 0\): called Lidstone smoothing or additive smoothing.
💡 Intuition — Virtual Documents

Laplace smoothing is equivalent to augmenting your training corpus with \(\alpha\) synthetic documents, each containing exactly one occurrence of every vocabulary word. These imaginary documents prevent any word from having a zero count, without adding any real signal.

Worked example

Vocabulary: {spam, buy, hello, friend, money} — size \(|\mathcal{V}|=5\).

SPAM training: "buy money buy spam" + "spam money buy" → total \(N_{\text{spam}}=7\) words. Raw counts: spam=2, buy=3, hello=0, friend=0, money=2.

With \(\alpha=1\): \(P(\text{hello} \mid \text{SPAM}) = (0+1)/(7+5) = 1/12 \approx 0.0833\) — no longer zero.

The denominator \(N_c + \alpha|\mathcal{V}| = 7 + 1 \times 5 = 12\) ensures the distribution still sums to 1: \(\sum_w (N_{wc}+\alpha)/(N_c+\alpha|\mathcal{V}|) = (N_c + \alpha|\mathcal{V}|)/(N_c+\alpha|\mathcal{V}|) = 1\). ✓

🔑 Why the denominator must grow

Adding \(\alpha\) to every numerator adds \(\alpha \times |\mathcal{V}|\) total counts. To keep probabilities summing to 1, the denominator must absorb this: \(N_c \to N_c + \alpha|\mathcal{V}|\). Forgetting this — a classic bug — makes all probabilities larger than 1.

3
Full Derivation — MLE, MAP, and Dirichlet Prior
▼

Step 1 — MLE and the zero problem

Let \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_c = (\theta_{1c}, \ldots, \theta_{Vc})\) be the multinomial parameter vector for class \(c\), where \(\theta_{wc} = P(w \mid c)\). The multinomial likelihood for the observed counts \(\mathbf{N}_c = (N_{1c}, \ldots, N_{Vc})\) is:

$$\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_c) = \prod_{w=1}^{V} \theta_{wc}^{N_{wc}}$$

Maximizing subject to \(\sum_w \theta_{wc} = 1\) via Lagrange multipliers gives MLE \(\hat{\theta}_{wc}^{\text{MLE}} = N_{wc}/N_c\). Any zero count \(\to\) zero probability.

Step 2 — The Dirichlet prior

Place a Dirichlet prior on \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_c\) with concentration parameter \(\alpha\) (symmetric prior, same \(\alpha\) for each word):

$$P(\boldsymbol{\theta}_c) = \mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_c \mid \alpha \mathbf{1}) = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha V)}{\Gamma(\alpha)^V} \prod_{w=1}^{V} \theta_{wc}^{\alpha - 1}$$

The Dirichlet is the conjugate prior for the multinomial — the posterior is also Dirichlet.

Step 3 — MAP estimate = Laplace smoothing

The posterior is \(P(\boldsymbol{\theta}_c \mid \mathbf{N}_c) \propto \prod_w \theta_{wc}^{N_{wc} + \alpha - 1}\), i.e. \(\mathrm{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_c \mid \mathbf{N}_c + \alpha)\). The Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) estimate of a Dirichlet is:

$$\hat{\theta}_{wc}^{\text{MAP}} = \frac{N_{wc} + \alpha - 1}{N_c + \alpha V - V} = \frac{N_{wc} + \alpha - 1}{N_c + (\alpha-1)V}$$

Setting \(\alpha_{\text{prior}} \leftarrow \alpha + 1\) (i.e. using the mean of the posterior rather than the mode), or equivalently using the posterior mean:

$$\hat{\theta}_{wc}^{\text{Bayes}} = \frac{N_{wc} + \alpha}{N_c + \alpha V}$$

This is exactly the Laplace smoothing formula. So Laplace smoothing with parameter \(\alpha\) is equivalent to placing a symmetric \(\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha)\) prior and using the posterior mean.

Step 4 — The interpolation interpretation

We can rewrite the smoothed estimate as a convex combination of the MLE and the uniform prior:

$$\hat{\theta}_{wc}^{\alpha} = \underbrace{\frac{N_c}{N_c + \alpha V}}_{\lambda} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{N_{wc}}{N_c}}_{\text{MLE}} + \underbrace{\frac{\alpha V}{N_c + \alpha V}}_{1-\lambda} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{1}{V}}_{\text{uniform}}$$

As \(\alpha \to 0\): \(\lambda \to 1\), recovers MLE. As \(\alpha \to \infty\): \(\lambda \to 0\), collapses to uniform \(1/V\). The parameter \(\alpha\) controls how much we trust the data vs. our prior belief of uniformity.

∑ Key Identities to Know

MLE: \(\hat{P}(w\mid c) = N_{wc}/N_c\)
Laplace (\(\alpha\)): \(\hat{P}(w\mid c) = (N_{wc}+\alpha)/(N_c+\alpha V)\)
Posterior: \(P(\boldsymbol{\theta}_c \mid \text{data}) = \mathrm{Dir}(\mathbf{N}_c + \alpha)\)
Interpolation: \(\hat{P} = \lambda \hat{P}_{\text{MLE}} + (1-\lambda)/V\) where \(\lambda = N_c/(N_c+\alpha V)\)

Step 5 — Naive Bayes with smoothing

The full Naive Bayes classifier with Laplace-smoothed likelihoods:

$$\hat{y} = \arg\max_{c} \left[ \log P(c) + \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log \frac{N_{w_i c} + \alpha}{N_c + \alpha V} \right]$$

Working in log-space avoids floating-point underflow from multiplying many small probabilities. The class priors \(P(c)\) are estimated as \(D_c / D\) where \(D_c\) is the number of training documents in class \(c\).

⚠️ Choosing \(\alpha\)

\(\alpha = 1\) is the classic default, but optimal \(\alpha\) depends on the task. Use held-out validation or cross-validation to tune it. In language modeling, \(\alpha = 0.5\) (Jeffreys prior) or smaller values often work better than \(\alpha=1\) when vocabularies are large.

Naive Bayes with Laplace Smoothing — Python from scratch

import numpy as np
from collections import defaultdict

class NaiveBayes:
    def __init__(self, alpha=1.0):
        self.alpha = alpha    # Laplace smoothing parameter
        self.class_log_prior = {}
        self.word_log_prob = {}
        self.vocab = set()

    def fit(self, X_docs, y):
        """X_docs: list of tokenised docs; y: list of class labels."""
        classes = set(y)
        n = len(y)
        counts = {c: defaultdict(int) for c in classes}
        doc_counts = defaultdict(int)

        for doc, label in zip(X_docs, y):
            doc_counts[label] += 1
            for word in doc:
                counts[label][word] += 1
                self.vocab.add(word)

        V = len(self.vocab)
        for c in classes:
            self.class_log_prior[c] = np.log(doc_counts[c] / n)
            N_c = sum(counts[c].values())
            # Laplace: (count + α) / (N_c + α * V)
            self.word_log_prob[c] = {
                w: np.log((counts[c][w] + self.alpha) / (N_c + self.alpha * V))
                for w in self.vocab
            }

    def predict(self, X_docs):
        preds = []
        for doc in X_docs:
            scores = {}
            for c, log_prior in self.class_log_prior.items():
                score = log_prior
                for word in doc:
                    if word in self.word_log_prob[c]:
                        score += self.word_log_prob[c][word]
                    # unseen words → treated as unseen vocab, skip or add UNK
                scores[c] = score
            preds.append(max(scores, key=scores.get))
        return preds

Interview Questions & Model Answers

Q1 — Why does a single zero count destroy the Naive Bayes posterior?

Naive Bayes multiplies per-word likelihoods \(\prod_i P(w_i \mid c)\). If any one \(P(w_i \mid c) = 0\), the product is zero regardless of all other words. The argmax then picks the other class by default, which may be completely wrong. Even working in log-space, \(\log(0) = -\infty\) dominates the sum.

Q2 — Why must the denominator grow by \(\alpha V\)?

Adding \(\alpha\) to each of the \(V\) numerators adds \(\alpha V\) total pseudo-counts. Probabilities must sum to 1: \(\sum_w \hat{P}(w\mid c) = (N_c + \alpha V)/(N_c + \alpha V) = 1\) only if the denominator is \(N_c + \alpha V\). Omitting the correction is the most common implementation bug.

Q3 — What is the Bayesian interpretation of \(\alpha\)?

Laplace smoothing is the posterior mean of \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_c\) under a symmetric Dirichlet prior \(\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha, \ldots, \alpha)\). The prior acts as \(\alpha\) pseudocounts of each word. MAP would give \((\alpha-1)\) pseudocounts (mode), but the posterior mean \((N_{wc}+\alpha)/(N_c+\alpha V)\) is more commonly used and avoids the numerator going negative when \(\alpha < 1\).

Q4 — How does \(\alpha\) affect the bias-variance trade-off?

Larger \(\alpha\) shrinks all estimates toward \(1/V\), introducing bias but reducing variance (especially useful with sparse data). As \(N_c \to \infty\), the smoothing term vanishes and both MLE and Laplace converge. Optimal \(\alpha\) is found by cross-validation; typical defaults are \(\alpha=1\) (text) or \(\alpha=0.5\) (large vocabulary NLP tasks).

Q5 — When would you choose Laplace vs. Good-Turing smoothing?

Laplace is simple, interpretable, and works well for moderate-sized vocabulary classification (e.g., sentiment, spam). Good-Turing redistributes probability mass from observed events to unseen ones using frequency-of-frequencies statistics — it's statistically more sophisticated and standard in language modeling (n-gram LMs). For small classification tasks, Laplace wins on simplicity; for large-vocabulary LMs, use Kneser-Ney or interpolated smoothing.

Related Topics

  • Naive Bayes Classifier — the primary consumer of Laplace smoothing; assumes feature independence given the class.
  • Multinomial Distribution — the underlying probability model for word counts; Laplace smoothing regularises its MLE.
  • Dirichlet Distribution — the conjugate prior; Laplace smoothing = posterior mean under \(\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha)\).
  • N-gram Language Models — use additive/Kneser-Ney smoothing to handle unseen n-grams.
  • Bayesian Estimation (MAP vs. MLE) — Laplace smoothing bridges the two: MLE at \(\alpha=0\), full Bayesian posterior at any \(\alpha>0\).
  • Regularisation — Laplace smoothing is an \(L_2\)-like regulariser on the simplex; it's the discrete analog of ridge regression.
💡 Take-Aways at a Glance

1. Always smooth discrete probability estimates — MLE zeros are catastrophic in products.
2. \(\hat{P}(w\mid c) = (N_{wc}+\alpha)/(N_c+\alpha V)\) — memorize this, get the denominator right.
3. Bayesian framing: symmetric \(\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha)\) prior → posterior mean.
4. \(\alpha\) interpolates MLE ↔ uniform; tune via cross-validation.
5. For production NLP, use Kneser-Ney instead; Laplace is the right first step to understand.
