Understanding Momentum
▼

What is momentum? Plain gradient descent takes tiny, equal-sized steps in the steepest downhill direction. On a ravine — a loss landscape that's steep in one direction and flat in another — GD zig-zags across the ravine walls and creeps slowly along the valley floor. Momentum fixes this by treating the optimizer like a heavy ball rolling downhill: it builds up speed in consistent directions and slows automatically when it oscillates.

Analogy. Imagine pushing a shopping cart down a long, narrow aisle (the shallow direction) with a bumpy floor (the steep direction). Without momentum you over-correct left and right. With momentum the cart's inertia averages out the wobbles and you glide forward. Nesterov Accelerated Gradient (NAG) is smarter still: instead of braking after overshooting, it looks ahead to where the momentum would carry it and computes the gradient there, so it brakes earlier.

💡 If you remember one thing

Momentum accumulates a velocity vector \(\mathbf{v}_t = \beta\,\mathbf{v}_{t-1} - \eta\,\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_t)\) and updates \(\mathbf{x}_{t+1} = \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{v}_t\). NAG evaluates the gradient at the lookahead point \(\mathbf{x}_t + \beta\,\mathbf{v}_{t-1}\) instead of at \(\mathbf{x}_t\), achieving the optimal \(O(1/t^2)\) convergence rate for smooth convex functions.

Why does it matter? On ill-conditioned problems (large condition number \(\kappa = L/\mu\)), GD converges in \(O(\kappa)\) steps, whereas NAG converges in only \(O(\sqrt{\kappa})\) steps — an exponential improvement in the condition number dependence. For a problem with \(\kappa = 10{,}000\), that's the difference between 10,000 and 100 iterations.

Why It Was Invented
▼

The Problem with Plain GD

By the early 1960s, gradient descent (Cauchy, 1847) was the canonical first-order method. Its convergence on smooth strongly convex functions was known to be \(O(1/t)\) for function values — provably slow for ill-conditioned quadratics. Boris Polyak (1964) observed that physicists already knew how to make rolling balls converge faster: add inertia.

Classical Momentum — Polyak (1964)

Polyak introduced the heavy-ball method. By carrying a fraction \(\beta\) of the previous velocity into the next step, oscillations across the short axis of an elliptical level set cancel while progress along the long axis compounds. On a quadratic with condition number \(\kappa\), the optimal momentum parameter \(\beta^* = \left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}\right)^2\) achieves convergence in \(O(\sqrt{\kappa})\) steps — matching the information-theoretic lower bound for quadratics. However, classical momentum is not guaranteed to achieve this rate on general convex functions.

Nesterov's Breakthrough (1983)

Yurii Nesterov's 1983 paper "A method for solving the convex programming problem with convergence rate O(1/k²)" (Soviet Mathematics Doklady) established that the lower bound for first-order methods on smooth convex functions is \(\Omega(1/t^2)\), and provided an algorithm that matches it: NAG. The key insight was to evaluate the gradient at the extrapolated point (where momentum will carry you) rather than the current point, enabling corrective braking.

Polyak (1964): "Some methods of speeding up the convergence of iteration methods." USSR Computational Mathematics and Mathematical Physics.

Evolution Timeline

Gradient Descent (Cauchy, 1847) → Heavy Ball / Classical Momentum (Polyak, 1964) → Nesterov Accelerated Gradient (Nesterov, 1983) → Adam / AdaGrad / RMSProp (2010s, adaptive variants) → Momentum with restarts, Polyak step size, stochastic NAG variants (2010s–2020s).

The Mathematics
▼

Classical Momentum (Heavy Ball)

Given a differentiable objective \(f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}\), the update rule is:

$$\mathbf{v}_{t+1} = \beta\,\mathbf{v}_t - \eta\,\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_t) \qquad \mathbf{x}_{t+1} = \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{v}_{t+1}$$

where \(\beta \in [0,1)\) is the momentum coefficient and \(\eta > 0\) is the learning rate. At steady state, when \(\nabla f \approx \mathbf{g}\) (constant), the velocity converges to \(\mathbf{v}^* = -\tfrac{\eta}{1-\beta}\,\mathbf{g}\). The factor \(\tfrac{1}{1-\beta}\) is the effective step-size amplification: \(\beta=0.9\) gives a \(10\times\) larger effective step in consistent directions.

Nesterov Accelerated Gradient (NAG)

NAG evaluates the gradient at the lookahead point \(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_t = \mathbf{x}_t + \beta\,\mathbf{v}_t\):

$$\mathbf{v}_{t+1} = \beta\,\mathbf{v}_t - \eta\,\nabla f\!\left(\mathbf{x}_t + \beta\,\mathbf{v}_t\right) \qquad \mathbf{x}_{t+1} = \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{v}_{t+1}$$

Equivalently, defining the iterate sequence \(\mathbf{y}_t\) (the "momentum point"):

$$\mathbf{y}_{t+1} = \mathbf{x}_{t+1} + \frac{t}{t+3}\bigl(\mathbf{x}_{t+1} - \mathbf{x}_t\bigr)$$

This is the momentum-as-extrapolation form used in FISTA and many convex optimization libraries. The sequence of coefficients \(\lambda_t = \frac{t}{t+3}\) increases from 0 toward 1, providing progressively bolder extrapolation as confidence grows.

Convergence Rates

$$f(\mathbf{x}_t) - f^* \leq \begin{cases} \dfrac{L\|\mathbf{x}_0 - \mathbf{x}^*\|^2}{2t} & \text{GD (smooth convex)} \\[8pt] \left(1 - \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\right)^t \!\cdot\! (f(\mathbf{x}_0) - f^*) & \text{NAG (strongly convex)} \\[8pt] \dfrac{2L\|\mathbf{x}_0 - \mathbf{x}^*\|^2}{(t+1)^2} & \text{NAG (smooth convex)} \end{cases}$$

For GD on smooth strongly convex functions (condition number \(\kappa = L/\mu\)), the rate is \(\left(1 - \frac{2}{\kappa+1}\right)^t \approx e^{-2t/\kappa}\). For NAG the rate is \(\left(1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\right)^t \approx e^{-t/\sqrt{\kappa}}\). The ratio of iteration counts to reach \(\varepsilon\)-accuracy is \(\sqrt{\kappa}\) — NAG needs \(\sqrt{\kappa}\) fewer iterations.

Why the Lookahead Gradient Works — Proof Sketch

The core idea is a quadratic upper bound. Smoothness of \(f\) with constant \(L\) gives:

$$f(\mathbf{y}) \leq f(\mathbf{x}) + \langle \nabla f(\mathbf{x}),\, \mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x} \rangle + \tfrac{L}{2}\|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|^2$$

Classical momentum uses \(\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_t)\) to build the next iterate, then adds momentum post-hoc. NAG applies momentum before the gradient evaluation, so the gradient captures the curvature at the extrapolated point — the point the ball will actually reach. The Lyapunov function \(\Phi_t = t^2(f(\mathbf{x}_t) - f^*) + 2L\|\mathbf{z}_t - \mathbf{x}^*\|^2\) (for an auxiliary sequence \(\mathbf{z}_t\)) is shown to be non-increasing, yielding the \(O(1/t^2)\) bound.

⚠️ Common Misconception

Classical momentum achieves optimal convergence on quadratics but is not provably accelerated on general convex functions. Only NAG carries the rigorous \(O(1/t^2)\) guarantee. In practice on deep networks (non-convex, stochastic), the difference is less clear-cut; PyTorch's SGD(momentum=0.9) uses the classical form and remains the default.

Stability Condition

For a \(L\)-smooth function, momentum and NAG are stable (convergent) when \(\eta < \tfrac{2(1-\beta)}{L}\). Note this is more restrictive than GD's \(\eta < \tfrac{2}{L}\) by a factor of \((1-\beta)\). With \(\beta=0.9\), the stable learning rate is \(10\times\) smaller — compensated by the \(\tfrac{1}{1-\beta}\) effective step amplification.

From Scratch — NumPy

import numpy as np def gradient_descent(grad_f, x0, lr=0.01, n_steps=200): x = np.array(x0, dtype=float) trace = [x.copy()] for _ in range(n_steps): x = x - lr * grad_f(x) # bare gradient step trace.append(x.copy()) return np.array(trace) def classical_momentum(grad_f, x0, lr=0.01, beta=0.9, n_steps=200): x = np.array(x0, dtype=float) v = np.zeros_like(x) # velocity initialised to 0 trace = [x.copy()] for _ in range(n_steps): v = beta * v - lr * grad_f(x) # accumulate velocity x = x + v # apply velocity trace.append(x.copy()) return np.array(trace) def nesterov_ag(grad_f, x0, lr=0.01, beta=0.9, n_steps=200): x = np.array(x0, dtype=float) v = np.zeros_like(x) trace = [x.copy()] for _ in range(n_steps): x_lookahead = x + beta * v # extrapolate with current velocity v = beta * v - lr * grad_f(x_lookahead)# gradient at the lookahead point x = x + v trace.append(x.copy()) return np.array(trace) # ── Demo on ill-conditioned quadratic f(x) = 0.5*(0.5*x₀² + 50*x₁²) ───────── grad = lambda x: np.array([0.5*x[0], 50*x[1]]) f = lambda x: 0.5*(0.5*x[0]**2 + 50*x[1]**2) x0 = np.array([2.0, 0.5]) tr_gd = gradient_descent(grad, x0, lr=0.003) tr_mom = classical_momentum(grad, x0, lr=0.003, beta=0.85) tr_nag = nesterov_ag(grad, x0, lr=0.003, beta=0.85) for name, tr in [("GD", tr_gd), ("Mom", tr_mom), ("NAG", tr_nag)]: print(f"{name} at step 80: f = {f(tr[80]):.5f}") # GD at step 80: f = 0.78649 # Mom at step 80: f = 0.20655 # NAG at step 80: f = 0.20940

Production Usage — PyTorch

import torch, torch.nn as nn model = nn.Linear(784, 10) # Classical Momentum (Polyak heavy ball) opt_mom = torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=0.01, momentum=0.9, # beta: typically 0.85–0.99 nesterov=False) # classical Polyak form # Nesterov Accelerated Gradient opt_nag = torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=0.01, momentum=0.9, nesterov=True) # evaluates gradient at x + beta*v # ⚠️ Gotcha: nesterov=True REQUIRES momentum > 0 and dampening == 0 # ⚠️ PyTorch uses the "Sutskever form" of NAG (equivalent, different algebra) # v = beta*v + g(x); x = x - lr*(beta*v + g(x)) [not the standard form above] # With learning rate schedule (cosine annealing) scheduler = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingLR(opt_nag, T_max=100) for epoch in range(100): train_one_epoch(model, opt_nag) scheduler.step() # Key hyperparameter choices: # momentum = 0.9 (safe default, used in ResNet, VGG papers) # momentum = 0.99 (aggressive; can oscillate if lr too large) # nesterov = True improves convergence speed on convex problems; # on non-convex deep nets the gain is problem-dependent
⚠️ PyTorch Implementation Detail

PyTorch's SGD with nesterov=True uses the Sutskever et al. 2013 reformulation: it absorbs the gradient into the velocity buffer and uses a different but mathematically equivalent form. This means the learning rate semantics differ slightly from the standard \(\eta\) in textbook NAG. When porting hyperparameters between frameworks, verify which NAG form is used.

Interview Q&A

Q1 (Easy): What does momentum do to gradient descent?

A: It adds a velocity term that accumulates exponentially weighted past gradients. In consistent directions the velocity grows (amplifying progress); in oscillating directions the positive and negative gradients cancel (damping zig-zags).

Q2 (Medium): How is NAG different from classical momentum?

A: NAG evaluates the gradient at the lookahead point \(\mathbf{x}_t + \beta\mathbf{v}_t\) rather than at \(\mathbf{x}_t\). This means the gradient "sees" where momentum is about to take the iterate, enabling corrective braking before overshooting. The result is provably \(O(1/t^2)\) convergence on smooth convex functions — the information-theoretic optimal rate for first-order methods.

Q3 (Medium): What is the effective step size with momentum?

A: In steady state with constant gradient \(\mathbf{g}\), velocity converges to \(\mathbf{v}^* = -\frac{\eta}{1-\beta}\mathbf{g}\). So the effective step size is \(\frac{\eta}{1-\beta}\). With \(\beta=0.9\) and \(\eta=0.01\), the effective step is \(0.1\) — 10× the nominal learning rate. This is why momentum users typically use smaller \(\eta\) than bare GD.

Q4 (Hard): Why does classical momentum NOT achieve O(1/t²) on general convex functions?

A: The heavy-ball method uses a fixed momentum coefficient \(\beta\) throughout optimization. Nesterov's analysis shows that to achieve optimal rates, the effective extrapolation coefficient must increase over time as \(\lambda_t = (t-1)/(t+2)\). Classical momentum with fixed \(\beta\) can be shown to diverge (or converge at sub-optimal rates) on certain non-quadratic convex problems. The adaptive coefficient in NAG provides the necessary "increasing boldness" as the iterate approaches the optimum.

Q5 (Hard): What is momentum's effect on the condition number?

A: GD convergence on a strongly convex quadratic with eigenvalues \([l, L]\) is governed by the ratio \(\rho_{GD} = \frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}\) where \(\kappa = L/l\). With optimal momentum \(\beta^* = \left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}\right)^2\), the convergence ratio becomes \(\rho_{Mom} = \frac{\sqrt{\kappa}-1}{\sqrt{\kappa}+1}\), which equals \(\sqrt{\rho_{GD}}\) — the square root of GD's ratio, meaning momentum requires only \(\sqrt{\kappa}\) times as many iterations as GD.

🎓 System Design Angle

In large-scale distributed training (data-parallel SGD), momentum is almost always used because: (1) it reduces variance across noisy mini-batches, (2) with learning rate warmup + cosine decay, NAG/momentum reliably trains ResNets and ViTs to SOTA. Gradient accumulation (simulating large batches) interacts non-trivially with momentum — the effective \(\beta\) changes with accumulation steps, so frameworks like PyTorch Lightning zero the velocity buffer across accumulation boundaries.

Red flags in interviews:

  • Saying classical momentum is provably O(1/t²) — it's not on general convex functions
  • Confusing the lookahead point with Adam's first moment — they are unrelated
  • Claiming momentum always helps — on strongly non-convex landscapes it can overshoot saddle points and oscillate
  • Not knowing that PyTorch's nesterov=True uses a different algebraic form than the textbook formula
