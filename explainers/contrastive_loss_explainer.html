Understanding Contrastive Loss
▼

What is it? SimCLR learns image representations without any labels by teaching a network a single rule: two different crops/augmentations of the same photo should map to nearby points in embedding space, while crops from different photos should map far apart. No human ever says "this is a dog" — the supervision signal comes entirely from data augmentation and the batch itself.

Analogy: Imagine a police lineup where you're shown one "suspect" photo and a room full of people, told that exactly one person in the room is the same person from a different angle. Your job is to pick them out among all the decoys. Contrastive loss trains the network to always win this lineup — the correct match should stand out clearly from every other candidate (the "negatives") in the room (the "batch").

Summary: Two augmented views of one image form a positive pair; every other image in the mini-batch (and its augmentations) become negatives. The NT-Xent (Normalized Temperature-scaled Cross Entropy) loss is InfoNCE applied to cosine similarities of learned embeddings.

💡 If you remember one thing

Contrastive loss is just softmax classification in disguise — the "correct class" is the positive pair, the "wrong classes" are every other embedding in the batch: $$\mathcal{L}_{i,j} = -\log \frac{\exp(\operatorname{sim}(z_i, z_j)/\tau)}{\sum_{k=1}^{2N} \mathbb{1}_{[k \neq i]} \exp(\operatorname{sim}(z_i, z_k)/\tau)}$$

How NT-Xent Works Internally
▼

The Pipeline

1) Sample a minibatch of \(N\) images. 2) Apply two independent random augmentations (crop, color jitter, blur, flip) to each image, producing \(2N\) views. 3) Pass every view through a shared encoder \(f(\cdot)\) (e.g. a ResNet) to get representations \(h\). 4) Pass \(h\) through a small projection head \(g(\cdot)\) — typically a 2-layer MLP — to get \(z = g(h)\), the vector the loss actually operates on. 5) L2-normalize every \(z\) onto the unit hypersphere. 6) Compute cosine similarity between all \(\binom{2N}{2}\) pairs and apply NT-Xent.

Why a Projection Head?

The SimCLR authors found that computing the contrastive loss on \(z = g(h)\) rather than directly on \(h\) significantly improves the quality of \(h\) for downstream tasks. Intuition: the projection head absorbs information that is useful for the augmentation-invariance task but would otherwise distort the encoder's representation (e.g. color/rotation information gets thrown away by \(g\), but the encoder \(h\) is still free to retain it). After pretraining, \(g\) is discarded and only \(f\) is kept for fine-tuning.

Why In-Batch Negatives?

SimCLR needs no memory bank or momentum encoder (unlike MoCo) — every other example already in the mini-batch is reused for free as a negative. This is simple but means quality scales with batch size: at \(N{=}4096\), each anchor sees 8190 negatives, making the "lineup" hard and the learned representations very discriminative. Small batches make the lineup too easy, weakening the signal.

Design Decision: Cosine Similarity + Temperature, Not Raw Dot Product

Using cosine similarity (dot product of L2-normalized vectors) bounds similarities to \([-1, 1]\), preventing embeddings from "cheating" by inflating their norm to trivially raise dot-product scores. The temperature \(\tau\) then rescales this bounded range before the softmax — without it, all pairs would look nearly equally similar and gradients would be too weak to separate hard negatives.

🔑 Key Property

NT-Xent is symmetric: the loss is computed once with \(z_i\) as anchor and \(z_j\) as positive, and once with the roles reversed, then averaged over all \(2N\) views. Every embedding takes a turn being the anchor in one term and a negative in \(2N-2\) other terms.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Cosine Similarity

For two projected, L2-normalized embeddings \(z_i, z_j \in \mathbb{R}^d\) with \(\|z_i\|=\|z_j\|=1\):

$$\operatorname{sim}(z_i, z_j) = \frac{z_i^\top z_j}{\|z_i\|\|z_j\|} = z_i^\top z_j \in [-1, 1]$$

The NT-Xent / InfoNCE Loss

For a positive pair \((i,j)\) drawn from a batch of \(2N\) augmented views, the loss treats "find \(j\) among \(2N-1\) candidates" as a \((2N-1)\)-way classification problem, solved with cross-entropy over a softmax of scaled similarities:

$$\ell(i,j) = -\log \frac{\exp(\operatorname{sim}(z_i,z_j)/\tau)}{\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{2N}\mathbb{1}_{[k\neq i]}\exp(\operatorname{sim}(z_i,z_k)/\tau)}$$

The full batch loss averages this over both orderings of every pair:

$$\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\big[\ell(2k{-}1, 2k) + \ell(2k, 2k{-}1)\big]$$

Gradient Intuition

Let \(p_k = \dfrac{\exp(\operatorname{sim}(z_i,z_k)/\tau)}{\sum_{m\neq i}\exp(\operatorname{sim}(z_i,z_m)/\tau)}\) be the softmax probability assigned to candidate \(k\). Differentiating \(\ell(i,j)\) with respect to \(\operatorname{sim}(z_i,z_k)\) gives \(\frac{1}{\tau}(p_k - \mathbb{1}_{[k=j]})\). For the positive (\(k=j\)) the gradient is negative — it increases the similarity; for every negative it is positive — it decreases similarity. This is exactly "pull the positive in, push everything else out," and the \(1/\tau\) factor amplifies gradients for hard negatives (those with similarity close to the positive's).

⚠️ Common Misconception

Lower temperature is not simply "better." As \(\tau \to 0\), the softmax approaches a hard argmax and gradients concentrate almost entirely on the single hardest negative — this can destabilize training. As \(\tau \to \infty\), the distribution flattens toward uniform and the gradient signal vanishes. SimCLR uses \(\tau \approx 0.1\)–\(0.5\) as a sweet spot found empirically.

Key Takeaways
▼

When it shines:

  • Pretraining visual (or any) encoders when labels are scarce or expensive
  • Large-batch / multi-GPU setups — negatives are "free," but quality scales with batch size
  • Domains with strong, label-preserving augmentations (natural images: crop, jitter, blur)

When to be careful:

  • Small batches starve the loss of hard negatives — consider MoCo's momentum queue instead
  • Augmentations that change semantics (e.g. aggressive color jitter on medical scans) teach the wrong invariance
  • Poor temperature choice can collapse training — always tune \(\tau\) as a first-class hyperparameter
💡 Family Tree

NT-Xent (SimCLR) generalizes the earlier triplet loss (1 positive, 1 negative) to N-pair / InfoNCE (1 positive, many negatives), and is itself a special case used by CLIP (image-text pairs), MoCo (queue-based negatives), and SupCon (multiple positives per anchor via labels).

Code Trace
▼
import torch, torch.nn.functional as F
# z: (2N, d) projected, L2-normalized embeddings.
# Rows [0..N-1] = view A, rows [N..2N-1] = view B, paired by index.
def nt_xent(z, tau=0.5):
n2 = z.shape[0] # 2N
z = F.normalize(z, dim=1) # cosine sim = dot product
sim = z @ z.T / tau # (2N, 2N) scaled similarities
mask = torch.eye(n2, dtype=torch.bool)
sim.masked_fill_(mask, float('-inf')) # exclude self-similarity
n = n2 // 2
pos_idx = torch.arange(n2)
pos_idx = (pos_idx + n) % n2 # index of each row's positive
# cross-entropy: "correct class" = the positive's column index
loss = F.cross_entropy(sim, pos_idx)
return loss
# --- production usage (PyTorch Lightning style) ---
z_a = projection_head(encoder(view_a)) # (N, d)
z_b = projection_head(encoder(view_b)) # (N, d)
loss = nt_xent(torch.cat([z_a, z_b], dim=0), tau=0.1)
loss.backward()
# Gotcha: forgetting to mask self-similarity makes the trivial
# "match yourself" solution always win — loss collapses to ~0
# while learning nothing useful.
