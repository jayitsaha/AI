Local vs Global Optima

Convex vs Non-Convex Loss Landscapes — Optimization Theory

1
Understanding the Concept
▼

When training a machine learning model, we minimize a loss function — a mathematical measure of how wrong the model's predictions are. The loss function defines a landscape over the space of model parameters. The goal of optimization is to find the lowest point in that landscape.

A global minimum is the absolute lowest point in the entire landscape — the parameter setting that minimizes loss best of all. A local minimum is a valley that is lower than all its immediate neighbors, but may not be the deepest valley overall. The critical difference between convex and non-convex problems is whether local minima can trap you away from the global minimum.

Real-World Analogy

Convex setting: Imagine a bowl — no matter where you place a marble, it rolls to the same bottom. That bottom is the unique global minimum. Non-convex setting: Imagine a mountain range with many valleys. A marble dropped at different points gets stuck in different valleys. Deep learning loss surfaces are like mountain ranges — but it turns out most valleys are nearly the same depth, so even "local" solutions work well.

If You Remember One Thing

In a convex function, every local minimum IS the global minimum — gradient descent is guaranteed to converge to the best solution. In non-convex functions (like deep neural network losses), local minima, saddle points, and plateaus abound — but modern empirical evidence suggests most local minima in deep networks are nearly as good as the global minimum, making them surprisingly tractable.

Convexity, Saddle Points & Second-Order Conditions
▼

Convex Functions — Formal Definition

A function \(f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}\) is convex if for all \(x, y \in \mathbb{R}^n\) and all \(\lambda \in [0,1]\):

$$f(\lambda x + (1-\lambda)y) \;\leq\; \lambda f(x) + (1-\lambda)f(y)$$

Geometrically: the chord between any two points on the graph lies above the graph. Equivalently (for twice-differentiable \(f\)), \(f\) is convex if and only if its Hessian is positive semi-definite everywhere:

$$\nabla^2 f(x) \succeq 0 \quad \forall x$$

Local vs Global Minima — Precise Definitions

A point \(x^*\) is a local minimum if there exists \(\delta > 0\) such that \(f(x^*) \leq f(x)\) for all \(x\) with \(\|x - x^*\| < \delta\). It is a global minimum if \(f(x^*) \leq f(x)\) for all \(x \in \mathbb{R}^n\).

The Fundamental Theorem of Convex Optimization

If \(f\) is convex, then every local minimum is a global minimum. Proof sketch: suppose \(x^*\) is a local min but not global. Then \(\exists y\) with \(f(y) < f(x^*)\). By convexity, \(f(\lambda y + (1-\lambda)x^*) \leq \lambda f(y) + (1-\lambda)f(x^*) < f(x^*)\) for any \(\lambda \in (0,1)\). But for small enough \(\lambda\), \(\lambda y + (1-\lambda)x^*\) is in the \(\delta\)-neighbourhood of \(x^*\), contradicting the local minimum assumption. \(\square\)

Critical Points: Minima, Maxima, and Saddle Points

Any point where \(\nabla f(x) = 0\) is called a critical point. The Hessian \(\nabla^2 f\) classifies them:

  • Local minimum: \(\nabla^2 f \succ 0\) (all eigenvalues positive)
  • Local maximum: \(\nabla^2 f \prec 0\) (all eigenvalues negative)
  • Saddle point: \(\nabla^2 f\) has both positive and negative eigenvalues — the function curves up in some directions and down in others

Why Saddle Points Dominate in High Dimensions

In an \(n\)-dimensional parameter space, a critical point is a local minimum only if all \(n\) Hessian eigenvalues are positive. Assuming each eigenvalue is independently ±1 with equal probability, the probability of a local minimum is \(2^{-n}\). For a network with millions of parameters, local minima are exponentially rare — saddle points dominate. This is the Dauphin et al. (2014) observation that motivated second-order methods and saddle-point-escaping optimizers.

Common Misconception

Many practitioners worry about getting stuck in local minima when training deep networks. Empirical studies (Goodfellow et al. 2015, Draxler et al. 2018) suggest the real obstacle is saddle points and plateaus — regions where \(\|\nabla f\|\) is near zero but the loss is not at a minimum. SGD's noise actually helps escape saddle points, which is one reason mini-batch SGD outperforms full-batch gradient descent in practice.

Why Deep Networks Are Non-Convex Yet Trainable
▼

Neural network loss functions are highly non-convex — the composition of nonlinear activation functions with matrix weights produces a landscape with exponentially many critical points. Yet, in practice, SGD reliably trains networks to solutions with excellent generalization. Why?

The Near-Equivalence of Local Minima

The key insight, formalized by Choromanska et al. (2015) and empirically confirmed by many subsequent works: in overparameterized deep networks, the vast majority of local minima have loss values very close to the global minimum. The "bad" local minima (with high training loss) are concentrated near the surface of parameter space, while the "good" ones cluster near the bottom. Since SGD starts from a random point and descends, it tends to fall into a good local minimum.

The Overparameterization Insight

When a network has many more parameters than training examples (overparameterized regime), the loss surface has so many degrees of freedom that the global minimum is essentially a manifold — a connected surface of equally good solutions. SGD finds a point on this manifold, and the particular point it finds often generalizes well due to implicit regularization.

Loss Surface Geometry: Plateaus, Saddles, and Valleys

The deep network loss landscape has three problematic regions:

  • Plateaus: Regions where \(\|\nabla L\| \approx 0\) and loss is high. Slow training. Fixed by careful initialization and batch normalization.
  • Saddle points: \(\nabla L = 0\) with mixed-sign Hessian. Gradient descent stalls. SGD noise helps escape. Adam's per-parameter momentum is particularly effective.
  • Sharp vs flat minima: Some local minima have a narrow, sharp basin (high curvature); others are wide and flat. Flat minima generalize better — small parameter perturbations don't increase loss much. SGD with large batch noise tends to find flatter minima (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber 1997; Keskar et al. 2017).

Mode Connectivity

A striking empirical finding (Garipov et al. 2018, Draxler et al. 2018): the local minima found by SGD from different random initializations can often be connected by low-loss paths through parameter space — the loss doesn't increase significantly along the path between two minima. This suggests the "minima" are actually part of a connected manifold, not isolated valleys.

Why Deep Networks Are Trainable (Summary)

1. Most local minima are near-equivalent in loss value (Choromanska 2015). 2. Saddle points (not local minima) are the main obstruction, and SGD noise helps escape them. 3. Overparameterization creates a manifold of global solutions. 4. Implicit regularization of SGD finds flat minima that generalize well. 5. Mode connectivity confirms solutions are in a connected, approachable region.

The Math Behind Convexity & Optima
▼

Convexity and the Global Minimum Guarantee

A differentiable function \(f\) is convex iff for any \(x, y\):

$$f(y) \;\geq\; f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top (y - x)$$

This is the first-order characterization: the tangent hyperplane at any point lies below the function. For twice-differentiable functions, the equivalent second-order condition is:

$$\nabla^2 f(x) \succeq 0 \quad \forall x \in \mathbb{R}^n$$

Convergence Rate for Strongly Convex Functions

A function \(f\) is strongly convex with parameter \(\mu > 0\) if:

$$f(y) \;\geq\; f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top(y-x) + \frac{\mu}{2}\|y-x\|^2$$

If additionally \(\nabla f\) is \(L\)-Lipschitz continuous (\(\|\nabla^2 f\| \leq L\)), then gradient descent with step size \(\eta = 1/L\) achieves linear convergence:

$$f(x_t) - f^* \;\leq\; \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\right)^t \bigl(f(x_0) - f^*\bigr)$$

The quantity \(\kappa = L/\mu\) is the condition number. For \(f(x) = x^2\): \(\mu = L = 2\), \(\kappa = 1\) — perfectly conditioned, one-step convergence in theory.

Critical Points of Non-Convex Functions

For the non-convex example \(f(x) = x^4 - 3x^2 + 2\):

$$f'(x) = 4x^3 - 6x = 2x(2x^2 - 3) = 0 \implies x \in \left\{0,\; \pm\sqrt{\tfrac{3}{2}}\right\}$$

Second derivative test:

$$f''(x) = 12x^2 - 6 \implies \begin{cases} f''(0) = -6 < 0 & \text{(saddle)} \\ f''(\pm\sqrt{3/2}) = 12 \cdot \tfrac{3}{2} - 6 = 12 > 0 & \text{(local minima)} \end{cases}$$

Both local minima achieve \(f(\pm\sqrt{3/2}) = \frac{9}{4} - \frac{9}{2} + 2 = -\frac{1}{4}\). They are both global minima of this function (equal values), illustrating that non-convex functions can have multiple global minima.

Gradient Descent on Non-Convex Functions

Gradient descent on non-convex \(f\) with \(L\)-Lipschitz gradient and learning rate \(\eta \leq 1/L\) guarantees convergence to a critical point (not necessarily a minimum): \(\min_{t \leq T} \|\nabla f(x_t)\|^2 \leq \frac{2(f(x_0)-f^*)}{\eta T}\). This is an \(O(1/T)\) rate — much slower than the exponential rate for convex functions.

Saddle Points Dominate in High Dimensions

For a random symmetric matrix of size \(n \times n\), the probability that all eigenvalues are positive (i.e., the critical point is a local min) is \(2^{-n}\) under a simple Bernoulli model. In a network with \(n = 10^6\) parameters, local minima are essentially non-existent — almost all critical points are saddle points with some negative curvature directions. First-order methods escape saddles slowly (polynomial time); second-order and noise-perturbed methods can escape in \(O(1/\epsilon)\) steps.

Math to Double-Check

The \(2^{-n}\) probability argument assumes independent ±1 eigenvalue signs with equal probability — a simplification. The actual GOE (Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble) analysis by Dauphin et al. (2014) uses random matrix theory and shows that the expected fraction of negative eigenvalues at a critical point scales with the loss value: critical points with low loss have nearly all positive eigenvalues (near-minima), while high-loss critical points are saddles. The simple \(2^{-n}\) model conveys the exponential suppression of local minima correctly.

Decision Guide

Use convex methods (guaranteed convergence, no restarts needed) when your loss is convex: linear models, SVMs, logistic regression, LASSO, ridge regression. Use SGD with restarts + momentum for non-convex neural network losses — the landscape, while non-convex, has the "lottery ticket" property and mode connectivity making it benign. For critical applications, run multiple random initializations and pick the best.
