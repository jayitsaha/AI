OLS Regression Assumptions

The five Gauss-Markov conditions — satisfied vs violated, and the consequence of each
Foundational Classical ML Statistics Gauss-Markov Theorem
1
The 30-Second Version
▼

What is this? Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) is the workhorse regression method. It works beautifully — producing the best possible estimates — but only when five conditions about the data are satisfied. These conditions are the "fine print" of the regression contract. Violate them and your estimates may be biased, inefficient, or your conclusions may be statistically wrong.

Analogy: Think of OLS like a GPS navigation app. The app gives optimal routes under normal conditions (clear roads, correct maps). But if the roads are closed (violated assumption), the app might route you in circles or give you wildly incorrect arrival times. The assumptions are the "normal conditions" the app was designed for.

💡 If you remember one thing

The five OLS assumptions are (1) Linearity: the true model is linear in \(\beta\); (2) Independence: errors \(\varepsilon_i\) are uncorrelated across observations; (3) Homoscedasticity: \(\text{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma^2\) (constant); (4) Normality: \(\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)\); (5) No Multicollinearity: columns of \(X\) are not perfectly collinear. Only when all five hold is OLS the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE).

🔑 The Gauss-Markov Theorem

Under assumptions 1–3 and 5 (linearity, independence, homoscedasticity, no multicollinearity), OLS is BLUE — the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator. This means among all linear unbiased estimators, OLS has the smallest variance. Normality (assumption 4) is additionally required for valid finite-sample inference (t-tests, F-tests).

Historical Context & Core Theory
▼

The Origins

Carl Friedrich Gauss developed the method of least squares in 1795 (published 1809) to predict the orbit of the asteroid Ceres. Adrien-Marie Legendre independently published it in 1805. The formal theorem guaranteeing OLS optimality — named after Gauss and Andrey Markov — was proved rigorously in 1900. The normality assumption was added later to enable exact hypothesis testing (Student's t-distribution, 1908; F-distribution, 1920s).

The Five Assumptions Precisely

The linear model is \(y = X\beta + \varepsilon\) where \(y \in \mathbb{R}^n\), \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}\), \(\beta \in \mathbb{R}^p\), \(\varepsilon \in \mathbb{R}^n\).

#AssumptionFormal StatementWhat Breaks If Violated
A1 Linearity \(\mathbb{E}[y \mid X] = X\beta\) — the conditional mean is linear Biased estimates; model is misspecified
A2 Independence \(\text{Cov}(\varepsilon_i, \varepsilon_j) = 0\) for \(i \neq j\) Standard errors are wrong; t/F tests invalid
A3 Homoscedasticity \(\text{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma^2\) (constant for all \(i\)) OLS is inefficient; SEs wrong; CIs too wide/narrow
A4 Normality \(\varepsilon_i \stackrel{iid}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\) Small-sample t/F tests invalid (CLT saves in large \(n\))
A5 No Multicollinearity \(\text{rank}(X) = p\) — columns are linearly independent Estimates are non-unique; numerically unstable; huge SE
⚠️ Severity Hierarchy

Not all violations are equally serious. Linearity (A1) and perfect multicollinearity (A5) cause fundamental estimation failure. Independence (A2) and homoscedasticity (A3) corrupt inference but not point estimates (OLS remains unbiased). Normality (A4) matters mainly for small samples — the Central Limit Theorem rescues large-sample inference asymptotically.

The Math — Derivation & Violations
▼

OLS Estimator

OLS minimizes the residual sum of squares:

$$\hat{\beta}_{OLS} = \underset{\beta}{\operatorname{argmin}} \|y - X\beta\|_2^2 = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y$$

Its distribution under A1–A5: \(\hat{\beta} \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\beta,\, \sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\right)\), where the covariance achieves the Cramér-Rao lower bound.

What Happens Under Each Violation

A1 Violated (Nonlinearity): The true model is \(y = f(X) + \varepsilon\) where \(f\) is nonlinear, but we fit \(\hat{y} = X\hat{\beta}\). Then \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}] \neq \beta\) — OLS is biased. Residuals form a systematic curved pattern around zero.

$$\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}] = \beta + (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbb{E}[f(X) - X\beta] \neq \beta$$

A2 Violated (Autocorrelation): Errors follow \(\varepsilon_t = \rho\varepsilon_{t-1} + u_t\) with \(|\rho| < 1\). OLS is still unbiased but the covariance is no longer \(\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\) — it is:

$$\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}_{OLS}) = \sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1}\left(X^\top \Omega_\rho X\right)(X^\top X)^{-1}$$

where \(\Omega_\rho\) is the Toeplitz autocorrelation matrix. Using the wrong SE inflates Type I error.

A3 Violated (Heteroscedasticity): \(\text{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma_i^2\). OLS estimates are unbiased but inefficient — WLS or GLS has smaller variance. The Breusch-Pagan and White tests detect this. Residual plots show a "funnel" or "fan" shape.

A4 Violated (Non-normality): OLS point estimates are still BLUE (Gauss-Markov does not require normality), but the exact t and F distributions no longer apply. With \(n \gtrsim 30\), CLT provides approximate normality. Highly skewed or heavy-tailed residuals in small samples invalidate confidence intervals.

A5 Violated (Multicollinearity): When columns of \(X\) are nearly collinear, \(X^\top X\) is nearly singular. VIF (Variance Inflation Factor) quantifies this:

$$\text{VIF}_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2}$$

where \(R_j^2\) is the \(R^2\) from regressing \(X_j\) on all other predictors. VIF \(> 10\) signals serious collinearity. The condition number \(\kappa(X^\top X) = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\) is another diagnostic.

📐 Key Formula Summary

OLS: \(\hat{\beta} = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y\)  |  Var under A1–A5: \(\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\)  |  VIF: \((1-R_j^2)^{-1}\)  |  Durbin-Watson (autocorrelation): \(d = \frac{\sum_{t=2}^n(e_t - e_{t-1})^2}{\sum_{t=1}^n e_t^2}\)

Diagnostics & Remedies
▼
AssumptionDiagnostic Test / PlotRemedy
A1 Linearity Residuals vs \(\hat{y}\) plot (look for curves); RESET test; partial regression plots Add polynomial terms, log transform, splines, or switch to nonlinear model
A2 Independence Durbin-Watson test; ACF/PACF of residuals; Ljung-Box test Use GLS / FGLS with AR errors; HAC (Newey-West) robust SEs; add lagged variables
A3 Homoscedasticity Residuals vs \(\hat{y}\) plot (funnel shape); Breusch-Pagan; White test WLS with \(w_i = 1/\sigma_i^2\); log-transform \(y\); GLS; robust (HC) standard errors
A4 Normality Q-Q plot of residuals; Shapiro-Wilk; histogram of residuals Transform \(y\) (Box-Cox); large-sample CLT; bootstrap CIs; robust regression
A5 No Multicollin. VIF \(> 10\); correlation matrix; condition number \(\kappa > 30\) Drop or combine collinear predictors; PCA; Ridge regression (\(\ell_2\) regularization)
💡 Practical Pecking Order

Always check A1 (linearity) first via residual plots — if the model is misspecified, all other diagnostics are meaningless. Then check A5 (VIF) before fitting. A2 and A3 violations are best addressed with robust standard errors if sample is large. A4 (normality) is the least critical in practice — central limit theorem handles it for \(n \geq 30\).

Interview Deep-Dive
▼

Q1 (Easy): What are the OLS assumptions?

Linearity in parameters, independence of errors, constant error variance (homoscedasticity), normally distributed errors, and no perfect multicollinearity. Under assumptions 1–3 and 5, Gauss-Markov guarantees OLS is BLUE. Normality is additionally needed for exact finite-sample inference.

Q2 (Medium): OLS is unbiased if the model is misspecified — true or false?

False. If the true model is \(y = x^2 + \varepsilon\) but you fit \(y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \varepsilon\), OLS is biased — \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\beta}] \neq \beta\). OLS is only unbiased when the true conditional mean \(\mathbb{E}[y|X] = X\beta\) (Assumption A1).

Q3 (Medium): What happens to OLS under heteroscedasticity?

OLS point estimates remain unbiased and consistent (because \(\mathbb{E}[X^\top \varepsilon] = 0\) still), but they are no longer efficient — WLS has smaller variance. More critically, the OLS standard errors are computed assuming \(\text{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2 I\), which is wrong when heteroscedasticity exists, making all t-tests and F-tests invalid. Fix: use HC (heteroscedasticity-consistent) robust standard errors, e.g., White's HC3 estimator.

Q4 (Hard): How does multicollinearity affect OLS and how does Ridge regression help?

When predictors are collinear, \(X^\top X\) approaches singularity and its condition number explodes. This means \((X^\top X)^{-1}\) has huge entries, so \(\text{Var}(\hat{\beta}) = \sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\) is enormous — tiny changes in data produce wildly different coefficient estimates. Ridge regression adds a penalty: \(\hat{\beta}_{ridge} = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1} X^\top y\), which regularizes \(X^\top X\) away from singularity. This introduces a small bias but drastically reduces variance (bias-variance tradeoff), and the condition number of \(X^\top X + \lambda I\) is bounded by \(\kappa_{ridge} \leq (\lambda_{\max} + \lambda)/\lambda\).

Q5 (Hard): Why does autocorrelation (A2 violation) produce wrong p-values even though OLS is still unbiased?

The OLS variance formula \(\hat{\sigma}^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\) assumes \(\text{Cov}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2 I\). With AR(1) errors, the true covariance is \(\sigma^2\Omega_\rho\) where \(\Omega_\rho\) is a Toeplitz matrix with off-diagonal entries \(\rho^{|i-j|}\). The true variance of \(\hat{\beta}\) involves the sandwich \((X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\Omega_\rho X(X^\top X)^{-1}\). When \(\rho > 0\), consecutive errors point the same direction — the effective sample size is much smaller than \(n\), so OLS underestimates SEs, artificially inflating t-statistics. The fix is Newey-West (HAC) standard errors or explicitly modeling the autocorrelation via GLS.
