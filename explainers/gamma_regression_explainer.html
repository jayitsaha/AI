Gamma Regression

GLM for positive, right-skewed continuous responses — insurance claims, medical costs, survival durations
Intermediate Classical ML · GLM Supervised Learning · Regression Variance ∝ μ²
1
Understanding Gamma Regression
▾

What is it? Many real-world quantities — insurance claims, hospital costs, loan durations, manufacturing defect sizes — are always positive and have a long right tail: most values are small, but a few are very large. Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) fails here because it can predict negative values and assumes errors have constant spread regardless of the mean. Gamma Regression fixes this by modelling \(y > 0\) with a distribution that is naturally right-skewed and whose variance grows quadratically with the mean.

Real-world analogy: Think of car insurance claims. Most drivers file small claims; some have catastrophic accidents costing fifty times the average. OLS draws a straight line — it might predict a negative claim for low-risk drivers. Gamma Regression fits a curved mean function that can never go below zero and automatically allows bigger variability around large claims than small ones, reflecting how monetary risk actually scales.

💡 If you remember one thing

Gamma Regression is a GLM for positive, right-skewed data. Its defining property is \(\text{Var}(Y) = \phi\,\mu^2\) — variance grows as the square of the mean — so the coefficient of variation \(\text{CV} = \sqrt{\phi}\) is constant, not the standard deviation. This is fundamentally different from Normal/OLS where \(\text{Var}(Y) = \sigma^2\) (constant spread).

2
The GLM Framework & Log Link
▾

Three Components of a GLM

Gamma Regression is a member of the Generalized Linear Model family. Three components define it completely:

1. Random component: \(Y_i \sim \text{Gamma}(\mu_i, \phi)\), where \(\mu_i = \mathbb{E}[Y_i]\) is the conditional mean and \(\phi = 1/k\) is the dispersion parameter (reciprocal of the shape \(k\)). The variance is \(\text{Var}(Y_i) = \phi\,\mu_i^2\).

2. Systematic component: A linear predictor \(\eta_i = \mathbf{x}_i^\top\boldsymbol{\beta} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \cdots + \beta_p x_{ip}\).

3. Link function: Connects \(\mu_i\) to \(\eta_i\). The log link \(\eta_i = \log(\mu_i)\) is most common: it guarantees \(\mu_i = e^{\eta_i} > 0\) for any \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\), and gives multiplicative coefficient interpretation. The canonical link is the inverse \(\eta_i = 1/\mu_i\), used rarely in practice because it can produce negative fitted values for extreme predictors.

IRLS: Fitting via Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares

Coefficients are estimated by maximizing the log-likelihood. Since the score equations are non-linear in \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\), we use IRLS. Each iteration solves a weighted least-squares problem:

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{(t+1)} = \left(X^\top W^{(t)} X\right)^{-1} X^\top W^{(t)} \mathbf{z}^{(t)}$$

where \(\mathbf{z}^{(t)}\) is the working response and \(W^{(t)}\) is a diagonal weight matrix. For the log link with Gamma family:

$$z_i = \hat{\eta}_i + \frac{y_i - \hat{\mu}_i}{\hat{\mu}_i}, \qquad w_i = \frac{(\partial\mu/\partial\eta)^2}{V(\mu)} = \frac{\mu^2}{\mu^2} = 1$$

Remarkably, the log link gives unit weights — the IRLS reduces to unweighted least squares on the working response! This makes convergence fast (4–8 iterations) and numerically stable.

🔑 Key Property: Constant Coefficient of Variation

The Gamma family has \(\text{Var}(Y) = \phi\,\mu^2\), so \(\text{SD}(Y)/\mu = \sqrt{\phi}\) is constant across all x-values. This is realistic for monetary data: doubling the average claim also doubles the typical spread. OLS assumes \(\text{SD}(Y) = \sigma\) is constant regardless of the mean — a structurally incorrect assumption for right-skewed data.

3
The Math — Likelihood, Deviance & Dispersion
▾

Exponential Family Form

The Gamma distribution with mean \(\mu\) and dispersion \(\phi = 1/k\) has pdf:

$$f(y;\mu,\phi) = \frac{1}{\Gamma(1/\phi)}\left(\frac{y}{\phi\mu}\right)^{1/\phi} \exp\!\left(-\frac{y}{\phi\mu}\right)\frac{1}{y}, \quad y > 0$$

In exponential family form \(f(y;\theta,\phi) \propto \exp\!\left[\frac{y\theta - b(\theta)}{\phi}\right]\), the canonical parameter is \(\theta = -1/\mu\), confirming the canonical link is \(\eta = \theta = -1/\mu\), i.e., the inverse link. The cumulant function is \(b(\theta) = -\log(-\theta)\), giving \(b'(\theta) = \mu\) (mean) and \(b''(\theta) = \mu^2\) (variance function \(V(\mu) = \mu^2\)).

Log-Likelihood and Score Equations

For the log link \(\mu_i = e^{\mathbf{x}_i^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}}\), the log-likelihood (dropping constants in \(\phi\)) is:

$$\ell(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \frac{1}{\phi}\sum_{i=1}^n \left(-\frac{y_i}{\mu_i} - \log\mu_i\right) = \frac{1}{\phi}\sum_{i=1}^n \left(-y_i e^{-\mathbf{x}_i^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \mathbf{x}_i^\top\boldsymbol{\beta}\right)$$

The score equations \(\partial\ell/\partial\boldsymbol{\beta} = \mathbf{0}\) become:

$$\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{y_i - \mu_i}{\mu_i}\,\mathbf{x}_i = \mathbf{0}$$

These cannot be solved in closed form, hence IRLS. Note: \(\phi\) cancels from the score, so MLE of \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\) does not depend on knowing \(\phi\) — a key GLM convenience.

Deviance and Goodness of Fit

The deviance measures fit relative to the saturated model (one parameter per observation):

$$D = 2\sum_{i=1}^n\left[\frac{y_i - \hat{\mu}_i}{\hat{\mu}_i} - \log\!\frac{y_i}{\hat{\mu}_i}\right]$$

Each term \(d_i = 2[(y_i - \hat\mu_i)/\hat\mu_i - \log(y_i/\hat\mu_i)] \geq 0\) by Jensen's inequality (the function \(t - \log t \geq 1\) for all \(t > 0\)). Under correct specification, \(D/\phi \stackrel{\cdot}{\sim} \chi^2_{n-p}\).

Dispersion Estimation

$$\hat{\phi}_{\text{Pearson}} = \frac{1}{n-p}\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{(y_i - \hat{\mu}_i)^2}{\hat{\mu}_i^2}$$
⚠️ Critical Pitfall: OLS on log(y) ≠ Gamma GLM

Fitting OLS to \(\log y\) (log-normal regression) estimates \(\mathbb{E}[\log Y]\), which by Jensen's inequality satisfies \(\mathbb{E}[\log Y] \leq \log\mathbb{E}[Y]\). Back-transforming gives a biased downward estimate of the mean. Gamma GLM directly models \(\mathbb{E}[Y]\) on the log scale — the correct target for prediction and policy evaluation. Prefer Gamma GLM when the mean of \(y\) is your quantity of interest.

💡 Decision Guide

Use Gamma GLM: y > 0 strictly, right-skewed, variance fan-shaped (Var ∝ μ²). Check: plot Pearson residuals vs fitted — should show no pattern.
Use Tweedie: y ≥ 0 with exact zeros present (e.g., total claim cost including non-claimers).
Use log-normal: when the median on the original scale matters more than the mean, or multiplicative lognormal errors are the mechanistic model.
Avoid Gamma when: zeros are present; data is symmetric; response is bounded (use Beta regression instead).

6
Key Takeaways & Related Topics
▾

Three-line summary:

  • Gamma Regression is a GLM for strictly positive, right-skewed data with variance \(\phi\mu^2\) — the coefficient of variation \(\sqrt{\phi}\) is constant.
  • The log link keeps all predictions positive and gives multiplicative interpretation: each unit increase in x multiplies the mean by \(e^\beta\).
  • Fitting uses IRLS — fast and convergent — which reduces to ordinary (unweighted) least squares on the working response under the log link.
📐 Core Formulas to Know

Log link: \(\mu_i = \exp(\mathbf{x}_i^\top\boldsymbol{\beta})\) — always positive

Variance: \(\text{Var}(Y_i) = \phi\,\mu_i^2\), \(\text{CV} = \sqrt{\phi}\) = constant

Deviance: \(D = 2\sum_i\!\left[\frac{y_i-\hat\mu_i}{\hat\mu_i} - \ln\frac{y_i}{\hat\mu_i}\right]\)

Dispersion: \(\hat\phi = \frac{1}{n-p}\sum_i\frac{(y_i-\hat\mu_i)^2}{\hat\mu_i^2}\)
