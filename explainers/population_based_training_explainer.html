Population-based Training (PBT)

Discover hyperparameter schedules through parallel training & competitive selection

Advanced HPO / Optimization Population × Steps
1 Understanding the Concept
▼

Population-based Training (PBT) simultaneously trains a population of N models in parallel. Every T steps it evaluates all members, then the bottom-k models EXPLOIT (copy the weights of a top performer) and EXPLORE (randomly perturb that worker's hyperparameters). Instead of picking a fixed hyperparameter value before training starts, PBT discovers an adaptive schedule where hyperparameters change throughout training.

Key Insight PBT produces a hyperparameter schedule, not a single fixed value — the learning rate at step 10,000 may be completely different from step 1,000.

The Evolutionary Analogy

Think of PBT as natural selection for neural networks. Weak organisms (models with poor scores) die and are replaced by offspring that inherit winning genes (weights from a top performer) but with random mutations (hyperparameter perturbations). Over time, the population converges toward a high-performing configuration — but one that changes over the course of training rather than staying fixed.

Why Not Just Tune Once at the Start?

A learning rate that works well early in training (when gradients are large and noisy) may be completely wrong later (when fine-tuning near a minimum). Standard HPO finds one value for the whole run. PBT finds the best value for each phase of training automatically, without requiring the human to specify a schedule in advance.

  • Grid Search: Tries all combinations — exponentially expensive, no adaptation
  • Bayesian Optimisation: Smarter search, but still produces a fixed HP value per run
  • PBT: Adapts HPs during each run based on what's working right now
2 How PBT Works Internally
▼

Algorithm Step-by-Step

1Initialise population of N workers, each with random θ (weights) and h (hyperparams)
2while training_budget_not_exhausted:
3// Each worker trains independently for T steps
4for each worker i in population:
5θ_i ← train(θ_i, h_i, steps=T)
6
7// Synchronisation point: evaluate all workers
8for each worker i:
9Q_i ← evaluate(θ_i, h_i) // score metric (val loss, reward, etc.)
10
11// Exploit/Explore for underperforming workers
12for each worker i in bottom_fraction(population, p_exploit):
13j ← sample_from_top_fraction(population) // pick a winner
14// EXPLOIT: copy weights AND hyperparams from winner
15θ_i, h_i ← θ_j, h_j
16// EXPLORE: perturb the copied hyperparameters
17h_i ← perturb(h_i)
18
19Workers resume training from their new (θ_i, h_i)

EXPLORE Strategies

Strategy How It Works Best For
Perturb Multiply by \(\mathcal{U}(0.8, 1.2)\) Continuous HPs (learning rate, momentum)
Resample Draw fresh from original prior Categorical HPs (optimizer type, activation)
Noise Injection Add \(\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma)\) Bounded numeric HPs (dropout rate, weight decay)

Two Critical Design Choices

  • Synchronisation period T: Too short → workers can't diverge meaningfully; too long → wasted compute on bad configs
  • Fraction to exploit \(p_{exploit}\): Typically 20-25% of the population. Too high causes premature convergence; too low wastes compute on poor configs
3 The Mathematics of PBT
▼

Let the population be defined as:

$$\mathcal{P} = \{(\theta_i, h_i)\}_{i=1}^N$$

where \(\theta_i\) are the model weights for worker \(i\), and \(h_i\) are its hyperparameters. After \(T\) steps of training, each worker achieves a score:

$$Q_i = Q(\theta_i, h_i)$$

Exploit Rule

If worker \(i\) is an underperformer (\(Q_i < Q_j\) for some high-performing worker \(j\)), we copy both the weights AND hyperparameters:

$$(\theta_i, h_i) \leftarrow (\theta_j, h_j)$$

Explore Rule (Multiplicative Perturbation)

After copying, we perturb each hyperparameter dimension \(k\) by a random factor:

$$h_i^{(k)} \leftarrow h_j^{(k)} \cdot \mathcal{U}(1-\delta,\ 1+\delta), \quad \delta \in (0,1)$$

Common choices: \(\delta = 0.2\) (giving a \(\pm 20\%\) perturbation window). This ensures the new hyperparameter stays in the same order of magnitude as the winner, while allowing fine-grained exploration.

The Hyperparameter Schedule

The effective hyperparameter trajectory for worker \(i\) across all synchronisation checkpoints \(t_0 < t_1 < \ldots < t_K\) is:

$$\mathbf{h}_i^* = \{h_i^{(t_0)},\ h_i^{(t_1)},\ \ldots,\ h_i^{(t_K)}\}$$

This is the key insight: the schedule space of possible HP trajectories is exponentially larger than the per-step HP space. PBT implicitly searches this richer space by combining parallel exploration with weight-level exploitation.

Common Misconception PBT is NOT a multi-armed bandit. Exploit/explore in PBT does not use a Bayesian prior or posterior over hyperparameters. It is fundamentally a genetic algorithm operating on the joint space of model weights θ and hyperparameters h. The key difference: PBT copies weight checkpoints (not just HP values), making exploit far more powerful than any bandit-style warm-start.

Convergence Intuition

Under mild conditions (sufficient population size, appropriate \(T\)), PBT can be viewed as stochastic gradient descent in the joint \((\theta, h)\) space, where the exploit step provides a noisy gradient step in HP space guided by empirical performance differences \(Q_j - Q_i\).

5 Takeaways & Comparisons
▼

PBT vs. Alternatives

Method HPs Parallelism Adapts During Training Compute Cost
Grid Search Fixed None No Very High
Random Search Fixed None No Medium
Bayesian Opt. Fixed Sequential No Medium
Hyperband Fixed Yes (bracket) No Medium
PBT Schedule Full (population) Yes High

When to Use PBT

  • Long training runs — RL agents, large neural networks where HPs matter at different phases
  • Compute budget allows ≥8 parallel workers — otherwise the population is too small to be meaningful
  • HPs interact with training dynamics — learning rate schedule, entropy coefficient in RL, temperature annealing
  • You suspect a fixed HP is suboptimal — when you know the HP should change but don't know how

When NOT to Use PBT

  • Small datasets where each run is fast — use random search or Bayesian optimisation instead
  • Tight compute budgets with fewer than 4 workers
  • Short training runs where HPs don't interact with training dynamics
  • When you already have a well-validated fixed HP schedule from prior experiments
Historical Context PBT was introduced by Jaderberg et al. (2017) at DeepMind. It was used to train AlphaGo-style agents for games — and found learning rate schedules that human experts could not have designed manually. The key discovery: the optimal learning rate for RL agents should rise initially (to encourage exploration) then decay, but the exact shape of this schedule depends on the specific game and architecture in a way only PBT can discover automatically.
