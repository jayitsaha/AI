The Delta Method

Deriving the asymptotic distribution of nonlinear transformations of sample means via Taylor expansion

1
Understanding the Delta Method
▼

What is it? The Delta method is a statistical technique that tells you the approximate distribution of a transformed estimator. If you know the distribution of a sample mean \(\bar{X}_n\), the Delta method tells you how the distribution of \(g(\bar{X}_n)\) — for any smooth function \(g\) — behaves as your sample size grows. It is one of the core workhorse results of mathematical statistics and asymptotic theory.

Real-world analogy: Imagine you're measuring the average fuel efficiency (miles per gallon) of a car model, and you want to know the uncertainty in the cost per mile — which is a nonlinear function of MPG and fuel price. The Delta method says: "Linearize the cost function around the average MPG, then propagate the uncertainty through that linear approximation." The steeper the cost function is at that point, the more the output uncertainty is amplified.

💡 If you remember one thing

The Delta method says: if \(\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu) \xrightarrow{d} N(0,\sigma^2)\), then for any differentiable \(g\) with \(g'(\mu)\neq 0\): $$\sqrt{n}\bigl(g(\bar{X}_n) - g(\mu)\bigr) \xrightarrow{d} N\!\left(0,\; [g'(\mu)]^2\,\sigma^2\right)$$ Variance gets scaled by the square of the derivative evaluated at the mean.

The key insight is linearization: a differentiable function looks locally linear near any point, so the first-order Taylor expansion around \(\mu\) captures most of the behavior when \(\bar{X}_n\) is close to \(\mu\) (which it is for large \(n\) by the law of large numbers).

2
How It Works — The Taylor Expansion Engine
▼

Step 1: Start with the CLT

Let \(X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n \overset{iid}{\sim} F\) with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2 < \infty\). By the Central Limit Theorem:

$$\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu) \xrightarrow{d} N(0, \sigma^2)$$

Step 2: Expand g around μ

For a function \(g\) that is differentiable at \(\mu\), apply a first-order Taylor expansion:

$$g(\bar{X}_n) = g(\mu) + g'(\mu)(\bar{X}_n - \mu) + R_n$$

where \(R_n\) is the remainder term. Under mild regularity conditions, \(\sqrt{n}\,R_n \xrightarrow{p} 0\) — the remainder is negligible at the rate we care about.

Step 3: Rearrange and apply Slutsky

Multiply both sides by \(\sqrt{n}\):

$$\sqrt{n}\bigl(g(\bar{X}_n) - g(\mu)\bigr) = g'(\mu) \cdot \underbrace{\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu)}_{\xrightarrow{d}\, N(0,\sigma^2)} + \underbrace{\sqrt{n}\,R_n}_{\xrightarrow{p}\, 0}$$

By Slutsky's theorem, multiplying a sequence converging in distribution by a constant \(g'(\mu)\), and adding a term converging in probability to 0, gives:

$$\sqrt{n}\bigl(g(\bar{X}_n) - g(\mu)\bigr) \xrightarrow{d} g'(\mu) \cdot N(0,\sigma^2) = N\!\left(0,\,[g'(\mu)]^2\sigma^2\right)$$

Second-Order Delta Method (when g′(μ) = 0)

If \(g'(\mu) = 0\), the first-order term vanishes and we need to go to second order. Using the second-order Taylor expansion:

$$g(\bar{X}_n) - g(\mu) = \tfrac{1}{2}g''(\mu)(\bar{X}_n - \mu)^2 + o\!\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\right)$$

Multiplying by \(n\) (not \(\sqrt{n}\)) and using \(n(\bar{X}_n-\mu)^2 \xrightarrow{d} \sigma^2\chi^2(1)\):

$$n\bigl(g(\bar{X}_n) - g(\mu)\bigr) \xrightarrow{d} \tfrac{\sigma^2 g''(\mu)}{2}\,\chi^2(1)$$
🔑 Key Property

The normalizing rate changes: first-order delta method uses \(\sqrt{n}\) (normal limit), second-order uses \(n\) (chi-squared limit). The limiting distribution is qualitatively different — no longer Gaussian — when \(g'(\mu) = 0\).

⚠️ Common Mistake

Many texts state the delta method for statistics \(T_n\) that converge at rate \(\sqrt{n}\). If your statistic converges at a different rate (e.g., \(n^{1/3}\) for certain quantile estimates), the delta method still applies but you must track the correct normalization.

3
The Math — Full Derivation & Key Examples
▼

Formal Statement

Let \(T_n\) be a sequence of statistics satisfying \(\sqrt{n}(T_n - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} N(0, \tau^2)\). Let \(g: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) be continuously differentiable at \(\theta\) with \(g'(\theta) \neq 0\). Then:

$$\sqrt{n}\bigl(g(T_n) - g(\theta)\bigr) \xrightarrow{d} N\!\left(0,\;\bigl[g'(\theta)\bigr]^2\tau^2\right)$$

Equivalently, for \(\bar{X}_n\) from iid data with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\):

$$g(\bar{X}_n) \overset{\text{approx}}{\sim} N\!\left(g(\mu),\; \frac{[g'(\mu)]^2\sigma^2}{n}\right) \quad \text{for large }n$$

Multivariate Delta Method

For a vector \(\mathbf{T}_n \in \mathbb{R}^k\) with \(\sqrt{n}(\mathbf{T}_n - \boldsymbol{\theta}) \xrightarrow{d} N(\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})\) and \(g: \mathbb{R}^k \to \mathbb{R}\) differentiable:

$$\sqrt{n}\bigl(g(\mathbf{T}_n) - g(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigr) \xrightarrow{d} N\!\left(0,\; \nabla g(\boldsymbol{\theta})^\top \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\, \nabla g(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)$$

where \(\nabla g(\boldsymbol{\theta})\) is the gradient vector of \(g\) evaluated at \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\).

Worked Example 1: Confidence Interval for log(μ)

Let \(X_i \overset{iid}{\sim}\) any distribution with mean \(\mu = 2\) and variance \(\sigma^2 = 1\). We want a CI for \(\log(\mu)\).

Here \(g(x) = \log(x)\), so \(g'(x) = 1/x\). At \(\mu = 2\): \(g'(\mu) = 1/2 = 0.5\).

Delta method gives asymptotic variance of \(\sqrt{n}(g(\bar{X}_n) - g(\mu))\):

$$[g'(\mu)]^2\sigma^2 = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 \cdot 1 = \frac{1}{4} = 0.25$$

So \(g(\bar{X}_n) = \log(\bar{X}_n)\) has approximate distribution for \(n = 50\):

$$\log(\bar{X}_{50}) \approx N\!\left(\log(2),\; \frac{0.25}{50}\right) = N(0.6931,\; 0.005)$$

Standard deviation \(\approx 0.0707\). A 95% CI for \(\log(\mu)\) is \(\log(\bar{X}_n) \pm 1.96/\sqrt{n} \cdot |g'(\bar{X}_n)|\hat{\sigma}\).

Numerical verification (50,000 simulations, n=50): empirical std dev of \(\sqrt{50}(\log(\bar{X})-\log(2)) = 0.5013\) vs. predicted \(0.5000\). Match confirmed.

Worked Example 2: Variance of a Sample Variance estimator

The MLE of the standard deviation is \(\hat{\sigma} = \sqrt{\hat{\sigma}^2}\). Writing \(g(x) = \sqrt{x}\), \(g'(x) = 1/(2\sqrt{x})\). Since \(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\sigma}^2 - \sigma^2) \xrightarrow{d} N(0, \mu_4 - \sigma^4)\) where \(\mu_4\) is the 4th central moment:

$$\sqrt{n}\bigl(\hat{\sigma} - \sigma\bigr) \xrightarrow{d} N\!\left(0,\; \frac{\mu_4 - \sigma^4}{4\sigma^2}\right)$$

For Gaussian data, \(\mu_4 = 3\sigma^4\), giving asymptotic variance \(\sigma^2/2\).

Second-Order Example: g(x) = (x − 3)²

Let \(\mu = 3\), \(\sigma^2 = 1\), \(g(x) = (x-3)^2\). Then \(g'(\mu) = 2(\mu-3) = 0\) — the first-order term disappears. Use the second-order formula:

$$n\bigl(g(\bar{X}_n) - 0\bigr) = n(\bar{X}_n - 3)^2 \xrightarrow{d} \frac{\sigma^2 g''(\mu)}{2}\chi^2(1) = \frac{1 \cdot 2}{2}\chi^2(1) = \chi^2(1)$$

Simulation confirms: empirical mean of \(n\,g(\bar{X}_n)\) is \(1.013 \approx 1\) (expected for \(\chi^2(1)\)), variance \(2.047 \approx 2\). The distribution is skewed, not Gaussian.

🚨 Critical Warning

When \(g'(\mu) = 0\), the delta method does NOT give a normal limit — it gives a scaled chi-squared (or more generally a quadratic form). Applying the standard formula anyway yields a degenerate \(N(0,0)\), which is meaningless. You must use the second-order expansion.

Code Implementation
▼

From Scratch (NumPy)

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
def delta_method_ci(data, g, g_prime, alpha=0.05):
"""Compute a delta-method confidence interval for g(mu).
Args:
data : 1-D array of iid observations
g : callable, the transformation g(mu)
g_prime : callable, the derivative g'(mu)
alpha : significance level (default 0.05 → 95% CI)
Returns: (point_estimate, lower_ci, upper_ci)
"""
n = len(data)
x_bar = np.mean(data) # sample mean (plug-in for mu)
s2 = np.var(data, ddof=1) # sample variance (plug-in for sigma^2)
# Delta method asymptotic variance: [g'(x_bar)]^2 * s^2 / n
avar = (g_prime(x_bar) ** 2) * s2 / n
se = np.sqrt(avar) # standard error of g(x_bar)
# Normal-theory CI: g(x_bar) +/- z_{alpha/2} * se
z = stats.norm.ppf(1 - alpha / 2) # 1.96 for 95%
est = g(x_bar)
return est, est - z * se, est + z * se
# --- Example usage ---
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
data = rng.exponential(scale=2.0, size=200) # mean = 2, sigma^2 = 4
est, lo, hi = delta_method_ci(
data,
g = np.log, # g(x) = log(x)
g_prime = lambda x: 1.0 / x, # g'(x) = 1/x
)
print(f"Estimate: {est:.4f}, 95% CI: [{lo:.4f}, {hi:.4f}]")
# True value: log(2.0) = 0.6931

Production Usage (statsmodels)

# statsmodels provides delta_method for fitted models
import statsmodels.api as sm
from statsmodels.misc.delta_method import NonlinearDeltaCov
# Fit a logistic regression model
model = sm.Logit(y, X).fit()
# Use delta method to get CI for a nonlinear prediction
# e.g., the marginal effect d(P(Y=1))/dX1 at a specific point x0
nldc = NonlinearDeltaCov(
func = lambda params: expit(x0 @ params), # g(beta)
args = (model.params,),
cov = model.cov_params(),
)
ci = nldc.conf_int(alpha=0.05)
# ci = [lower, upper] from delta-method normal approximation
# ⚠️ Gotcha: delta method assumes n is large relative to model complexity.
# For small n or near a boundary (e.g., p ≈ 0 or 1), bootstrap is safer.

Interview Q&A & Comparisons
▼

Interview Questions: Easy → Hard

Q1 (Easy): What does the delta method do?

A: It gives the asymptotic distribution of \(g(\bar{X}_n)\) — a smooth transformation of a sample mean — using a first-order Taylor expansion to "propagate" the known normal distribution of \(\bar{X}_n\) through the function \(g\).

Q2 (Medium): Why does variance get scaled by \([g'(\mu)]^2\)?

A: From the linear approximation \(g(\bar{X}) \approx g(\mu) + g'(\mu)(\bar{X}-\mu)\), the variance is \(\operatorname{Var}[g'(\mu)(\bar{X}-\mu)] = [g'(\mu)]^2 \operatorname{Var}[\bar{X}] = [g'(\mu)]^2 \sigma^2/n\). The derivative acts as a local "magnification factor" — a steep curve amplifies, a flat curve compresses uncertainty.

Q3 (Medium): When would you use bootstrap instead of the delta method?

A: Use bootstrap when: (1) the sample size \(n\) is small and asymptotic approximations are poor; (2) \(g\) has a discontinuity or kink near \(\mu\); (3) \(g'(\mu) \approx 0\) (the delta method becomes inaccurate); (4) the true sampling distribution is highly non-normal even for moderate \(n\); or (5) you need second-order accuracy without deriving higher-order terms.

Q4 (Hard): What happens to the delta method at a local maximum or minimum of \(g\)?

A: If \(g'(\mu) = 0\) (e.g., \(\mu\) is a turning point of \(g\)), the first-order delta method gives a degenerate limit \(N(0,0)\). We must expand to second order. The normalizing rate changes from \(\sqrt{n}\) to \(n\), and the limiting distribution is \(\frac{\sigma^2 g''(\mu)}{2}\chi^2(1)\) — which is skewed, bounded below if \(g''>0\), and requires different inference tools.

Q5 (Hard, multi-level):

Explain to a PhD Researcher: The delta method is an application of the continuous mapping theorem combined with a Taylor remainder argument. Write \(g(\bar{X}_n) - g(\mu) = g'(\mu)(\bar{X}_n - \mu) + \frac{g''(\tilde{\mu})}{2}(\bar{X}_n-\mu)^2\) for some \(\tilde{\mu}\) between \(\bar{X}_n\) and \(\mu\). Under \(\sqrt{n}\)-convergence, the second term is \(O_p(1/n)\) so \(\sqrt{n}\cdot O_p(1/n) = O_p(1/\sqrt{n}) \to 0\) in probability. Apply Slutsky: \(\sqrt{n}(g(\bar{X}_n)-g(\mu)) = g'(\mu)\cdot\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n-\mu) + o_p(1) \xrightarrow{d} N(0,[g'(\mu)]^2\sigma^2)\).

Explain to a Research Scientist/Engineer: The CLT tells us \(\bar{X}_n\) is approximately normal near its mean \(\mu\). For large \(n\), \(\bar{X}_n\) rarely strays far from \(\mu\), so the nonlinear function \(g\) looks like a straight line there. The slope of that line is \(g'(\mu)\). Variance scales by slope squared. The delta method formalizes this intuition.

🎯 Decision Guide

Use delta method when \(n\geq 30\), \(g\) is smooth, \(|g'(\mu)|\) is not near zero, and you need a fast analytical answer. Switch to bootstrap when the normal approximation is poor, \(g'(\mu)\approx 0\), or exact coverage matters.
