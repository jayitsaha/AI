Correlation Analysis

Pearson r · Spearman ρ · Kendall τ · Point-Biserial · Phi Coefficient
Foundational EDA & Statistics Part IV · 1.3 5 Measures of Association
1
The 30-Second Version
▼

What is this? Correlation is a number that tells you how closely two variables move together — whether knowing the value of one lets you predict the other. A value near +1 means they rise together, near −1 means one rises as the other falls, and near 0 means they are unrelated.

Real-world analogy. Imagine tracking how warm it is (temperature) versus how much ice cream people buy. Those two things tend to rise together — that's positive correlation. Umbrella sales and sunshine have negative correlation. But ice cream sales don't cause summer — both are caused by hot weather. Correlation measures co-movement, never causation.

There isn't one single "correlation number" — five different measures exist for five different data situations:

  • Pearson r — both variables are continuous, relationship is linear
  • Spearman ρ — ordinal or continuous with non-linear (but monotonic) relationship, robust to outliers
  • Kendall τ — same setting as Spearman but based on counting consistent pairs; better for small samples
  • Point-biserial r — one variable is binary (yes/no), the other is continuous
  • Phi φ — both variables are binary; computed from a 2×2 contingency table
💡 If you remember one thing

Pick the measure that matches your data types. Pearson for two linear-continuous variables; Spearman/Kendall for ranks or non-linear monotonic; point-biserial for binary vs continuous; phi for binary vs binary. And correlation is never causation — always consider confounders.

2
How Each Measure Works Internally
▼

Pearson r — Linear Co-variation

Pearson r is the cosine similarity between the mean-centered vectors of two variables. It measures how much variance they share relative to their individual variances.

$$r = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i-\bar{x})^2}\;\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i-\bar{y})^2}}$$

Numerically: with \(x=[1..5]\), \(y=[1.2,2.8,2.1,4.5,5.3]\): \(\bar{x}=3, \bar{y}=3.18\), numerator = \(\sum (x_i-3)(y_i-3.18) = 9.26\), denominator = \(\sqrt{10}\cdot\sqrt{9.282} = 9.638\), so \(r = 0.9245\).

Spearman ρ — Rank Correlation

Replace each raw value with its rank, then compute Pearson r on the ranks. The simplified formula applies when there are no ties:

$$\rho = 1 - \frac{6\sum_{i=1}^n d_i^2}{n(n^2-1)}$$

where \(d_i = \text{rank}(x_i) - \text{rank}(y_i)\). For the example above: ranks of \(y\) are [1,3,2,4,5], so \(d=[0,-1,1,0,0]\), \(\sum d^2=2\), \(\rho = 1 - 6\cdot2/120 = 0.9000\).

Kendall τ — Concordant Pairs

For every pair \((i,j)\) with \(i < j\), call it concordant if \((x_j-x_i)(y_j-y_i)>0\) (both go the same way) and discordant otherwise.

$$\tau = \frac{C - D}{\binom{n}{2}} = \frac{C - D}{\tfrac{1}{2}n(n-1)}$$

For the five-point example: \(C=9\), \(D=1\), \(\tau=(9-1)/10=0.80\). Only one inversion (pair (2,3): x goes up but y goes down 2.8→2.1).

Point-Biserial r

When one variable is binary (group indicator), the point-biserial reduces to a rescaled difference in group means:

$$r_{pb} = \frac{M_1 - M_0}{s_Y}\sqrt{\frac{n_1 n_0}{n^2}}$$

where \(M_1,M_0\) are continuous-variable means in the two groups, \(s_Y\) is the overall SD, \(n_1,n_0\) are group sizes. It equals the Pearson r when the binary variable is coded 0/1.

Phi Coefficient (φ)

For a 2×2 table with cell counts \(a,b,c,d\):

$$\varphi = \frac{ad - bc}{\sqrt{(a+b)(c+d)(a+c)(b+d)}}$$

Phi is identical to Pearson r on two binary variables coded 0/1. Example: with \(a=30,b=10,c=15,d=45\), \(\varphi=(30\cdot45-10\cdot15)/\sqrt{40\cdot60\cdot45\cdot55} = 1200/2438.7 = 0.492\).

⚠️ Anscombe's Quartet

Four datasets can have identical Pearson r ≈ 0.816, mean, and variance yet look completely different — one linear, one curved, one with an outlier, one with a leverage point. Always plot the data. Pearson r alone is never sufficient for EDA.

🚫 Correlation ≠ Causation

Per capita cheese consumption correlates \(r=0.947\) with deaths by bedsheet tangling (spurious). High correlation can arise from: a common cause (confounder), reverse causality, coincidence, or selection bias. Establish causation with experiments or causal inference methods.

3
The Mathematics Behind Each Measure
▼

Pearson r — Geometric Interpretation

Let \(\tilde{x}_i = x_i - \bar{x}\) and \(\tilde{y}_i = y_i - \bar{y}\). Then:

$$r = \frac{\mathbf{\tilde{x}} \cdot \mathbf{\tilde{y}}}{\|\mathbf{\tilde{x}}\|\|\mathbf{\tilde{y}}\|} = \cos\theta$$

where \(\theta\) is the angle between the two centered vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^n\). Thus \(-1 \le r \le 1\) is guaranteed by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality \(|\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}| \le \|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|\). Equality (r=±1) holds iff one vector is a scalar multiple of the other — perfect linearity.

Alternatively, \(r^2 = R^2\) from simple linear regression: Pearson r is the square root of the proportion of variance explained, signed by the slope's direction.

Spearman ρ — Connection to Pearson

Spearman ρ is the Pearson r applied to ranks \(R_i = \text{rank}(x_i)\), \(S_i = \text{rank}(y_i)\). The simplified formula follows from the fact that ranks \(1,\ldots,n\) have mean \((n+1)/2\) and variance \((n^2-1)/12\):

$$\rho = 1 - \frac{6\sum d_i^2}{n(n^2-1)}, \quad d_i = R_i - S_i$$

This formula is exact only when there are no ties. With ties, use the full Pearson r formula on average ranks.

Kendall τ — Asymptotic Distribution

Under \(H_0\) (no association), \(\tau\) is asymptotically normal:

$$\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{\dfrac{2(2n+5)}{9n(n-1)}}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1)$$

Kendall τ converges slower than Spearman but is more interpretable: it is a probability, \(\tau = P(\text{concordant}) - P(\text{discordant})\). For large n, \(\tau \approx \frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin(\rho)\) empirically.

Point-Biserial — t-test Equivalence

The t-statistic for testing \(H_0: M_1 = M_0\) and the t-statistic for testing \(H_0: r_{pb}=0\) are identical:

$$t = r_{pb}\sqrt{\frac{n-2}{1-r_{pb}^2}}$$

with \(n-2\) degrees of freedom. This means a point-biserial test and an independent-samples t-test always give the same p-value — they are mathematically equivalent.

Phi — Chi-Square Link

Phi is related to Pearson's chi-square statistic \(\chi^2\) on the 2×2 table by:

$$\varphi = \sqrt{\frac{\chi^2}{n}}$$

where \(n = a+b+c+d\). This means \(\varphi^2 = \chi^2/n\) is Cramér's V for 2×2 tables. Testing \(\varphi=0\) is equivalent to testing independence with chi-square.

📐 Summary of Formulas

Pearson: \(r = \frac{\sum \tilde{x}_i\tilde{y}_i}{\|\tilde{x}\|\|\tilde{y}\|}\)  |  Spearman: \(\rho = 1 - \frac{6\sum d_i^2}{n(n^2-1)}\)  |  Kendall: \(\tau = \frac{C-D}{n(n-1)/2}\)  |  P-Bis: \(r_{pb} = \frac{M_1-M_0}{s_Y}\sqrt{\frac{n_1n_0}{n^2}}\)  |  Phi: \(\varphi = \frac{ad-bc}{\sqrt{(a+b)(c+d)(a+c)(b+d)}}\)

Comparison & When to Use Each
▼
MeasureData TypesAssumptionOutlier RobustnessSmall nUse When
Pearson r continuous + continuous linear, bivariate normal Low — very sensitive OK Both variables are numeric, relationship is linear, few outliers
Spearman ρ ordinal or continuous monotonic (not necessarily linear) High — uses ranks OK Ordinal data, exponential/log curves, data with outliers
Kendall τ ordinal or continuous monotonic High Better than ρ Small samples, ties present, interpretable as concordance probability
Point-biserial r binary + continuous continuous var is normal in each group Moderate OK Group membership vs test score, gender vs income, disease vs biomarker
Phi φ binary + binary none (nonparametric) N/A OK Two yes/no variables, pass/fail vs treatment/control
🎯 Decision Rule

Both continuous + linear → Pearson · Both continuous/ordinal + nonlinear monotonic → Spearman · Small n / many ties → Kendall · Binary vs continuous → Point-biserial · Binary vs binary → Phi

Code: All Five in One Call (Python)

from scipy import stats
import numpy as np
# Pearson
r, p_r = stats.pearsonr(x, y)
# Spearman (monotonic, handles non-linearity)
rho, p_rho = stats.spearmanr(x, y)
# Kendall tau-b (handles ties)
tau, p_tau = stats.kendalltau(x, y)
# Point-biserial (binary 0/1 in `group`, continuous in `score`)
rpb, p_rpb = stats.pointbiserialr(group, score)
# Phi (2x2 contingency table) [a,b,c,d] = [TT,TF,FT,FF]
a,b,c,d = 30, 10, 15, 45
phi = (a*d - b*c) / np.sqrt((a+b)*(c+d)*(a+c)*(b+d))
# Or: phi = np.corrcoef(row1, row2)[0,1] when data is 0/1 arrays

Interview Deep-Dive & Key Takeaways
▼

Q1 (Easy): When would you NOT use Pearson r?

A: When the relationship is nonlinear, when variables are ordinal (e.g., Likert scale), or when outliers are present. Pearson is sensitive to single influential points — one outlier can drag r from 0.95 to 0.20. Use Spearman or Kendall instead.

Q2 (Medium): Why does Spearman ρ handle nonlinear relationships?

A: Spearman operates on ranks. Any strictly monotonic transformation \(f\) preserves ranks: \(\text{rank}(f(x_i)) = \text{rank}(x_i)\). So whether \(y = x^3\) or \(y = e^x\) or \(y = \log x\), if the relationship is monotonic, the ranks are perfectly ordered and \(\rho = 1\). For the exponential dataset: Pearson r = 0.865 but Spearman ρ = 1.000.

Q3 (Medium): What is the difference between Spearman and Kendall?

A: Both are rank-based. Spearman uses squared rank differences (an indirect measure of agreement). Kendall counts concordant vs discordant pairs directly, giving an estimate of the probability \(P(\text{concordant pair}) - P(\text{discordant pair})\) — a more interpretable quantity. Kendall is preferred for small samples because its null distribution is better approximated by the exact permutation distribution. For large n, they yield similar conclusions but different absolute values (typically \(|\tau| < |\rho|\) for the same data).

Q4 (Medium): A researcher reports r = 0.03 and says "no correlation." Is this correct?

A: Not necessarily. Three failure modes: (1) The relationship may be nonlinear — a U-shaped relationship has r ≈ 0 but perfect predictability. (2) Restriction of range — if you sample only high performers, you may see near-zero correlation where the full population has a strong one. (3) With large n (e.g., n=10,000), r=0.03 may be statistically significant. Always plot, always check effect size (r) vs significance (p).

Q5 (Hard): What assumptions does Pearson r require for the p-value to be valid?

A: The test statistic \(t = r\sqrt{(n-2)/(1-r^2)}\) follows a t-distribution with \(n-2\) df under the null hypothesis if (X,Y) follow a bivariate normal distribution, OR if n is large (CLT). Crucially: normality of X and Y individually is not sufficient — you need joint bivariate normality. If this fails, use a permutation test or report confidence intervals via Fisher's z-transformation: \(z = \frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1+r}{1-r}\), which is approximately \(\mathcal{N}(\text{arctanh}(\rho), 1/(n-3))\).

Q6 (Hard): How does point-biserial r relate to the t-test?

A: They are algebraically equivalent. The t-statistic for two-sample equality of means equals \(t = r_{pb}\sqrt{(n-2)/(1-r_{pb}^2)}\), giving identical p-values. This means reporting \(r_{pb}\) alongside a t-test provides the effect size for free — Cohen's d and \(r_{pb}\) are also monotonically related: \(d = 2r_{pb}/\sqrt{1-r_{pb}^2}\).

⚠️ Interviewer Red Flags
  • Saying "Spearman just handles nonlinear data" — it handles monotonic nonlinear. A sine wave (non-monotonic) will still fool Spearman.
  • Confusing statistical significance with practical significance — with n=1M, r=0.001 can be p<0.001 but explains 0.0001% of variance.
  • Not knowing that phi = Pearson r on binary-coded variables.
  • Claiming correlation implies any direction of causality.

Key Takeaways

  • Five measures cover all combinations of variable types: continuous–continuous (Pearson/Spearman/Kendall), binary–continuous (point-biserial), binary–binary (phi).
  • Pearson r is sensitive to outliers and nonlinearity; rank-based measures (Spearman, Kendall) are not.
  • Kendall τ has a direct probabilistic interpretation as P(concordant) − P(discordant).
  • Phi and point-biserial are special cases of Pearson r — the formulas simplify when one or both variables are binary.
  • Always visualize. Anscombe's quartet shows four datasets with identical Pearson r ≈ 0.816 but completely different shapes.
  • Correlation measures association, not causation. Always consider confounders.
