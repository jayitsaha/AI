SVR: Support Vector Regression

ε-insensitive loss · flatness regularization · only boundary violations pay · sparse support vectors
Intermediate Classical ML SVM Family QP · O(n²–n³)
1
Understanding SVR
▼

What is SVR? Support Vector Regression extends the SVM idea from classification to regression. Instead of finding a separating hyperplane, SVR finds a regression function that fits within an ε-wide tube around the data. Points inside the tube are considered "good enough" — they pay zero loss. Only points that fall outside the tube (violating the ε boundary) incur any penalty.

Analogy: The "good enough" zone. Imagine an archer shooting at a target. OLS says every millimeter off-center costs something — even tiny deviations add up. SVR says: "If you hit within the bullseye ring, I don't care by exactly how much — all those count as zero error. Only shots completely outside the ring are penalized." This ε-wide bullseye is the ε-tube.

Why does this matter? In real data, there is always irreducible noise. OLS is "greedy" — it tries to explain every jitter, even noise, which can overfit. SVR explicitly tolerates noise within ε, producing a sparser and often more robust model: the fitted function depends only on the points at or beyond the tube boundary (the support vectors).

💡 If you remember one thing

SVR fits a function inside an ε-tube. Points inside cost nothing. Points outside become support vectors and pay slack \(\xi_i = |r_i| - \varepsilon > 0\). The objective is $$\min_{w,b,\xi} \; \frac{1}{2}\|w\|^2 + C\sum_i (\xi_i + \xi_i^*)$$ balancing flatness (small \(\|w\|\)) against tolerance for outliers beyond the tube.

🔑 Key Property — Sparsity

The dual solution shows \(w = \sum_i (\alpha_i - \alpha_i^*) x_i\). Only support vectors (points at or outside the tube boundary) have \(\alpha_i \neq 0\) or \(\alpha_i^* \neq 0\). All interior points drop out entirely. This is the kernel trick entry point: \(w\) lives in the span of training examples, enabling nonlinear SVR via \(K(x_i, x_j)\).

2
The ε-Tube and Support Vectors
▼

The ε-Insensitive Loss Function

The core idea of SVR is the ε-insensitive loss, which ignores all errors within a threshold ε and penalizes only the excess beyond it:

$$L_\varepsilon(r) = \max(0,\, |r| - \varepsilon) = \begin{cases} 0 & |r| \le \varepsilon \\ |r| - \varepsilon & |r| > \varepsilon \end{cases}$$

where \(r_i = y_i - f(x_i)\) is the residual for point \(i\). This differs fundamentally from OLS, which uses \(L_{OLS}(r) = r^2\) — every residual, no matter how small, contributes to the loss.

What are Support Vectors in SVR?

In classification SVM, support vectors are the points closest to the decision boundary. In SVR, support vectors are the points at or beyond the ε-tube boundary: points where \(|r_i| \geq \varepsilon\). These are the only points that influence \(w\). Points strictly inside the tube (\(|r_i| < \varepsilon\)) are irrelevant to the fitted hyperplane — you could move them slightly and the fit would not change.

Slack Variables: Tolerating Violations

When a point falls outside the tube, it needs a slack variable to account for the violation. Two slack variables per point handle both sides of the tube:

$$\xi_i \ge 0: \quad y_i - (w^\top x_i + b) \le \varepsilon + \xi_i \quad\text{(above tube)}$$
$$\xi_i^* \ge 0: \quad (w^\top x_i + b) - y_i \le \varepsilon + \xi_i^* \quad\text{(below tube)}$$

Geometrically: if the prediction is within ε of the truth, both slacks are zero. If the prediction misses by more than ε, the excess is captured by the corresponding slack. The total penalty is \(C \sum_i (\xi_i + \xi_i^*)\).

Numerical Example (from the interactive viz)

With \(w=0.917\), \(b=0.25\), \(\varepsilon=0.5\), \(C=1.0\):

  • x=−2: \(f(-2) = -1.583\), truth=−2.8, residual=−1.217. \(|\text{−1.217}| - 0.5 = \xi^* = 0.717\) → outside, SV
  • x=2: \(f(2) = 2.083\), truth=2.7, residual=0.617. \(|0.617| - 0.5 = \xi = 0.117\) → outside, SV
  • x=0: \(f(0) = 0.25\), truth=0.2, residual=−0.05. \(|-0.05| < 0.5\) → inside tube, zero loss
⚠️ Common Confusion

In SVR, "support vector" means a point AT or BEYOND the tube boundary — not only the points exactly on the boundary. This includes points outside the tube (with \(\xi > 0\)) and points exactly on the boundary (with \(\xi = 0\), called "free support vectors"). The dual variable \(\alpha_i > 0\) for exactly-on-boundary SVs, and \(0 < \alpha_i < C\) encodes the boundary case vs. the slack case.

The Math — Primal, Dual & KKT
▼

Primal Problem

SVR solves a constrained convex optimization (Quadratic Program):

$$\min_{w,b,\xi,\xi^*} \; \frac{1}{2}\|w\|^2 + C\sum_{i=1}^n (\xi_i + \xi_i^*)$$

subject to: \(y_i - (w^\top x_i + b) \le \varepsilon + \xi_i\), \((w^\top x_i + b) - y_i \le \varepsilon + \xi_i^*\), \(\xi_i, \xi_i^* \ge 0\)

The \(\frac{1}{2}\|w\|^2\) term promotes flatness — a flatter function generalizes better. \(C > 0\) trades off flatness against the total slack (how much we tolerate violations). Large \(C\): tighter tube, fewer violations tolerated. Small \(C\): wider effective tube, more violations accepted.

Dual Problem (Wolfe Dual)

Introducing Lagrange multipliers \(\alpha_i, \alpha_i^* \ge 0\) for each constraint, the dual (via KKT) is:

$$\max_{\alpha,\alpha^*} \; -\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}(\alpha_i - \alpha_i^*)(\alpha_j - \alpha_j^*)\langle x_i, x_j\rangle - \varepsilon\sum_i(\alpha_i + \alpha_i^*) + \sum_i y_i(\alpha_i - \alpha_i^*)$$

subject to: \(\sum_i (\alpha_i - \alpha_i^*) = 0\), \(\quad 0 \le \alpha_i, \alpha_i^* \le C\)

The dual encodes the kernel trick: replace \(\langle x_i, x_j\rangle\) with \(K(x_i, x_j)\) to get nonlinear SVR. The prediction becomes:

$$f(x) = \sum_i (\alpha_i - \alpha_i^*) K(x_i, x) + b$$

KKT Conditions — When Is a Point a Support Vector?

The KKT complementary slackness conditions reveal the structure:

  • \(\alpha_i (\varepsilon + \xi_i - y_i + w^\top x_i + b) = 0\) — if \(\alpha_i > 0\): point at or above upper tube boundary
  • \(\alpha_i^* (\varepsilon + \xi_i^* + y_i - w^\top x_i - b) = 0\) — if \(\alpha_i^* > 0\): point at or below lower tube boundary
  • \(\alpha_i (C - \alpha_i) = 0\) and \(\alpha_i^* (C - \alpha_i^*) = 0\)

This gives three cases: (1) \(\alpha_i = \alpha_i^* = 0\): inside tube, doesn't contribute to \(w\); (2) \(0 < \alpha_i < C\) or \(0 < \alpha_i^* < C\): on tube boundary, \(\xi=0\), free SV; (3) \(\alpha_i = C\) or \(\alpha_i^* = C\): outside tube, \(\xi > 0\), bounded SV.

📐 The Weight Vector Decomposition

From KKT stationarity: \(\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w} = 0 \Rightarrow w = \sum_{i=1}^n (\alpha_i - \alpha_i^*) x_i\). Only SVs (where \(\alpha_i \neq \alpha_i^*\)) contribute. This is the sparsity property of SVR — not all \(n\) training points matter, only those at or beyond the tube. In our 7-point example, 4 SVs determine the entire fit.

ε-Insensitive Loss vs. Squared Loss Shape

The shape of the loss function is the key distinction. OLS squares every residual. SVR uses the ε-insensitive loss: zero inside the tube, linear outside.

$$L_\varepsilon(r) = \max(0,\, |r| - \varepsilon) = \begin{cases} 0 & |r| \le \varepsilon \\ |r| - \varepsilon & |r| > \varepsilon \end{cases}$$

When to Use SVR

  • You expect some noise tolerance is appropriate (errors within ε truly don't matter)
  • Data has meaningful outliers you want to resist without removing
  • Nonlinear regression with small-to-medium datasets (kernel SVR)
  • You need a sparse model that depends only on key examples

When NOT to Use SVR

  • Very large datasets (n > 100k) — QP becomes expensive, prefer gradient boosting or neural nets
  • You need probabilistic predictions / confidence intervals — use GP regression instead
  • ε is hard to tune and your noise scale is unknown — OLS or Ridge may be simpler
🎯 Key Takeaways

1. SVR uses ε-insensitive loss — zero cost inside the tube, linear penalty outside.

2. Only support vectors (at or beyond tube boundary) determine the fit — interior points are irrelevant.

3. Two hyperparameters: C controls outlier tolerance, ε controls tube width.

4. The kernel trick extends SVR to nonlinear regression without explicit feature maps.
