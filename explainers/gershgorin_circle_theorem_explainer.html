Gershgorin Circle Theorem

Bounding eigenvalues on the complex plane using row sums — no computation required
Foundational Lean Intermediate Linear Algebra Spectral Theory O(n²) bound
1
Understanding the Theorem
▼

What is it? The Gershgorin Circle Theorem gives you guaranteed regions in the complex plane where every eigenvalue of a matrix must live — and it costs almost nothing to compute. For each row \(i\) of the matrix, you draw a disc centered at the diagonal entry \(a_{ii}\) with radius equal to the sum of the absolute values of the off-diagonal entries in that row. Every eigenvalue is inside the union of all these discs.

Analogy: Think of each diagonal entry as a city centre, and the off-diagonal entries as roads radiating outward. The "Gershgorin disc" tells you: "this eigenvalue cannot have wandered further from the city centre than the total road length." If the roads are short (small off-diagonal entries), the eigenvalue is pinned close to the diagonal — the matrix is "almost diagonal" and its spectrum is almost read off the diagonal.

💡 If you remember one thing

Every eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of an \(n \times n\) matrix \(A\) lies in at least one Gershgorin disc \(D_i\): $$|\lambda - a_{ii}| \leq R_i \quad \text{where} \quad R_i = \sum_{j \neq i} |a_{ij}|$$ If \(k\) discs are isolated from the rest, exactly \(k\) eigenvalues lie in their union.

Why does this matter? Eigenvalue computation is expensive for large matrices. Gershgorin lets you instantly certify stability (all eigenvalues in left half-plane?), check invertibility (no disc contains zero?), or bound spectral radius — all from a single pass through the matrix entries. It is a tool used in numerical analysis, control theory, and convergence proofs for iterative solvers.

How It Works — Proof Sketch & Internal Mechanism
▼

The Proof in Three Lines

Let \(\lambda\) be an eigenvalue of \(A\) and \(\mathbf{x}\) a corresponding eigenvector (\(A\mathbf{x} = \lambda \mathbf{x}\), \(\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}\)). Pick the index \(i\) such that \(|x_i| = \|\mathbf{x}\|_\infty\) (the largest component in absolute value). The \(i\)-th equation of \(A\mathbf{x} = \lambda\mathbf{x}\) is:

$$\sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} x_j = \lambda x_i \implies (\lambda - a_{ii}) x_i = \sum_{j \neq i} a_{ij} x_j$$

Take absolute values on both sides and use the triangle inequality:

$$|\lambda - a_{ii}| \cdot |x_i| \leq \sum_{j \neq i} |a_{ij}| \cdot |x_j| \leq |x_i| \sum_{j \neq i} |a_{ij}| = |x_i| \cdot R_i$$

Dividing by \(|x_i| > 0\) (it is the maximum component, hence positive) gives \(|\lambda - a_{ii}| \leq R_i\). QED — \(\lambda\) lies in disc \(D_i\). Since \(i\) is the index of the maximum component, we only know \(\lambda\) is in that specific disc, which is why the theorem guarantees "at least one disc" rather than "all discs."

The Isolated Component Corollary

If a connected component of the union of discs contains exactly \(k\) discs (by centre count), then exactly \(k\) eigenvalues (counting multiplicity) lie in that component. This follows from a continuity argument: as you dial off-diagonal entries from 0 (where eigenvalues are exactly the diagonal entries) to their actual values, eigenvalues vary continuously. As long as components stay isolated, eigenvalues cannot jump between them.

🔑 Diagonal Dominance Connection

A matrix is strictly diagonally dominant if \(|a_{ii}| > R_i\) for all \(i\). In this case every Gershgorin disc excludes the origin, so \(\lambda = 0\) is not an eigenvalue — the matrix is invertible. This is one of the most commonly cited applications of the theorem.

Column Version

Because \(A\) and \(A^\top\) share the same eigenvalues, the theorem also holds with column sums: \(|\lambda - a_{jj}| \leq C_j = \sum_{i \neq j} |a_{ij}|\). Taking the intersection of row-based and column-based regions gives tighter bounds.

3
Mathematical Precision — Definitions & Properties
▼

Formal Statement

Let \(A = (a_{ij}) \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}\). For each \(i \in \{1, \ldots, n\}\), define the Gershgorin disc:

$$D_i = \left\{ z \in \mathbb{C} : |z - a_{ii}| \leq R_i \right\}, \quad R_i = \sum_{j=1, j \neq i}^{n} |a_{ij}|$$

The Gershgorin domain is \(\mathcal{G}(A) = \bigcup_{i=1}^n D_i\). The theorem states:

$$\sigma(A) \subseteq \mathcal{G}(A)$$

where \(\sigma(A)\) denotes the spectrum (set of all eigenvalues) of \(A\).

Sharpness

The bound is tight: for any configuration of discs you can find a matrix whose eigenvalues touch the boundary of \(\mathcal{G}(A)\). The bound is achieved, e.g., by triangular matrices where the off-diagonal entries conspire to push an eigenvalue to the disc boundary.

Perturbation Bound (Bauer-Fike Connection)

If \(A = D + E\) where \(D\) is diagonal, Gershgorin applied to \(A\) with the diagonal \(D\) gives: each eigenvalue of \(A\) lies within \(\|E\|_\infty\) of some diagonal entry of \(D\). This directly connects to the Bauer-Fike theorem for diagonalizable matrices under the operator \(\infty\)-norm.

⚠️ What the Theorem Does NOT Say

The theorem tells you where eigenvalues can be — it does not tell you the exact eigenvalues. Two discs that overlap may jointly contain anywhere from 1 to \(|D_i \cup D_j|\) eigenvalues. The region is a sufficient but not necessary condition. Eigenvalues can be far from all disc boundaries (deep inside the union).

Numerical Trace — 3×3 Example

Consider the matrix:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 & 0 \\ 0.5 & 6 & 0.5 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$$

Gershgorin discs:

  • Row 1: centre \(a_{11} = 4\), radius \(R_1 = |1| + |0| = 1\). Disc: \(|z - 4| \leq 1\), covers \([3, 5]\) on real axis.
  • Row 2: centre \(a_{22} = 6\), radius \(R_2 = |0.5| + |0.5| = 1\). Disc: \(|z - 6| \leq 1\), covers \([5, 7]\) on real axis.
  • Row 3: centre \(a_{33} = 3\), radius \(R_3 = |0| + |1| = 1\). Disc: \(|z - 3| \leq 1\), covers \([2, 4]\) on real axis.

Actual eigenvalues (computed with numpy.linalg.eigvals): \(\lambda_1 \approx 2.819\), \(\lambda_2 \approx 3.821\), \(\lambda_3 \approx 6.361\). Check: \(\lambda_1 \in D_3\) since \(|2.819-3|=0.181 \leq 1\) ✓; \(\lambda_2 \in D_1 \cap D_3\) ✓; \(\lambda_3 \in D_2\) since \(|6.361-6|=0.361 \leq 1\) ✓. Trace \(= 13 =\) sum of eigenvalues ✓.

For the diagonally dominant example:

$$B = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0.5 & 0 \\ 0.3 & 6 & 0.3 \\ 0 & 0.5 & 11 \end{bmatrix}$$

Discs: \(D_1: |z-2| \leq 0.5\) (interval \([1.5, 2.5]\)), \(D_2: |z-6| \leq 0.6\) (interval \([5.4, 6.6]\)), \(D_3: |z-11| \leq 0.5\) (interval \([10.5, 11.5]\)). Gaps between discs: 2.9, 8.0, 3.9 — all strictly disjoint. Actual eigenvalues: \(\approx 1.963, 6.007, 11.030\) — exactly one per disc, as the corollary guarantees.

From-Scratch Python Implementation
▼

Computing Gershgorin discs and checking the theorem numerically:

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
def gershgorin_discs(A):
"""Return list of (centre, radius) for each row."""
n = A.shape[0]
discs = []
for i in range(n):
centre = A[i, i] # diagonal entry
radius = np.sum(np.abs(A[i, :])) - np.abs(A[i, i]) # sum off-diag
discs.append((centre, radius))
return discs
A = np.array([[4, 1, 0],
[0.5, 6, 0.5],
[0, 1, 3]], dtype=float)
discs = gershgorin_discs(A)
eigenvalues = np.linalg.eigvals(A) # actual eigenvalues
for i, (c, r) in enumerate(discs):
print(f"D_{i+1}: centre={c:.2f}, radius={r:.2f}")
# Verify: each eigenvalue inside at least one disc
for lam in eigenvalues:
inside = [abs(lam - c) <= r + 1e-10 for c, r in discs]
print(f"λ={lam:.3f} in discs: {[i+1 for i,v in enumerate(inside) if v]}")

Key Takeaways & When to Use
▼

Use Gershgorin When You Need To:

  • Quickly certify that a matrix is non-singular (check no disc contains zero)
  • Bound the spectral radius \(\rho(A) = \max_i |\lambda_i|\) cheaply
  • Verify stability: do all discs lie in the left half-plane (Re \(\lambda\) < 0)?
  • Diagnose whether an iterative solver (Jacobi, Gauss-Seidel) will converge — they converge for strictly diagonally dominant matrices
  • Quickly localize eigenvalues during perturbation analysis

Do NOT rely on Gershgorin When:

  • You need exact eigenvalues — the discs can be much larger than necessary
  • Off-diagonal entries are large — the discs will be huge and provide no useful info
  • The matrix has structure (symmetric, normal) that lets sharper bounds apply (e.g., interlacing theorem for symmetric tridiagonal matrices)
