Projection Matrices & Orthogonal Projections

How vectors "shadow" onto subspaces — the geometry behind least squares
Intermediate Linear Algebra Math Foundations P = A(AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀ
1
Understanding Projection Matrices
▼

What is it? A projection matrix \(P\) takes any vector \(\mathbf{b}\) and maps it to the nearest point \(\hat{\mathbf{b}}\) inside a given subspace (like a line or a plane through the origin). "Nearest" means minimising distance — so the leftover vector \(\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{b} - \hat{\mathbf{b}}\) is always perpendicular to the subspace.

Analogy: Shine a flashlight straight down (orthogonally) onto a table. The shadow of a pen on the table is the projection of the pen onto the 2D plane. No matter which way you tilt the pen, its shadow is always the closest point on the table to the pen's tip. The "flashlight" operation is the projection matrix \(P\).

💡 If you remember one thing

The formula \(P = A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\) projects onto the column space of \(A\). It has two magical properties: hit it twice and nothing changes (\(P^2 = P\), called idempotency), and it equals its own transpose (\(P^\top = P\), called symmetry). The residual \(\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{b} - P\mathbf{b}\) is always orthogonal to every column of \(A\).

One-sentence summary: A projection matrix crushes a vector onto a subspace by the shortest possible path, leaving a remainder that points straight away from that subspace.

2
How Projection Matrices Work Internally
▼

The Orthogonality Condition

We want \(\hat{\mathbf{b}} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) (some combination of the columns of \(A\)) such that the error \(\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}\) is orthogonal to every column of \(A\). "Orthogonal to every column of \(A\)" means:

$$A^\top(\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}) = \mathbf{0}$$

These are the famous normal equations. Solving for \(\hat{\mathbf{x}}\):

$$A^\top A\,\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^\top \mathbf{b} \implies \hat{\mathbf{x}} = (A^\top A)^{-1} A^\top \mathbf{b}$$

The projection is then \(\hat{\mathbf{b}} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}}\), and collecting the matrix acting on \(\mathbf{b}\) gives us \(P\):

$$P = A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top$$

The Two Key Properties: Idempotency and Symmetry

Idempotency \((P^2 = P)\): Projecting twice does nothing new. If \(\hat{\mathbf{b}}\) is already in the subspace, projecting it again returns \(\hat{\mathbf{b}}\) unchanged. Algebraically:

$$P^2 = A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top \cdot A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top = A(A^\top A)^{-1}(A^\top A)(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top = A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top = P$$

Symmetry \((P^\top = P)\): The projection direction is the same as the projection's own range. Algebraically \(P^\top = (A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top)^\top = A((A^\top A)^{-1})^\top A^\top = A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top = P\) (since \(A^\top A\) is symmetric, so is its inverse).

🔑 Characterisation Theorem

A matrix \(P\) is an orthogonal projector if and only if it is symmetric (\(P^\top = P\)) and idempotent (\(P^2 = P\)). No other conditions needed. These two properties together force \(P\) to project orthogonally onto its column space.

The Complementary Projector

The matrix \(I - P\) projects onto the orthogonal complement (the null space of \(A^\top\)). It is also symmetric and idempotent: \((I-P)^2 = I - 2P + P^2 = I - P\). Every vector splits cleanly: \(\mathbf{b} = P\mathbf{b} + (I-P)\mathbf{b}\), where the two parts are perpendicular.

The Hat Matrix in Least Squares

In regression with design matrix \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}\), the OLS fitted values are \(\hat{\mathbf{y}} = X\hat{\beta} = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top \mathbf{y} = H\mathbf{y}\). The matrix \(H = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\) is exactly the projection matrix onto the column space of \(X\), nicknamed the hat matrix because it "puts a hat on \(y\)." The residuals \(\mathbf{e} = (I - H)\mathbf{y}\) live in the orthogonal complement.

⚠️ Common Confusion

The formula \(P = A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\) requires \(A^\top A\) to be invertible, i.e., the columns of \(A\) must be linearly independent. If \(A\) has dependent columns, use a pseudoinverse: \(P = A A^+\). In numerical code, always prefer QR decomposition: \(P = QQ^\top\) where \(A = QR\).

The Math — Full Derivation & Numerical Trace
▼

Setup: projecting \(\mathbf{b}\) onto \(\operatorname{col}(A)\)

Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 1 \\ 0\end{bmatrix}\) (a column vector spanning a line in \(\mathbb{R}^3\)) and \(\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix}1 \\ 2 \\ 3\end{bmatrix}\).

Step 1 — Compute AᵀA (scalar here, since A is a column vector)
AᵀA = [1, 1, 0] · [1, 1, 0]ᵀ = 1·1 + 1·1 + 0·0 = 2
Step 2 — Compute (AᵀA)⁻¹ Aᵀb
Aᵀb = [1, 1, 0] · [1, 2, 3]ᵀ = 1 + 2 + 0 = 3 x̂ = (AᵀA)⁻¹ Aᵀb = 3/2 = 1.5
Step 3 — Projection b̂ = A x̂
b̂ = [1, 1, 0]ᵀ · 1.5 = [1.5, 1.5, 0]ᵀ
Step 4 — Residual r = b − b̂
r = [1, 2, 3]ᵀ − [1.5, 1.5, 0]ᵀ = [−0.5, 0.5, 3]ᵀ
Step 5 — Verify orthogonality: Aᵀr = 0?
Aᵀr = [1, 1, 0] · [−0.5, 0.5, 3]ᵀ = −0.5 + 0.5 + 0 = 0 ✓
Step 6 — Projection matrix P = A(AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀ
P = (1/2)·[1,1,0]ᵀ·[1,1,0] = [[0.5, 0.5, 0], [0.5, 0.5, 0], [0.0, 0.0, 0]]
Step 7 — Verify idempotency: P² = P?
P² = P·P = (since P is a rank-1 outer product scaled by 1/(AᵀA)): (1/2)·A·Aᵀ · (1/2)·A·Aᵀ = (1/4)·A·(AᵀA)·Aᵀ = (1/4)·A·2·Aᵀ = (1/2)·A·Aᵀ = P ✓

Eigenvalues of a Projection Matrix

Because \(P^2 = P\), the eigenvalues \(\lambda\) of \(P\) satisfy \(\lambda^2 = \lambda\), so \(\lambda \in \{0, 1\}\). Eigenvectors for \(\lambda=1\) are vectors inside the subspace; eigenvectors for \(\lambda=0\) are vectors orthogonal to the subspace. The rank of \(P\) equals the dimension of the projected subspace.

📐 Key Formulas

Projection onto col(A): \(P = A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\)

Via QR (numerically stable): \(P = QQ^\top\) where \(A = QR\)

Residual projector: \(I - P\) (projects onto null space of \(A^\top\))

Least-squares hat matrix: \(H = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\), so \(\hat{\mathbf{y}} = H\mathbf{y}\)

Why Symmetry + Idempotency Implies Orthogonality

Suppose \(P^2 = P\) and \(P^\top = P\). Take any vector \(\mathbf{v}\) and split it: \(\mathbf{v} = P\mathbf{v} + (I-P)\mathbf{v}\). Their inner product is \(\langle P\mathbf{v},\,(I-P)\mathbf{v}\rangle = (P\mathbf{v})^\top(I-P)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}^\top P^\top(I-P)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}^\top P(I-P)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}^\top(P - P^2)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}^\top \cdot \mathbf{0} = 0\). So the two parts are orthogonal — proving the projection is orthogonal.

Code — From Scratch & Production
▼

From Scratch (NumPy)

import numpy as np
def projection_matrix(A: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
"""Compute the orthogonal projection matrix onto col(A).
Uses QR decomposition for numerical stability.
A: (m, n) matrix with linearly independent columns (full column rank).
Returns: (m, m) symmetric idempotent projection matrix P."""
# QR decomposition: A = Q R, Q has orthonormal columns
Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(A) # Q shape: (m, n), columns orthonormal
return Q @ Q.T # P = QQ^T, equivalent to A(A^TA)^-1 A^T
def project_vector(A: np.ndarray, b: np.ndarray):
"""Project b onto col(A). Returns (b_hat, residual, P)."""
P = projection_matrix(A)
b_hat = P @ b # projection: nearest point in col(A)
residual = b - b_hat # orthogonal complement component
return b_hat, residual, P
# --- Numerical checks ---
A = np.array([[1, 0], [0, 1], [1, 1]], dtype=float) # 3D, 2-column subspace
b = np.array([2, 3, 5], dtype=float)
b_hat, r, P = project_vector(A, b)
print("b_hat:", b_hat) # [2.33, 2.67, 5.0] approx
print("Residual:", r) # [-0.33, 0.33, 0.0] approx
print("A^T r ≈ 0:", A.T @ r) # near [0, 0] — orthogonality
print("P^2 - P ≈ 0:", np.max(np.abs(P@P - P))) # near 1e-16 — idempotency
print("P - P^T ≈ 0:", np.max(np.abs(P - P.T))) # near 1e-16 — symmetry

Hat Matrix in Regression (scikit-learn / NumPy)

import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import make_regression
X_raw, y, _ = make_regression(n_samples=50, n_features=3, noise=5, coef=True)
X = np.column_stack([np.ones(50), X_raw]) # add intercept column
# Hat matrix H = X(X^TX)^-1 X^T (projects y onto fitted values)
Q, _ = np.linalg.qr(X) # numerically stable
H = Q @ Q.T # (50x50) hat matrix
y_hat = H @ y # OLS fitted values via projection
residuals = y - y_hat # = (I - H) y
# Leverage scores: diagonal of H
leverage = np.diag(H) # h_ii in [1/n, 1]; high = influential point
print("Sum of leverages = p:", np.sum(leverage)) # = number of params (4)
print("Max leverage:", np.max(leverage)) # flag if > 2p/n = 0.16
# ⚠️ Gotcha: never explicitly form H for large n (50×50 fine, 100k×100k = disaster)
# Instead compute y_hat = X @ np.linalg.lstsq(X, y, rcond=None)[0] directly
⚠️ Numerical Gotchas

Never form \(P = A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\) explicitly by inverting \(A^\top A\) — this is numerically unstable when \(A\) is nearly rank-deficient. Always use numpy.linalg.qr and then \(P = QQ^\top\). For tall matrices or regression, numpy.linalg.lstsq is more efficient than forming \(P\) at all.

Interview Q&A, Trade-offs & Related Topics
▼

Interview Questions (Easy → Hard)

Q1 (Easy): What is an orthogonal projection?
A: It maps a vector \(\mathbf{b}\) to the nearest point \(\hat{\mathbf{b}}\) in a subspace, where "nearest" is measured in Euclidean distance. The error \(\mathbf{b} - \hat{\mathbf{b}}\) is perpendicular to the subspace.

Q2 (Easy): What are the two defining properties of a projection matrix?
A: Idempotency (\(P^2 = P\)) — applying it twice does nothing new; and symmetry (\(P^\top = P\)) — these two together characterise orthogonal projectors.

Q3 (Medium): How does the projection matrix relate to least squares?
A: OLS finds \(\hat{\beta}\) minimising \(\|\mathbf{y} - X\beta\|^2\). The solution \(\hat{\mathbf{y}} = X\hat{\beta} = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top \mathbf{y} = H\mathbf{y}\) is exactly the orthogonal projection of \(\mathbf{y}\) onto the column space of \(X\). The hat matrix \(H\) is the projection matrix. OLS minimises distance by projecting.

Q4 (Medium): What are the eigenvalues of a projection matrix?
A: Only 0 and 1. From \(P^2 = P\) we get \(\lambda^2 = \lambda\), so \(\lambda \in \{0, 1\}\). Eigenvectors for \(\lambda=1\) lie in the column space (subspace being projected onto); eigenvectors for \(\lambda=0\) lie in the orthogonal complement. The rank of \(P\) equals the dimension of the subspace.

Q5 (Hard): Why is the QR decomposition preferred over the normal equations?
A: Forming \(A^\top A\) squares the condition number: \(\kappa(A^\top A) = \kappa(A)^2\). For a nearly rank-deficient \(A\), this makes solving the normal equations numerically disastrous. QR keeps condition number at \(\kappa(A)\) and gives \(P = QQ^\top\) directly.

Q6 (Hard): What is the leverage score in regression, and why is it the diagonal of H?
A: The leverage \(h_{ii} = \mathbf{x}_i^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}\mathbf{x}_i\) measures how much observation \(i\) can influence its own fitted value. Since \(H\mathbf{e}_i = h_{ii}\mathbf{e}_i\) (where \(\mathbf{e}_i\) is a basis vector), \(h_{ii}\) is the \(i\)-th diagonal of \(H\). High leverage points can single-handedly steer the regression line.

🔑 PhD-Level Insight

Projection matrices are the atomic unit of linear algebra decompositions. Any symmetric matrix \(A\) with eigendecomposition \(A = Q\Lambda Q^\top\) can be written \(A = \sum_i \lambda_i \mathbf{q}_i\mathbf{q}_i^\top\) — a spectral decomposition into rank-1 projectors. Each \(P_i = \mathbf{q}_i\mathbf{q}_i^\top\) is the projection onto the \(i\)-th eigenvector. PCA, SVD, and the pseudoinverse all hinge on this.
