Understanding Semidefinite Programming
▼

What is it? Semidefinite Programming (SDP) is a generalization of linear programming where, instead of requiring variables to be non-negative scalars, you require that a symmetric matrix of variables be positive semidefinite (PSD) — meaning all its eigenvalues are ≥ 0. You still optimize a linear objective. This seemingly small change unlocks enormous modeling power.

Analogy: In a linear program (LP), the feasible region is a polygon — a flat-sided shape. In SDP, the feasible region is a spectrahedron: the intersection of the cone of PSD matrices with a linear affine subspace. Think of it like an LP, but instead of "all sides flat," the constraint region has gently curved faces that come from the PSD boundary. You're sliding an objective hyperplane over this curved, smooth region to find its highest (or lowest) touching point.

Why it's everywhere: SDP is the backbone of combinatorial optimization relaxations (Max-Cut, graph coloring), control theory (Lyapunov stability), quantum information, polynomial optimization, and robust statistics. It is solvable in polynomial time via interior-point methods — making it a practical and powerful tool.

💡 If you remember one thing

An SDP optimizes \(\langle C, X \rangle = \text{tr}(C^\top X)\) subject to \(X \succeq 0\) (positive semidefinite) and \(\langle A_i, X \rangle = b_i\). The feasible set is a spectrahedron — a convex body with curved faces shaped by the PSD cone. Interior-point methods solve it in \(O(m \cdot n^{3.5})\) time. The Goemans–Williamson Max-Cut algorithm uses SDP to achieve a \(0.878\)-approximation of the integer optimum.

The SDP Standard Form & the PSD Cone
▼

Standard Primal Form

The canonical SDP is written in terms of an \(n \times n\) symmetric matrix variable \(X\):

$$\min_{X \in \mathbb{S}^n} \langle C, X \rangle \quad \text{subject to} \quad \langle A_i, X \rangle = b_i,\ i=1,\ldots,m, \quad X \succeq 0$$

where \(\langle A, B \rangle = \text{tr}(A^\top B) = \sum_{i,j} A_{ij} B_{ij}\) is the matrix inner product, \(\mathbb{S}^n\) is the space of \(n{\times}n\) real symmetric matrices, and \(X \succeq 0\) means \(X\) is positive semidefinite (all eigenvalues \(\geq 0\), equivalently \(v^\top X v \geq 0\) for all \(v\)).

The PSD Cone \(\mathbb{S}^n_+\)

The set of \(n \times n\) PSD matrices forms a proper convex cone: closed under non-negative linear combinations and limits. Its boundary consists of matrices with at least one zero eigenvalue (rank-deficient PSD matrices). The interior of the cone is the set of strictly positive definite (PD) matrices.

The Spectrahedron = PSD Cone ∩ Affine Subspace

The feasible set of an SDP is a spectrahedron: an intersection of the PSD cone with an affine subspace (given by the equality constraints). For the 2×2 case with \(\text{tr}(X)=1\), the spectrahedron is the set \(\{(a,b) : a \geq 0,\ 1-a \geq 0,\ a(1-a)-b^2 \geq 0\}\) — a filled ellipse (actually a circle) in the \((a,b)\) plane. The boundary \(b^2 = a(1-a)\) corresponds to PSD matrices with one zero eigenvalue.

Dual SDP

Every SDP has a dual. The dual of the canonical primal (minimizing over \(X\)) is:

$$\max_{y \in \mathbb{R}^m} b^\top y \quad \text{subject to} \quad C - \sum_{i=1}^m y_i A_i \succeq 0$$

Under Slater's condition (strict feasibility for both primal and dual), strong duality holds: primal optimal = dual optimal, and both optima are attained. This mirrors LP duality but with the PSD constraint replacing the non-negativity of the dual slack.

🔑 Key Property — Strong Duality

If both primal and dual are strictly feasible (Slater's condition), then strong duality holds: \(p^* = d^*\). Furthermore, the complementary slackness condition is \(X^*(C - \sum_i y_i^* A_i) = 0\) — the primal variable and dual slack are orthogonal in the PSD cone.

⚠ Duality Gap Can Be Nonzero

Unlike LP, SDP duality gaps can occur when Slater's condition fails. The primal or dual may be infeasible or the optima may not be attained even when the optimal value is finite. Always check strict feasibility before assuming strong duality.

The Goemans–Williamson SDP Relaxation (Max-Cut)
▼

Max-Cut: The Integer Problem

Given an undirected weighted graph \(G=(V,E,w)\), partition vertices into two sets \(S\) and \(\bar{S}\) to maximize the total weight of edges crossing the cut. In \(\pm 1\) variables \(x_i \in \{-1,+1\}\):

$$\text{MAXCUT} = \max_{x \in \{-1,+1\}^n} \frac{1}{4} \sum_{(i,j)\in E} w_{ij}(1 - x_i x_j)$$

This is NP-hard. The key observation: \(\frac{1-x_i x_j}{2} = 1\) if edge \((i,j)\) is cut, \(0\) otherwise (since \(x_i x_j = \pm 1\)).

The SDP Relaxation: Lift to Vectors

Replace each scalar \(x_i \in \{-1,+1\}\) with a unit vector \(v_i \in \mathbb{R}^n\), \(\|v_i\|=1\). The dot product \(x_ix_j = v_i \cdot v_j\) when \(x\) is scalar. The SDP relaxation is:

$$\max_{v_1,\ldots,v_n \in \mathbb{R}^n} \frac{1}{4}\sum_{(i,j)\in E} w_{ij}(1-v_i^\top v_j) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad v_i^\top v_i = 1 \ \forall i$$

This is equivalent to the SDP in the Gram matrix \(X = VV^\top\) where \(V = [v_1|\cdots|v_n]^\top\):

$$\max_{X \in \mathbb{S}^n_+} \frac{1}{4}\sum_{(i,j)\in E} w_{ij}(1-X_{ij}) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad X_{ii} = 1\ \forall i,\quad X \succeq 0$$

The SDP is a relaxation: every integer solution \(x\) gives a feasible Gram matrix (rank-1 PSD), but the SDP also allows higher-rank solutions, so \(\text{SDP}^* \geq \text{MAXCUT}^*\).

Rounding: The Hyperplane Rounding Trick

Given the SDP optimal vectors \(v_1^*,\ldots,v_n^*\), draw a random unit vector \(r\) uniformly from \(\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\). Assign \(x_i = \text{sign}(r \cdot v_i)\). Then:

$$\Pr[(i,j) \text{ cut}] = \frac{\arccos(v_i^* \cdot v_j^*)}{\pi}$$

Since \(\frac{1-v_i^* \cdot v_j^*}{2}\) is the SDP contribution of edge \((i,j)\), the approximation ratio is:

$$\alpha = \min_{\theta \in [0,\pi]} \frac{2\arccos(\cos\theta)/\pi}{(1-\cos\theta)/2} = \min_{\theta \in [0,\pi]} \frac{2\theta}{\pi(1-\cos\theta)} \approx 0.87856\ldots$$

Using the standard \(\tfrac{1}{2}\) scaling: each of 6 edges contributes \(\tfrac{1}{2}(1-(-\tfrac{1}{3})) = \tfrac{2}{3}\), so \(\text{SDP}^* = 6 \times \tfrac{2}{3} = 4\). The integer Max-Cut of \(K_4\): the partition \(\{0,1\}|\{2,3\}\) cuts all \(4\) crossing edges, each contributing \(\tfrac{1}{2}(1-(-1))=1\), so integer optimum \(= 4\). The SDP is tight for \(K_4\): \(\text{SDP}^* = \text{INT}^* = 4\). No integrality gap; GW ratio for this instance = 1.0. This tight case arises because \(K_4\) is a perfect bipartite-like structure where the tetrahedral SDP vectors already lie on a hyperplane cut.

∑ SDP Complexity

An SDP with \(n \times n\) matrix variable and \(m\) constraints is solvable to precision \(\epsilon\) in time \(O\bigl(m \cdot n^{3.5} \cdot \log(1/\epsilon)\bigr)\) using interior-point methods (SDPT3, SeDuMi, MOSEK). This is polynomial — a key advantage over NP-hard integer programs.

Key Takeaways & When to Use SDP
▼

When to Reach for SDP

  • Combinatorial relaxations — Max-Cut, graph coloring, MIMO detection: lift integer variables to unit vectors, solve SDP, round.
  • Control & stability — Lyapunov stability of linear systems: find a PSD matrix \(P\) s.t. \(A^\top P + PA \prec 0\) (SDP feasibility).
  • Polynomial optimization (SOS) — Certify that a polynomial is non-negative via a sum-of-squares decomposition (Lasserre hierarchy).
  • Quantum information — Semidefinite programs appear in fidelity computation, channel capacity, and entanglement witnesses.
  • Sensor network localization, matrix completion — PSD constraints model geometry or low-rank structure.

💡 The Geometry in One Sentence

An SDP slides a linear objective hyperplane over a spectrahedron — a convex body whose faces are curved by the PSD cone boundary — finding where the hyperplane is tangent. Interior-point methods follow the central path inside the spectrahedron to the optimal boundary point.
