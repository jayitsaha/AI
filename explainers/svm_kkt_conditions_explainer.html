1
Understanding KKT in SVMs
▼

A Support Vector Machine finds the widest possible margin between two classes. But "widest margin" is a constrained optimization problem — you maximize the margin subject to every training point staying on the correct side. The KKT (Karush–Kuhn–Tucker) conditions are the optimality conditions that any solution to this constrained problem must satisfy.

Think of it like this: imagine stretching a rubber band between two clusters of dots. The band wants to be as wide as possible (maximize margin), but it's pinned by the dots closest to it — the support vectors. KKT conditions mathematically encode which dots are "touching the band" (\(\alpha_i > 0\)) and which dots are safely away (\(\alpha_i = 0\)).

💡 The One Thing to Remember

Complementary slackness says \(\alpha_i \bigl[y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b) - 1\bigr] = 0\) for every point. Either the point is on the margin (functional margin = 1, so \(\alpha_i \ge 0\)) or it is strictly inside (functional margin > 1, so \(\alpha_i = 0\)). Points with \(\alpha_i > 0\) are the support vectors — they are the only training points that determine \(\mathbf{w}\) and \(b\).

The hard-margin SVM primal is:

$$\min_{\mathbf{w},b}\;\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 \quad \text{s.t.}\;\; y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + b)\ge 1 \;\;\forall i$$

The Lagrangian introduces a dual variable \(\alpha_i \ge 0\) for each constraint:

$$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w},b,\boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 - \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i\bigl[y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i+b)-1\bigr]$$

The Five KKT Conditions — Internally
▼

At the saddle point of the Lagrangian, five conditions hold simultaneously. Together they are necessary and sufficient for optimality (because the SVM primal is convex and satisfies Slater's constraint qualification).

Key Concept — The Five KKT Conditions
  • Stationarity w.r.t. w: \(\nabla_\mathbf{w}\mathcal{L}=0 \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf{w}=\sum_i \alpha_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i\)
  • Stationarity w.r.t. b: \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial b=0 \;\Rightarrow\; \sum_i \alpha_i y_i = 0\)
  • Primal feasibility: \(y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i+b)\ge 1 \;\forall i\)
  • Dual feasibility: \(\alpha_i \ge 0 \;\forall i\)
  • Complementary slackness: \(\alpha_i\bigl[y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i+b)-1\bigr]=0 \;\forall i\)

What Complementary Slackness Means Geometrically

The product \(\alpha_i\bigl[y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i+b)-1\bigr]=0\) forces one of two cases for each training point:

CaseCondition\(\alpha_i\)MarginRole
Support vector\(y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i+b)=1\)\(\alpha_i>0\)Exactly on margin hyperplaneDetermines \(\mathbf{w},b\)
Interior point\(y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i+b)>1\)\(\alpha_i=0\)Beyond margin (safe)No contribution

Because \(\mathbf{w}=\sum_i \alpha_i y_i \mathbf{x}_i\) and \(\alpha_i=0\) for interior points, the decision boundary is entirely determined by the support vectors. You could delete every other training point and the solution would not change.

Finding b from Complementary Slackness

For any support vector \(j\) (where \(\alpha_j>0\)):

$$b = y_j - \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_j = y_j - \sum_i \alpha_i y_i (\mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_j)$$

In practice we average over all support vectors for numerical stability. This also shows why the dual formulation exposes the kernel trick: everything depends only on inner products \(\mathbf{x}_i^\top \mathbf{x}_j\), which can be replaced by \(k(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j)\).

3
Dual Derivation and Numerical Trace
▼

Substituting the stationarity conditions back into \(\mathcal{L}\) gives the Wolfe dual:

$$\max_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\;\sum_i \alpha_i - \frac{1}{2}\sum_i\sum_j \alpha_i\alpha_j y_i y_j (\mathbf{x}_i^\top\mathbf{x}_j) \quad\text{s.t.}\;\alpha_i\ge0,\;\sum_i\alpha_i y_i=0$$

This is a convex QP in \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\). By strong duality (Slater's condition is satisfied for linearly separable data), the duality gap is zero, so dual optimum = primal optimum.

  • Point A: \(\alpha_A=0.5>0\). Margin \(= y_A(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_A+b)=(+1)(1.5-0.5)=1.0\). Product \(= 0.5\cdot(1-1)=0\). ✓
  • Point B: \(\alpha_B=0\). Margin \(= (+1)(2+1.5-0.5)=3\). Product \(= 0\cdot(3-1)=0\). ✓

⚠️ Common Mistake

KKT conditions guarantee global optimality only because the SVM primal is convex. For non-convex problems (e.g., neural networks), KKT conditions give stationarity but not global optimality. Also: in the soft-margin SVM, each point has two dual variables (\(\alpha_i\) and \(\mu_i\)), and complementary slackness has three cases: SVs on margin, SVs inside margin (slack \(>0\)), and non-SVs.

Key Takeaways & When This Matters
▼
  • Only support vectors matter: \(\mathbf{w}=\sum_{\alpha_i>0}\alpha_i y_i\mathbf{x}_i\) — the sum is sparse in practice.
  • Kernel trick is born from KKT: The dual objective uses only \(\mathbf{x}_i^\top\mathbf{x}_j\) — replace with \(k(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j)\) for free.
  • Soft-margin extends cleanly: Add slack \(\xi_i\ge0\), add \(\mu_i(C-\alpha_i)=0\) as a second complementary slackness condition; \(0<\alpha_i
  • Strong duality requires convexity + Slater: This is why KKT gives the global optimum for SVMs but only a local condition for neural nets.
