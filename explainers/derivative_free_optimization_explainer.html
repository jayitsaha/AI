Understanding Derivative-Free Optimization
▼

What is it? Most optimization algorithms (gradient descent, Adam, LBFGS) require computing the derivative of the objective function — the mathematical "slope" that tells you which direction to step. But many real-world functions are non-differentiable, stochastic (noisy), or completely opaque (a simulation you can only query, not inspect). Derivative-free optimization (DFO) finds minima using only function evaluations — no gradients needed.

Analogy: Imagine you're blindfolded in a hilly landscape trying to find the lowest valley. With gradients, you'd feel the slope under your feet and walk downhill. Without gradients, you can only ask "how low am I right now?" at any position you choose to stand. DFO methods are intelligent strategies for choosing where to stand next based on the heights you've already measured.

💡 If you remember one thing

DFO methods optimize black-box functions using only \(f(x)\) evaluations. The three canonical approaches are: Nelder-Mead (geometric simplex — reflect/shrink a shape toward the minimum), Simulated Annealing (accept bad moves with probability \(e^{-\Delta E/T}\) to escape local minima), and CMA-ES (learn a multivariate Gaussian sampling distribution that concentrates probability mass near good regions).

When do you need DFO? Hyperparameter tuning of ML models (no gradient through the training loop), engineering design simulations, neural architecture search, noisy experimental data, RL policy search, and any objective with discrete or combinatorial structure.

2
Three Methods Explained
▼

Method 1: Nelder-Mead Simplex (1965)

A simplex in \(n\) dimensions is a geometric shape with \(n+1\) vertices — a triangle in 2D, a tetrahedron in 3D. Nelder-Mead maintains this simplex and evolves it toward the minimum through four geometric operations:

  • Reflection (α=1): Flip the worst vertex through the centroid of the rest. If the landscape is lower on the other side, accept.
  • Expansion (γ=2): If reflection was very successful, try stretching further in that direction.
  • Contraction (ρ=0.5): If reflection was worse than the current second-worst, try a point halfway between the centroid and the worst.
  • Shrink: If all else fails, pull all vertices except the best halfway toward the best vertex.
🔑 Nelder-Mead Key Property

No gradients or finite differences needed — the simplex feels the landscape by sampling \(n+1\) points. It adapts its shape to the local geometry, elongating along ridges and contracting in valleys. Works well for low-dimensional smooth problems (\(n \leq 20\)).

Method 2: Simulated Annealing (Kirkpatrick et al., 1983)

Inspired by the physical annealing of metals: slowly cooling a material so atoms settle into a low-energy crystalline configuration. The algorithm explores the search space by random perturbations, always accepting improvements but also accepting worse solutions with probability:

$$P(\text{accept}) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta E}{T}\right)$$

where \(\Delta E = f(\mathbf{x}_\text{new}) - f(\mathbf{x}_\text{current}) > 0\) is the cost increase and \(T\) is the "temperature" parameter. As \(T \to 0\) (cooling), this probability \(\to 0\) and the algorithm becomes greedy hill-climbing. The key is the cooling schedule: reduce \(T\) slowly enough to avoid getting trapped in poor local optima.

Method 3: CMA-ES (Evolution Strategy, Hansen & Ostermeier, 2001)

CMA-ES (Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy) maintains a multivariate Gaussian \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}, \sigma^2 \mathbf{C})\) and iteratively updates its mean \(\mathbf{m}\) and covariance matrix \(\mathbf{C}\) to make the distribution concentrate around high-fitness (low-cost) regions:

  1. Sample \(\lambda\) candidate solutions from \(\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}, \sigma^2 \mathbf{C})\)
  2. Evaluate \(f(\mathbf{x}_i)\) for each candidate
  3. Select the best \(\mu = \lambda/2\) candidates
  4. Update \(\mathbf{m} \leftarrow \sum_{i=1}^\mu w_i \mathbf{x}_{i:\lambda}\) (weighted mean of selected)
  5. Update \(\mathbf{C}\) to align with the successful search directions
🔑 CMA-ES Key Property

CMA-ES is invariant to rotations and rescalings of the search space — it discovers the problem's natural coordinate system automatically by adapting \(\mathbf{C}\). It is the strongest general-purpose DFO method for dimensions \(10 \leq n \leq 1000\).

The Mathematics Behind It
▼

Nelder-Mead: Formal Operations

Given sorted vertices \(\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_{n+1}\) with \(f(\mathbf{x}_1) \leq \cdots \leq f(\mathbf{x}_{n+1})\), the centroid of all but the worst is \(\bar{\mathbf{x}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{x}_i\). The four operations with canonical parameters \(\alpha=1, \gamma=2, \rho=0.5, \sigma=0.5\):

$$\mathbf{x}_r = \bar{\mathbf{x}} + \alpha(\bar{\mathbf{x}} - \mathbf{x}_{n+1}) \quad \text{(Reflect)}$$
$$\mathbf{x}_e = \bar{\mathbf{x}} + \gamma(\mathbf{x}_r - \bar{\mathbf{x}}) \quad \text{(Expand, if } f(\mathbf{x}_r) < f(\mathbf{x}_1)\text{)}$$
$$\mathbf{x}_c = \bar{\mathbf{x}} + \rho(\mathbf{x}_{n+1} - \bar{\mathbf{x}}) \quad \text{(Contract)}$$
⚠️ Common Misconception

Nelder-Mead has no convergence guarantee in \(n \geq 2\). McKinnon (1998) constructed a counterexample where it converges to a non-stationary point. Use it for low-dimensional, smooth, noiseless problems only.

Simulated Annealing: Convergence Theory

SA's acceptance criterion defines a Markov chain. If the temperature decreases as \(T_k = T_0 / \log(1+k)\) (logarithmic cooling), SA converges to a global minimum with probability 1 — but this schedule is impractically slow. In practice, geometric cooling \(T_k = \alpha^k T_0\) with \(\alpha \approx 0.95\) is used, trading the global guarantee for speed.

$$P(\text{accept } \mathbf{x}_\text{new}) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } f(\mathbf{x}_\text{new}) \leq f(\mathbf{x}) \\ e^{-(f(\mathbf{x}_\text{new}) - f(\mathbf{x}))/T} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

CMA-ES: Mean and Covariance Update

Let \(\mathbf{x}_{i:\lambda}\) denote the \(i\)-th ranked (sorted by fitness) candidate. The new mean is a weighted average of the top-\(\mu\) individuals:

$$\mathbf{m}^{(t+1)} = \sum_{i=1}^{\mu} w_i \,\mathbf{x}_{i:\lambda}^{(t)}, \quad w_i = \frac{\ln(\mu+\tfrac{1}{2}) - \ln i}{\sum_j (\ln(\mu+\tfrac{1}{2}) - \ln j)}$$

The covariance is updated using the rank-\(\mu\) update (simplified form with learning rate \(c_\mu\)):

$$\mathbf{C}^{(t+1)} = (1 - c_\mu)\mathbf{C}^{(t)} + c_\mu \sum_{i=1}^\mu w_i \mathbf{y}_{i:\lambda} \mathbf{y}_{i:\lambda}^\top, \quad \mathbf{y}_{i:\lambda} = \frac{\mathbf{x}_{i:\lambda} - \mathbf{m}^{(t)}}{\sigma^{(t)}}$$

The step size \(\sigma\) is adapted separately via cumulative step-size adaptation (CSA) using a path \(\mathbf{p}_\sigma\) that compares the actual step direction to its expected length under the isotropic Gaussian:

$$\sigma^{(t+1)} = \sigma^{(t)} \exp\!\left(\frac{c_\sigma}{d_\sigma}\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{p}_\sigma\|}{\mathbb{E}\|\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})\|} - 1\right)\right)$$
∑ Why Covariance Adaptation Works

If consecutive steps are correlated (same direction repeatedly), \(\mathbf{C}\) grows in that direction — the ellipse elongates toward the optimum. If steps are anti-correlated (oscillating), \(\mathbf{C}\) shrinks in that direction. CMA-ES is essentially second-order information recovery without computing Hessians.

Key Takeaways & When to Use
▼
Nelder-Mead

Best for: \(n \leq 10\), smooth, noiseless. No hyperparameters. Fragile in high-D. scipy default.

Simulated Annealing

Best for: discrete/combinatorial problems, escaping many local minima. Cooling schedule critical.

CMA-ES

Best for: continuous, \(10 \leq n \leq 1000\), ill-conditioned, noisy. Most powerful DFO for ML.

🎯 Decision Rule

Low-D, smooth → Nelder-Mead or COBYLA. Combinatorial / discrete → Simulated Annealing or genetic algorithms. Continuous, moderate-D, ML tuning → CMA-ES or Bayesian optimization (if evaluations are expensive). Never use DFO when gradients are available — gradient-based methods are orders of magnitude more efficient.
