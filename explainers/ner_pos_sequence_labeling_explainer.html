Understanding Sequence Labeling
▼

What is it? NER (Named Entity Recognition) and POS (Part-of-Speech) tagging both assign one label to every word in a sentence — but the "correct" label for a word depends on its neighbors, not just on the word itself. That's why both are framed as sequence labeling: score whole label sequences and decode the best one, rather than classifying each word alone.

Analogy: Picture proofreading with two highlighter sets. For POS, every word gets a grammar-role color (noun, verb, preposition...). For NER, whole phrases get boxed as "names" — "Barack Obama" is one pink box (a person), "Paris" is a green box (a place). You can't pick a highlight by staring at one word in isolation — "flew" is almost certainly a verb because the word right before it looks like the subject of a sentence.

💡 If you remember one thing

Sequence labeling scores the WHOLE tag sequence jointly — using an emission score per word and a transition score between neighboring tags — then finds the best sequence with the Viterbi algorithm: $$\hat{y} = \underset{y}{\operatorname{argmax}}\; P(y \mid x)$$ NER encodes entity spans as per-token BIO tags (\(\text{B-PER}\), \(\text{I-PER}\), \(\text{O}\)...); POS just assigns a grammar tag directly.

How It Works Internally
▼

BIO Tagging: Encoding Spans as Per-Token Labels

NER needs to output SPANS ("Barack Obama" is one entity), but sequence models output one tag per token. BIO bridges the gap: \(\text{B-X}\) begins an entity of type X, \(\text{I-X}\) continues it, \(\text{O}\) means outside any entity. "Barack Obama flew to Paris" becomes \(\text{B-PER I-PER O O B-LOC}\) — five tags, two decoded spans.

Emission + Transition Scores

Every tagger (HMM or CRF) combines two kinds of evidence: an emission score — how well tag \(y_t\) fits word \(x_t\) and its local features (capitalization, word shape, gazetteer membership) — and a transition score — how well tag \(y_t\) follows tag \(y_{t-1}\). Transition scores are exactly what encodes the BIO structural rule: a well-trained model learns a huge negative weight for illegal jumps like \(\text{O} \to \text{I-PER}\).

Design Decision: Why Decode the Whole Sequence?

Classifying each token independently can output structurally invalid sequences and ignores what a neighbor's tag reveals about the current word. Instead, the Viterbi dynamic program finds the single highest-scoring GLOBALLY-consistent tag sequence in \(O(TK^2)\) time — polynomial, not the \(O(K^T)\) of brute-force enumeration.

🔑 Key Property

An I-X tag is only legal immediately after a B-X or I-X of the SAME type X. This global structural constraint cannot be guaranteed by classifying tokens independently — it emerges from the transition potentials in the joint decoding.

Why It Works — The Math
▼

Viterbi Recursion (log-space)

Let \(\delta_t(j)\) be the score of the best tag sequence ending in tag \(j\) at position \(t\). The recursion combines the best predecessor with the current emission:

$$\delta_t(j) = \max_{i \in \mathcal{Y}} \big[\, \delta_{t-1}(i) + \psi_T(i,j) \,\big] + \psi_E(j, x, t)$$

with backpointer \(\text{bp}_t(j) = \operatorname{argmax}_i\, \delta_{t-1}(i)+\psi_T(i,j)\), so the optimal path is recovered by tracing backpointers from \(\operatorname{argmax}_j \delta_T(j)\) — no re-search needed.

CRF: Globally-Normalized Conditional Model

A linear-chain CRF models \(P(y\mid x)\) directly as a log-linear function of overlapping features, normalized over the WHOLE sequence space (fixing the "label bias" problem of locally-normalized models like MEMMs):

$$P(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \exp\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_k w_k\, f_k(y_{t-1},y_t,x,t)\Big)$$

Evaluation: Span-Level F1

NER is scored by EXACT span match (boundaries + type), not token accuracy:

$$P=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\quad R=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\quad F_1=\frac{2PR}{P+R}$$
⚠️ Common Pitfall

Most tokens are \(\text{O}\) in real text, so a tagger that predicts \(\text{O}\) everywhere can score 90%+ token accuracy with ZERO entity recall. Always evaluate NER with span-level F1 (e.g. seqeval), never per-token accuracy.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to reach for structured sequence labeling:

  • Labels depend on neighbors (POS grammar rules, BIO span structure) — independent per-token classifiers can't guarantee valid output
  • You need EXACT entity spans, not just per-word hints
  • Limited labeled data — the structural constraints act as useful inductive bias

When NOT to use flat BIO tagging:

  • Nested/overlapping entities (e.g. "Bank of [New York]") — flat BIO can't represent them; use span-based models
  • Cross-sentence or document-level dependencies — linear-chain models only see local + sentence context
💡 Evaluation Reminder

Always score NER with span-level F1 (exact boundary + type match), never token accuracy — the gap between the two can be enormous when most tokens are \(\text{O}\).
