Likelihood Ratio, Wald & Score Tests

Three asymptotically equivalent approaches to hypothesis testing — visualized geometrically on the log-likelihood curve
Advanced Probability & Statistics Hypothesis Testing Chi-squared Asymptotics
1
Understanding the Concept
▼

Imagine you flip a coin 20 times and get 14 heads. The coin might be fair (\(p_0 = 0.5\)) or it might be biased. You want to test whether the data are consistent with fairness, or whether the evidence is strong enough to reject that null hypothesis.

Three classical tests answer this — they all end up producing a test statistic that follows a \(\chi^2\) distribution asymptotically, but each starts from a different angle on the log-likelihood curve:

  • Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT): Compare the log-likelihood height at \(\hat\theta\) vs \(\theta_0\). If the MLE fits much better, the null is suspect.
  • Wald Test: Measure how far \(\hat\theta\) is from \(\theta_0\), scaled by the curvature of the log-likelihood (Fisher information). If they are far apart in a precise metric, reject.
  • Score Test (Lagrange Multiplier): Look only at the slope of the log-likelihood at \(\theta_0\). If the derivative is huge there, the data are pulling hard away from \(\theta_0\).
💡 Core Insight

All three measure the same thing from different angles. By Wilks' theorem and related results, as \(n \to \infty\) each converges to \(\chi^2_r\) (where \(r\) = number of restrictions). They agree asymptotically but differ in small samples and in what information they require.

🔑 Key Concept

LRT needs both \(\hat\theta\) and \(\theta_0\). Wald needs only \(\hat\theta\) (and estimated curvature). Score needs only \(\theta_0\) (and observed slope there) — making it uniquely useful when the unrestricted MLE is hard to compute.

2
How Each Test Works
▼

Setup

We observe \(X_1,\ldots,X_n \overset{iid}{\sim} f(x;\theta)\) and test \(H_0: \theta = \theta_0\) vs \(H_1: \theta \neq \theta_0\) (one-dimensional, for clarity). The log-likelihood and score function are:

$$\ell(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log f(X_i;\theta), \qquad s(\theta) = \frac{\partial \ell(\theta)}{\partial \theta}, \qquad \mathcal{I}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial \theta^2}\right]$$

Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT)

Form the ratio of maximized likelihoods and take −2 log of it (equivalently, twice the log-likelihood difference):

$$\Lambda = 2\bigl[\ell(\hat\theta) - \ell(\theta_0)\bigr] \;\overset{H_0}{\longrightarrow}\; \chi^2_1$$

This is the vertical drop on the log-likelihood curve from the MLE peak down to the null value, scaled by 2. Reject if \(\Lambda > \chi^2_{1,\alpha}\).

Wald Test

Start from the MLE. Use its asymptotic normality: \(\hat\theta \approx \mathcal{N}(\theta_0,\, \mathcal{I}(\hat\theta)^{-1})\) under \(H_0\). Standardize:

$$W = (\hat\theta - \theta_0)^2 \,\mathcal{I}(\hat\theta) \;\overset{H_0}{\longrightarrow}\; \chi^2_1$$

This is the horizontal distance between \(\hat\theta\) and \(\theta_0\), scaled by the estimated curvature at the MLE.

Score Test (Rao / Lagrange Multiplier)

Evaluate the score function only at \(\theta_0\). Under \(H_0\), the score is asymptotically normal with mean 0:

$$S = \frac{s(\theta_0)^2}{\mathcal{I}(\theta_0)} \;\overset{H_0}{\longrightarrow}\; \chi^2_1$$

This is the squared slope of the log-likelihood at \(\theta_0\), normalized by Fisher information.

📐 Numerical Example — Binomial(20, p), observe 14 heads

\(H_0: p = 0.5\), \(\hat p = 0.7\). The log-likelihood is \(\ell(p) = 14\log p + 6\log(1-p)\).

LRT: \(2[\ell(0.7)-\ell(0.5)] = 2[-12.217-(-13.863)] = \mathbf{3.291}\)
Score: \(s(0.5) = 14/0.5 - 6/0.5 = 16\); \(\mathcal{I}(0.5)=n/[p(1-p)]=80\); \(S=16^2/80=\mathbf{3.200}\)
Wald: \(\mathcal{I}(\hat p)=n/[\hat p(1-\hat p)]=20/(0.7{\times}0.3)\approx95.24\); \(W=(0.2)^2{\times}95.24=\mathbf{3.810}\)

All three give \(\chi^2_1\) p-values near 0.07 — mild evidence against \(H_0\), not quite significant at 5%.

3
The Math — Wilks' Theorem & Asymptotics
▼

Why all three → χ²?

The key is a Taylor expansion of the log-likelihood around the MLE \(\hat\theta\). Write \(\delta = \hat\theta - \theta_0\) and note that the score at the MLE is zero (\(s(\hat\theta)=0\)):

$$\ell(\theta_0) \approx \ell(\hat\theta) + (\theta_0-\hat\theta)\underbrace{s(\hat\theta)}_{=0} - \tfrac{1}{2}(\theta_0-\hat\theta)^2 \cdot [-\ell''(\hat\theta)]$$
$$\Rightarrow\quad 2[\ell(\hat\theta)-\ell(\theta_0)] \approx \underbrace{(\hat\theta-\theta_0)^2 \,(-\ell''(\hat\theta))}_{=W} \;\approx\; \underbrace{\frac{s(\theta_0)^2}{-\ell''(\theta_0)}}_{=S}$$

Since \(-\ell''(\hat\theta) \xrightarrow{p} \mathcal{I}(\theta_0)\) and \(\sqrt{n}(\hat\theta-\theta_0)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\mathcal{I}(\theta_0)^{-1})\), all three statistics converge to the same \(\chi^2_1\) limit. The approximation error is \(O(n^{-1/2})\).

Wilks' Theorem (General)

$$\Lambda = 2[\ell(\hat\theta) - \ell(\hat\theta_0)] \;\xrightarrow{d}\; \chi^2_r \quad\text{as }n\to\infty$$

where \(r = \dim(\Theta) - \dim(\Theta_0)\) is the number of constraints, provided regularity conditions hold (twice-differentiable log-likelihood, true \(\theta\) in interior of parameter space, information matrix non-singular).

Score Statistic (full form for \(q\)-dim \(\theta\))

$$S = s(\theta_0)^\top \mathcal{I}(\theta_0)^{-1} s(\theta_0) \;\xrightarrow{d}\; \chi^2_r$$

Wald Statistic (full form)

$$W = (\hat\theta - \theta_0)^\top \mathcal{I}(\hat\theta)(\hat\theta - \theta_0) \;\xrightarrow{d}\; \chi^2_r$$
⚠️ Finite-Sample Ordering

In most regular models, for one-sided alternatives: \(W \geq \Lambda \geq S\) (Wald ≥ LRT ≥ Score). This means Wald tends to reject most often in small samples, Score least often. They agree asymptotically but can differ substantially for \(n < 100\).

🚫 Boundary & Non-Regularity

Wilks' theorem fails if the null is on the boundary of the parameter space (e.g., \(H_0: \sigma^2=0\)), if the model is non-identifiable, or if the Fisher information is singular. The null distribution then involves mixtures of \(\chi^2\) distributions.

Interview Questions (Easy → Hard)

Q1 (Easy) — What is the LRT?

Form \(\Lambda = 2[\ell(\hat\theta)-\ell(\theta_0)]\). Under \(H_0\), this is asymptotically \(\chi^2_r\). Reject if \(\Lambda > \chi^2_{r,\alpha}\). The intuition: if the MLE fits much better than the null, the null is implausible.

Q2 (Medium) — When would you prefer the Score test?

When the unrestricted MLE \(\hat\theta\) is hard or expensive to compute (e.g., very high-dimensional models, or constrained optimization under \(H_1\) is non-convex). The Score test only requires fitting the null model — evaluate \(s(\theta_0)\) and \(\mathcal{I}(\theta_0)\) there.

Q3 (Medium) — Why is the Wald test not invariant to parameterization?

The Wald test uses \((\hat\theta - \theta_0)^2 \mathcal{I}(\hat\theta)\). Under a reparameterization \(\phi = g(\theta)\), \(\hat\phi = g(\hat\theta)\) but the quadratic form changes via the delta method: \(\mathcal{I}_\phi \approx [g'(\theta)]^2 \mathcal{I}_\theta\). The resulting test statistic is numerically different. The LRT and Score test are invariant because they are functions of likelihoods, not of specific parameterizations.

Q4 (Hard) — Prove the three tests are asymptotically equivalent

Taylor-expand \(\ell(\theta_0)\) around \(\hat\theta\): since \(s(\hat\theta)=0\), \(\ell(\theta_0) \approx \ell(\hat\theta) - \frac{1}{2}(\hat\theta-\theta_0)^2 [-\ell''(\hat\theta)]\). Then \(\Lambda \approx (\hat\theta-\theta_0)^2 \mathcal{I}(\hat\theta) = W\). Similarly, expand \(s(\theta_0) \approx s(\hat\theta) - (\hat\theta-\theta_0)[-\ell''(\hat\theta)] = (\hat\theta-\theta_0)\mathcal{I}(\hat\theta)\), so \(S = s(\theta_0)^2/\mathcal{I}(\theta_0) \approx (\hat\theta-\theta_0)^2 \mathcal{I}(\hat\theta) = W\). All three differ by \(O_p(n^{-1/2})\) terms.

⚠️ Common Follow-Up Trap

"If all three are asymptotically equivalent, why do we have three tests?" Answer: they differ in small samples, computational cost, and invariance properties. Wald is ubiquitous (standard errors in regression ARE Wald), LRT is more reliable in small samples, Score is used when constrained estimation is cheaper.

6
Key Takeaways & Related Topics
▼
  • All three tests are asymptotically \(\chi^2_r\) under \(H_0\) with \(r\) constraints — this is Wilks' theorem and its analogues.
  • LRT = vertical drop (twice log-lik ratio); Wald = horizontal distance scaled by curvature; Score = slope at \(\theta_0\) normalized by information.
  • In practice: t-tests and z-tests in regression are Wald tests; deviance/likelihood-ratio tests in GLMs are LRT; Lagrange multiplier tests in econometrics are Score tests.
  • Ordering \(W \geq \Lambda \geq S\) commonly holds: Wald most liberal, Score most conservative in finite samples.
  • Score test is uniquely practical when the unconstrained MLE is expensive — only the null model needs to be fitted.
