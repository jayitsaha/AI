R² and Adjusted R²

Variance Explained — how well does your model fit the data?
Intermediate Classical ML · Regression Evaluation Part III · Model Evaluation
1
The 30-Second Version
▼

What is this? After fitting a regression model, you need a single number that tells you "how much of the outcome did my predictors actually explain?" R² (R-squared) is that number. It ranges from 0 to 1, where 1 means the model explains everything and 0 means it does no better than just predicting the average every time.

Real-world analogy. Imagine you're trying to predict tomorrow's temperature. The dumbest possible forecast is "same as the yearly average — say 15°C every day." Your smarter model says "15°C, ±5°C based on season and cloud cover." R² measures how much of the day-to-day swings your smart model accounts for, compared to just saying 15°C always.

💡 If you remember one thing

R² = 1 − SSres / SStot. It is the fraction of total variance that the model captures. Adjusted R² penalizes you for adding predictors that don't help, so it can decrease when a predictor is useless.

One-sentence summary. R² tells you what fraction of variance in the outcome your model explains; adjusted R² corrects that fraction for model complexity so you can fairly compare models with different numbers of predictors.

2
Core Concepts & The Math
▼

The three sums of squares

Given \(n\) observations \(y_1, \ldots, y_n\) with mean \(\bar{y}\) and fitted values \(\hat{y}_i\):

Key Formulas
$$SS_{tot} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \bar{y})^2 \quad \text{(total variance in } y\text{)}$$
$$SS_{res} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 \quad \text{(unexplained residual variance)}$$
$$SS_{reg} = \sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})^2 \quad \text{(variance captured by the model)}$$

For OLS: \(SS_{tot} = SS_{res} + SS_{reg}\), so these three are not independent.

R² (Coefficient of Determination)

$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{SS_{res}}{SS_{tot}} = \frac{SS_{reg}}{SS_{tot}}$$

Interpretation: if \(R^2 = 0.82\), the model explains 82% of the variance in the outcome. The remaining 18% is unexplained noise.

Numerical example. Suppose \(SS_{tot} = 500\) and \(SS_{res} = 90\). Then \(R^2 = 1 - 90/500 = 1 - 0.18 = 0.82\). The model captured 82% of the total variation.

Adjusted R² — the complexity-penalized version

Adding any predictor to an OLS regression — even a random noise variable — can only increase or maintain R². Adjusted R² corrects for this by penalizing for the number of predictors \(p\):

$$\bar{R}^2 = 1 - \frac{SS_{res}/(n-p-1)}{SS_{tot}/(n-1)} = 1 - (1 - R^2)\frac{n-1}{n-p-1}$$

where \(n\) is the number of observations and \(p\) is the number of predictor variables (not counting the intercept).

Same numerical example. \(n=50\), \(p=3\), \(R^2=0.82\).

$$\bar{R}^2 = 1 - (1-0.82)\cdot\frac{50-1}{50-3-1} = 1 - 0.18\cdot\frac{49}{46} \approx 1 - 0.1917 = 0.808$$

Adding a useless 4th predictor (\(p=4\), \(R^2\) stays 0.82): \(\bar{R}^2 = 1 - 0.18\cdot49/45 \approx 0.804\). It decreased — the predictor didn't earn its complexity cost.

⚠️ Common Pitfalls
  • R² never decreases when you add predictors. Always check adjusted R² for model comparison.
  • High R² ≠ good model. Overfitting, spurious regressions, and near-multicollinearity can all produce high R² on training data but poor predictions.
  • R² is not defined the same way for non-OLS models. For GLMs, neural nets, etc., "pseudo R²" variants exist but don't share the same interpretation.
  • R² can be negative if you compute it for a model that was not fit by minimizing SSres (e.g., a model forced through the origin applied to data with a non-zero mean).
  • Low R² doesn't mean a bad model. In noisy domains (economics, psychology, ecology), R² of 0.1–0.3 can be highly significant and practically important.

Internal Workings & Design Decisions
▼

Why this decomposition?

OLS minimizes \(SS_{res}\). When you fit an OLS model with an intercept, the Gauss–Markov conditions guarantee that the fitted values \(\hat{y}\) are the orthogonal projection of \(y\) onto the column space of \(X\). Pythagoras in that inner-product space gives \(\|y-\bar{y}\|^2 = \|y-\hat{y}\|^2 + \|\hat{y}-\bar{y}\|^2\), i.e., \(SS_{tot} = SS_{res} + SS_{reg}\). This orthogonal decomposition is why R² is guaranteed to lie in \([0,1]\) for OLS with intercept.

Why does adding a predictor always increase R²?

OLS searches over a larger parameter space when you add a predictor. The original (smaller model) is always a special case: set the new coefficient to zero. Since OLS minimizes \(SS_{res}\), it will find a solution that is at least as good as the old one — so \(SS_{res}\) can only decrease (or stay equal), which means R² can only increase.

Degrees-of-freedom correction in Adjusted R²

Adjusted R² replaces the raw sums with their mean-square (per-degree-of-freedom) counterparts: \(MS_{res} = SS_{res}/(n-p-1)\) and \(MS_{tot} = SS_{tot}/(n-1)\). If a new predictor reduces \(SS_{res}\) but consumes one degree of freedom, \(MS_{res}\) can increase if the reduction wasn't worth the cost. That is what causes \(\bar{R}^2\) to decrease.

Key Invariant

Adjusted R² increases if and only if the F-statistic for the added predictor exceeds 1. That is the exact threshold at which the information gain outweighs the degrees-of-freedom penalty.

Relationship to the F-test

The overall F-statistic for the regression is:

$$F = \frac{SS_{reg}/p}{SS_{res}/(n-p-1)} = \frac{R^2/p}{(1-R^2)/(n-p-1)}$$

This lets you compute the F-test for overall model significance directly from R². High R² with small \(n\) may not be statistically significant; low R² with large \(n\) often is.

Correlation link

In simple linear regression (one predictor), R² equals the square of the Pearson correlation coefficient between \(y\) and \(x\): \(R^2 = r_{xy}^2\). This relationship does not generalise — in multiple regression R² is the squared multiple correlation, not the sum or average of pairwise correlations.

Interview Quick-Fire & Comparison
▼

Interview Q&A (Easy → Hard)

Q1 (Easy) — What is R²?

A: R² = 1 − SSres/SStot. It is the proportion of total variance in the response that is explained by the model. An R² of 0.85 means the model accounts for 85% of the variability in y.

Q2 (Easy) — Why can't R² decrease when you add predictors?

A: OLS minimises SSres over a parameter space that strictly contains the smaller model (set new β = 0). The optimum in a larger space is always at least as good, so SSres ≤ SSres,small and R² ≥ R²small.

Q3 (Medium) — When does adjusted R² increase?

A: Adjusted R² increases if and only if the F-statistic for the added predictor exceeds 1. The threshold is exactly F = 1, not the critical value for significance. A predictor can be statistically insignificant yet still raise adjusted R², and vice versa.

Q4 (Hard) — Can R² be negative? When?

A: Yes, if the model was not fit by OLS. For example, if you impose a zero intercept but the true mean of y is non-zero, SSres can exceed SStot, giving R² < 0. Also happens when you evaluate a model trained on different data on a new test set (test R² can be negative). In sklearn, r2_score(y_true, y_pred) can return negative values for bad predictions.

Q5 (Hard) — Is R² a good metric for comparing models with different transformations of y?

A: No. If one model predicts log(y) and another predicts y directly, their SStot values are on different scales and R² values are not comparable. Back-transform predictions first, then compute metrics in the original scale — ideally RMSE or MAE rather than R².

Decision Guide

Use R² to communicate how much variance one specific model explains. Use Adjusted R² to compare regression models with different predictor counts on the same data. Use AIC/BIC or cross-validated RMSE for serious model selection — they penalise overfitting more rigorously.

Key Takeaways
  • R² = fraction of variance explained = 1 − SSres/SStot.
  • Adding predictors can only increase R² — never decrease it — for OLS.
  • Adjusted R² penalises extra predictors via a degrees-of-freedom correction.
  • Adjusted R² increases iff the new predictor's F-statistic > 1.
  • High R² doesn't mean good predictions on new data — always validate out-of-sample.
  • For non-OLS models (GLMs, tree models), use cross-validated R² or other metrics.
