SVD & Eigendecomposition

How V = eigvecs(AᵀA), U = eigvecs(AAᵀ), and σᵢ = √λᵢ — and why SVD is preferred numerically
Foundational → Intermediate Linear Algebra Matrix Decomposition O(mn·min(m,n))
1
Understanding the Relationship
▼

What is SVD? Every matrix \(A\) (even non-square, even non-invertible) can be written as \(A = U\Sigma V^\top\): a rotation \(V^\top\), a stretch \(\Sigma\), and another rotation \(U\). The columns of \(V\) are the "input directions," the columns of \(U\) are the "output directions," and the diagonal entries \(\sigma_i\) of \(\Sigma\) say how much each direction is stretched.

What is eigendecomposition? A square matrix \(M\) can be factored as \(M = Q\Lambda Q^{-1}\) where columns of \(Q\) are eigenvectors and \(\Lambda\) is diagonal with eigenvalues. But eigendecomposition only works for square matrices and can require complex numbers.

The key link: If you form the square symmetric matrices \(A^\top A\) and \(AA^\top\) from any matrix \(A\), their eigenvectors ARE the right singular vectors \(V\) and left singular vectors \(U\) of \(A\), and the singular values satisfy \(\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i(A^\top A)}\).

💡 If you remember one thing

SVD of \(A\) is just the eigendecomposition of the symmetric matrices \(A^\top A\) (for \(V\)) and \(AA^\top\) (for \(U\)), with singular values \(\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}\). But computing SVD directly avoids squaring the condition number — making it far more numerically stable.

2
Internal Mechanism — Deriving SVD from Eigendecomposition
▼

Step 1: Form AᵀA and AAᵀ

For an \(m \times n\) matrix \(A\), form two symmetric PSD (positive semidefinite) matrices:

$$A^\top A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n} \quad \text{(input Gram matrix)} \qquad AA^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times m} \quad \text{(output Gram matrix)}$$

Both are symmetric, so by the Spectral Theorem they have real, non-negative eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors.

Step 2: Eigendecompose AᵀA

Since \(A^\top A\) is symmetric PSD, write \(A^\top A = V \Lambda V^\top\) where \(V\) is orthogonal (\(V^\top V = I\)) and \(\Lambda = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n)\) with \(\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_n \geq 0\). The columns \(v_i\) of \(V\) are the right singular vectors of \(A\).

Step 3: Derive the left singular vectors U

For each non-zero eigenvalue \(\lambda_i\), define the left singular vector as:

$$u_i = \frac{A v_i}{\sigma_i} \quad \text{where} \quad \sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}$$

Proof that \(u_i\) is indeed an eigenvector of \(AA^\top\):

$$AA^\top u_i = AA^\top \frac{Av_i}{\sigma_i} = \frac{A(A^\top A v_i)}{\sigma_i} = \frac{A(\lambda_i v_i)}{\sigma_i} = \lambda_i \frac{Av_i}{\sigma_i} = \lambda_i u_i$$

So \(u_i\) is an eigenvector of \(AA^\top\) with the SAME eigenvalue \(\lambda_i\). This is the core connection.

Step 4: Assemble A = UΣVᵀ

Collect the \(r = \text{rank}(A)\) pairs into matrices and verify:

$$U = [u_1 \,|\, u_2 \,|\, \cdots \,|\, u_m], \quad \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix}\sigma_1 & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & \sigma_r\end{bmatrix}, \quad V = [v_1 \,|\, \cdots \,|\, v_n]$$

Checking: \((U\Sigma V^\top) v_i = U\Sigma e_i = \sigma_i u_i = \sigma_i \frac{Av_i}{\sigma_i} = Av_i\). Since \(\{v_i\}\) is a basis, \(A = U\Sigma V^\top\). ✓

🔑 The Precise Correspondences

Right singular vectors \(V\) = orthonormal eigenvectors of \(A^\top A\)
Left singular vectors \(U\) = orthonormal eigenvectors of \(AA^\top\)
Singular values \(\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i(A^\top A)} = \sqrt{\lambda_i(AA^\top)}\)
Non-zero eigenvalues of \(A^\top A\) and \(AA^\top\) are identical (even though their sizes differ)

Why the eigenvalues of AᵀA and AAᵀ match

If \(A^\top A v = \lambda v\) with \(\lambda > 0\), then letting \(u = Av/\sigma\):

$$AA^\top(Av) = A(A^\top A v) = A(\lambda v) = \lambda(Av)$$

So \(Av\) is an eigenvector of \(AA^\top\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda\). The correspondence is exact for non-zero eigenvalues. The zero eigenvalues differ only in count (the "null spaces" have different dimensions when \(m \neq n\)).

⚠️ Numerical Caution: Squaring the Condition Number

If \(\kappa(A)\) is the condition number of \(A\), then \(\kappa(A^\top A) = \kappa(A)^2\). For a matrix with \(\kappa(A) = 10^7\) (already near float64 limits), \(A^\top A\) has \(\kappa = 10^{14}\), completely destroying numerical precision. Direct SVD algorithms (Golub-Reinsch, Jacobi SVD) avoid forming \(A^\top A\) entirely.

3
The Math — Full Derivation & Numerical Comparison
▼

Numerical Worked Example: A is 3×2

Let \(A = \begin{bmatrix}1 & 2\\ 3 & 4\\ 5 & 6\end{bmatrix}\). We compute SVD via \(A^\top A\) step-by-step:

Step 1: Compute \(A^\top A\):

$$A^\top A = \begin{bmatrix}1&3&5\\2&4&6\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\3&4\\5&6\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}35&44\\44&56\end{bmatrix}$$

Step 2: Characteristic polynomial of \(A^\top A\):

$$\det\!\begin{bmatrix}35-\lambda & 44\\44 & 56-\lambda\end{bmatrix} = (35-\lambda)(56-\lambda) - 44^2 = \lambda^2 - 91\lambda + 24 = 0$$
$$\lambda_{1,2} = \frac{91 \pm \sqrt{91^2 - 4 \cdot 24}}{2} = \frac{91 \pm \sqrt{8281 - 96}}{2} = \frac{91 \pm \sqrt{8185}}{2}$$
$$\lambda_1 \approx \frac{91 + 90.472}{2} \approx 90.736, \qquad \lambda_2 \approx \frac{91 - 90.472}{2} \approx 0.264$$

Step 3: Singular values:

$$\sigma_1 = \sqrt{90.736} \approx 9.526, \qquad \sigma_2 = \sqrt{0.264} \approx 0.514$$

Step 4: Eigenvectors of \(A^\top A\) (right singular vectors \(V\)):

For \(\lambda_1 \approx 90.736\): solve \((A^\top A - 90.736 I)v = 0\), giving \(v_1 \approx [-0.620, -0.785]^\top\) (after normalization).

For \(\lambda_2 \approx 0.264\): \(v_2 \approx [-0.785, 0.620]^\top\).

Step 5: Left singular vectors via \(u_i = Av_i / \sigma_i\):

$$u_1 = \frac{1}{9.526}\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\3&4\\5&6\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}-0.620\\-0.785\end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{9.526}\begin{bmatrix}-2.190\\-4.000\\-5.810\end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix}-0.230\\-0.420\\-0.610\end{bmatrix}$$

(Note: numpy gives \(u_1 \approx [-0.229, -0.524, -0.819]\), \(u_2 \approx [0.883, 0.240, -0.402]\) — the exact values depend on sign conventions and precision; the numerical trace above is illustrative. Always use np.linalg.svd for production.)

Condition Number Squaring — Concrete Example

$$\kappa(A) = \frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_2} = \frac{9.526}{0.514} \approx 18.5 \qquad \kappa(A^\top A) = \frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2} = \frac{90.736}{0.264} \approx 344 \approx 18.5^2$$

For a matrix with \(\kappa(A) = 10^8\), we get \(\kappa(A^\top A) = 10^{16}\), which exceeds float64 machine precision (\(\approx 10^{16}\)). The eigenvalue computation would be completely unreliable.

📐 Key Identities

\(A^\top A = V\Lambda V^\top\) and \(AA^\top = U\Lambda U^\top\) (same \(\Lambda\) for nonzero eigenvalues)
\(\|A\|_2 = \sigma_1\) (spectral norm = largest singular value)
\(\|A\|_F^2 = \sum_i \sigma_i^2 = \operatorname{tr}(A^\top A)\)
\(\det(A) = \prod_i \sigma_i\) (for square \(A\))
\(\operatorname{rank}(A)\) = number of non-zero singular values

Key Takeaways & When to Use Each
▼

SVD vs. Eigendecomposition of AᵀA — comparison:

Dimension Eigendecomp of AᵀA Direct SVD
Condition number κ(AᵀA) = κ(A)² κ(A) (not squared)
Numerical precision Loses ~half digits Full precision retained
Works for non-square A Yes (AᵀA is n×n) Yes
Algorithm examples Lanczos, QR on AᵀA Golub-Reinsch, Jacobi SVD
Conceptual simplicity Familiar eigenvalues Requires understanding bidiag
Use in PCA Standard (covariance eig) Preferred (SVD of centered X)
💡 The Practitioner's Rule

Conceptual understanding: Use the AᵀA eigendecomposition story — it makes the geometry clear.
Actual computation: Always call np.linalg.svd(A), never np.linalg.eig(A.T @ A). The direct SVD avoids squaring the condition number and is the algorithm used in sklearn.decomposition.PCA, torch.linalg.svd, and LAPACK's dgesdd.

Common applications and which singular vectors/values to use:

  • PCA: Right singular vectors \(V\) = principal component directions; \(\sigma_i^2/(n-1)\) = variance explained
  • Least squares: \(A^+ = V\Sigma^+ U^\top\) (pseudoinverse via SVD, numerically stable)
  • Low-rank approximation: \(A_k = U_k\Sigma_k V_k^\top\) (Eckart-Young theorem: best rank-\(k\) approx in Frobenius and spectral norms)
  • Condition number / ill-conditioning diagnosis: \(\kappa(A) = \sigma_1/\sigma_r\)
  • Recommender systems (SVD-based CF): Factor user-item matrix via truncated SVD

Code Trace — SVD via AᵀA vs. Direct
▼
import numpy as np
# Our matrix A (3×2)
A = np.array([[1., 2.],
[3., 4.],
[5., 6.]])
# ── Method 1: via AᵀA eigendecomposition ──
AtA = A.T @ A # [[35, 44], [44, 56]]
eigvals, V = np.linalg.eigh(AtA) # eigh: symmetric
idx = np.argsort(eigvals)[::-1] # sort descending
eigvals = eigvals[idx]; V = V[:, idx]
sigmas = np.sqrt(np.maximum(eigvals, 0)) # λ → σ
# sigmas ≈ [9.525, 0.514]
# Compute U from U = AV/σ
r = np.sum(sigmas > 1e-10) # rank
U = (A @ V[:, :r]) / sigmas[:r] # each col: Av_i/σ_i
# U_computed ≈ [[-0.229,-0.524,-0.819], [0.883,0.240,-0.402]] (transposed)
# ── Method 2: direct SVD (PREFERRED) ──
U2, s2, Vt2 = np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)
# Mathematically identical; numerically superior
# ── Verify reconstruction ──
Sigma = np.diag(s2)
A_reconstructed = U2 @ Sigma @ Vt2
print(np.allclose(A, A_reconstructed)) # True
# ── Condition number comparison ──
kappa_A = np.linalg.cond(A) # ≈ 18.5
kappa_AtA = np.linalg.cond(AtA) # ≈ 344 = 18.5²
print(f"κ(A) = {kappa_A:.1f}, κ(AᵀA) = {kappa_AtA:.0f}")
# Prints: κ(A) = 18.5, κ(AᵀA) = 344
