Multiple Integrals & Change of Variables

Double/triple integrals, the Jacobian determinant, and how the Gaussian integral is cracked open by polar coordinates
Intermediate Calculus Math Foundations Core for ML
1
Understanding Multiple Integrals
▼

What is it? A single integral measures the area under a curve. A double integral measures the volume under a surface over a 2D region. A triple integral measures a 3D quantity (mass, charge, probability) over a 3D region. When the region has an inconvenient shape in Cartesian coordinates, a change of variables lets you switch to a simpler coordinate system — but you must multiply by the Jacobian determinant to account for how areas (or volumes) stretch or shrink in the new system.

Analogy: Imagine tiling a bathroom floor. In Cartesian coordinates, the tiles are perfect squares — easy to count. But if the room is circular, you waste tiles at the edges. Switch to "polar tiles" (wedge shapes). Each polar tile has a different area depending on how far from the center it is. The Jacobian is the conversion factor that tells you: "this polar tile at radius \(r\) actually covers an area of \(r\,dr\,d\theta\) in real space." Without the Jacobian you'd get the wrong volume.

💡 If you remember one thing

When you change variables \((x,y) \to (u,v)\), every area element transforms as \(dA = |J|\,du\,dv\), where \(|J| = \left|\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|\) is the absolute Jacobian determinant. In polar coordinates \((r,\theta)\), \(|J| = r\), giving \(dA = r\,dr\,d\theta\). This factor is what makes the Gaussian integral \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}dx = \sqrt{\pi}\) provable.

Core Theory & Definitions
▼

The Double Integral as a Volume

For a function \(f(x,y) \geq 0\) over a region \(R \subset \mathbb{R}^2\), the double integral is defined as the limit of Riemann sums:

$$\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA = \lim_{m,n\to\infty} \sum_{i=1}^m \sum_{j=1}^n f(x_i^*, y_j^*)\,\Delta x\,\Delta y$$

Geometrically: partition \(R\) into tiny rectangles of area \(\Delta x\,\Delta y\), erect a column of height \(f(x_i^*, y_j^*)\) on each, sum the volumes, and take the limit.

Fubini's Theorem — Switching to Iterated Integrals

Computing a double integral directly requires a 2D Riemann sum. Fubini's theorem lets us reduce it to two nested 1D integrals (in any order), provided \(f\) is continuous on \(R\):

$$\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA = \int_a^b \int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)} f(x,y)\,dy\,dx = \int_c^d \int_{h_1(y)}^{h_2(y)} f(x,y)\,dx\,dy$$

Triple Integrals

For \(f: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}\) over a 3D region \(E\):

$$\iiint_E f(x,y,z)\,dV = \int \int \int f(x,y,z)\,dz\,dy\,dx$$

Common uses in ML: computing the normalizing constant of multivariate distributions, computing expectations \(\mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{x})] = \int g(\mathbf{x})\,p(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}\), and deriving partition functions in probabilistic models.

The Jacobian Matrix and Its Determinant

Given a smooth, invertible map \(\mathbf{T}: (u,v) \mapsto (x,y)\) where \(x = x(u,v)\), \(y = y(u,v)\), the Jacobian matrix of \(\mathbf{T}\) is:

$$J = \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial u} & \dfrac{\partial x}{\partial v} \\[8pt] \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial u} & \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial v} \end{bmatrix}$$

Its determinant \(\det(J)\) measures the local area-scaling factor: a tiny rectangle \(du\,dv\) in \((u,v)\)-space maps to a parallelogram of area \(|\det(J)|\,du\,dv\) in \((x,y)\)-space.

🔑 Change of Variables Theorem

$$\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA = \iint_S f(x(u,v),\,y(u,v))\left|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right|du\,dv$$ where \(S\) is the pre-image of \(R\) under \(\mathbf{T}\). The absolute value ensures area is always positive regardless of orientation.

Polar Coordinates: The Most Important Example

The map \((r,\theta) \mapsto (x,y)\): \(x = r\cos\theta\), \(y = r\sin\theta\). The Jacobian:

$$J = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta \end{bmatrix}, \quad \det(J) = r\cos^2\theta + r\sin^2\theta = r$$

So \(dA = r\,dr\,d\theta\). The factor \(r\) makes geometric sense: a thin annular wedge at radius \(r\) with width \(dr\) and angle \(d\theta\) has area \(\approx r\,d\theta \cdot dr\).

The Gaussian Integral — Why Jacobian Matters
▼

The Gaussian integral \(I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\,dx\) cannot be solved by standard antiderivatives. The trick is to compute \(I^2\) as a double integral, then change to polar coordinates using the Jacobian.

Step 1: Square It

$$I^2 = \left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\,dx\right)\!\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-y^2}\,dy\right) = \iint_{\mathbb{R}^2} e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dA$$

Step 2: Switch to Polar — Apply Jacobian \(|J|=r\)

$$I^2 = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\infty} e^{-r^2}\cdot r\,dr\,d\theta$$

The factor of \(r\) from the Jacobian is crucial — without it, the integral would diverge. With it:

Step 3: Evaluate

$$I^2 = \int_0^{2\pi}d\theta \cdot \int_0^{\infty} r\,e^{-r^2}\,dr = 2\pi \cdot \left[\!-\tfrac{1}{2}e^{-r^2}\right]_0^{\infty} = 2\pi \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = \pi$$

Conclusion

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\,dx = \sqrt{\pi}, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2/2}\,dx = \sqrt{2\pi}$$

The second form explains why the normal distribution has the normalization constant \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}\): the denominator is exactly the integral of the unnormalized Gaussian. Every time you use a Gaussian/Normal distribution in ML, this derivation is implicitly at work.

📐 ML Connection

The multivariate Gaussian \(\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma})\) normalizes as \(\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\right)d\mathbf{x} = (2\pi)^{d/2}|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{1/2}\). This follows from a change of variables \(\mathbf{u} = \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1/2}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\) whose Jacobian determinant is \(|\boldsymbol{\Sigma}|^{1/2}\).

⚠️ Common Mistake

Forgetting the Jacobian factor when changing variables gives the wrong answer. A frequent error: writing \(\iint e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dA = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}\,dr\,d\theta\) (missing the \(r\)). This integral diverges — the Jacobian factor \(r\) is what makes it converge.

Computing the Jacobian Determinant (General)

import numpy as np
def jacobian_det(T_x, T_y, u, v, eps=1e-7):
"""Numerical Jacobian det of map (u,v) -> (T_x(u,v), T_y(u,v))."""
dx_du = (T_x(u+eps, v) - T_x(u-eps, v)) / (2*eps)
dx_dv = (T_x(u, v+eps) - T_x(u, v-eps)) / (2*eps)
dy_du = (T_y(u+eps, v) - T_y(u-eps, v)) / (2*eps)
dy_dv = (T_y(u, v+eps) - T_y(u, v-eps)) / (2*eps)
J = np.array([[dx_du, dx_dv], [dy_du, dy_dv]])
return np.linalg.det(J) # = dx_du*dy_dv - dx_dv*dy_du
# Polar: x=r*cos(t), y=r*sin(t) -> J det = r
T_x = lambda r, t: r * np.cos(t)
T_y = lambda r, t: r * np.sin(t)
r, theta = 2.0, np.pi/4
print(jacobian_det(T_x, T_y, r, theta)) # → 2.0 (= r)

Gaussian Integral via Monte Carlo Double Integral

import numpy as np
# Verify: double integral of exp(-(x^2+y^2)) over R^2
# in polar: int_0^2pi int_0^R exp(-r^2) * r dr dtheta -> pi as R->inf
def polar_double_integral(f_r, R_max=10, nr=1000, nt=1000):
"""Numerically integrate f(r)*r over [0,R_max] x [0,2pi]."""
r = np.linspace(0, R_max, nr) # radial samples
dr = r[1] - r[0]
dth = 2 * np.pi / nt # angular step
# Jacobian factor r already in integrand
radial = np.sum(f_r(r) * r * dr) # inner integral
return radial * nt * dth # outer integral: 2*pi
I_sq = polar_double_integral(lambda r: np.exp(-r**2))
print(f"I^2 = {I_sq:.6f} (exact: {np.pi:.6f})") # → 3.141593
print(f"I = {np.sqrt(I_sq):.6f} (exact sqrt(pi))") # → 1.772454

Triple Integral — Mass of a Sphere with Density ρ(r)=r

def sphere_mass(density_fn, R=1.0, n=200):
"""Spherical coords: dV = r^2 sin(phi) dr dphi dtheta. Jacobian = r^2*sin(phi)."""
r = np.linspace(0, R, n)
phi = np.linspace(0, np.pi, n) # polar angle
r3, p3 = np.meshgrid(r, phi)
dr, dp = r[1]-r[0], phi[1]-phi[0]
# Jacobian for spherical coords: r^2 * sin(phi)
integrand = density_fn(r3) * r3**2 * np.sin(p3)
return np.sum(integrand) * dr * dp * 2 * np.pi
M = sphere_mass(lambda r: r, R=1.0) # density = r
print(f"Mass = {M:.4f} (exact = pi = {np.pi:.4f})") # → 3.1416

ML uses of multiple integrals

  • Normalizing constants: \(Z = \int p^*(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}\) in probabilistic models — often intractable, motivating variational inference and MCMC
  • Expectations: \(\mathbb{E}_{p}[f(\mathbf{x})] = \int f(\mathbf{x})\,p(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}\) — reparameterization trick uses change of variables with the Jacobian
  • Flow models (Normalizing Flows): \(\log p_X(\mathbf{x}) = \log p_Z(\mathbf{z}) + \log\left|\det J_f^{-1}(\mathbf{x})\right|\) — the log Jacobian determinant appears explicitly in the loss
  • Entropy: \(H(X) = -\int p(\mathbf{x})\log p(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}\) — differential entropy of continuous variables
  • KL divergence: \(\mathrm{KL}(p\|q) = \int p(\mathbf{x})\log\frac{p(\mathbf{x})}{q(\mathbf{x})}\,d\mathbf{x}\)
💡 Hierarchy: 1D → 2D → nD

Single integrals → double integrals → triple integrals → n-dimensional integrals (measure theory). Every step up in dimension requires a Jacobian when changing coordinates. In ML, you usually work in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) with \(d\) potentially thousands, making analytical integration impossible — hence Monte Carlo, variational methods, and normalizing flows.
