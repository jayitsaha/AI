1
Understanding Convergence Modes
▼

What is this? When a sequence of random variables \(X_1, X_2, \ldots\) approaches a limit \(X\), there are four rigorously distinct ways to define what "approaches" means. They differ in how much control you have over the probability of deviation, and over which outcomes converge.

Analogy: Imagine a train that is "converging" to a destination city. Almost sure convergence means the train eventually stays on the track and never leaves — it gets there for sure (except possibly on a set of freak days with zero probability). Convergence in probability means that for every future departure, the chance of the train being far from the destination is small — but it might still occasionally detour. Convergence in distribution only says the schedule of arrivals looks like the destination's schedule — the train itself may be a completely different train running on the same timetable. Lp convergence says the average distance squared (or p-th power) between the train's position and the destination shrinks to zero.

💡 If you remember one thing

The strength ordering is: almost sure (a.s.) \(\Rightarrow\) in probability \(\Rightarrow\) in distribution; and \(L^p \Rightarrow\) in probability \(\Rightarrow\) in distribution. Almost sure and \(L^p\) are incomparable — neither implies the other. In distribution is the weakest.

2
The Four Convergence Modes — Precise Definitions
▼

Mode 1 — Almost Sure (a.s.) Convergence

We say \(X_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} X\) if

$$P\!\left(\omega : \lim_{n\to\infty} X_n(\omega) = X(\omega)\right) = 1$$

This is pointwise convergence on a set of probability 1. For all "typical" outcomes \(\omega\), the sequence eventually stays arbitrarily close to \(X(\omega)\). This is the strongest mode for a single realisation.

Intuition: "With probability one, you could watch any individual realisation of the sequence and it would converge to X."

Mode 2 — Convergence in Probability

We say \(X_n \xrightarrow{P} X\) if for every \(\varepsilon > 0\),

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} P\!\left(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon\right) = 0$$

The probability of a non-negligible deviation shrinks to zero, but it doesn't guarantee that for any fixed outcome the sequence converges. Deviations are allowed as long as they become increasingly rare.

Intuition: "The train is almost certainly near the destination for large n — but it might occasionally detour for any individual run."

Mode 3 — Convergence in Distribution (in Law)

We say \(X_n \xrightarrow{d} X\) if for every continuity point \(x\) of \(F_X\),

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} F_{X_n}(x) = F_X(x)$$

where \(F_{X_n}(x) = P(X_n \le x)\). This is the weakest mode — it only asks that the distributions converge, not the random variables themselves. \(X_n\) and \(X\) need not even be defined on the same probability space.

Intuition: "The histogram of \(X_n\) looks more and more like the histogram of \(X\), but the specific values may be completely unrelated."

Classic example: if \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\) and \(X_n = -X\) for all \(n\), then \(X_n \xrightarrow{d} X\) (same distribution by symmetry) but \(X_n - X = -2X \not\to 0\) in any pathwise sense.

Mode 4 — Convergence in Lp

For \(p \ge 1\), we say \(X_n \xrightarrow{L^p} X\) if \(X_n, X \in L^p(\Omega)\) and

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} \mathbb{E}\!\left[|X_n - X|^p\right] = 0$$

The most common case is \(p=2\) (mean-square convergence): \(\mathbb{E}[(X_n - X)^2] \to 0\). This measures the average squared distance and is natural in Hilbert space theory and statistics.

Intuition: "The expected energy of the difference goes to zero." Lp convergence controls the tail behaviour of \(X_n - X\) more tightly than in-probability convergence alone.

🔑 Formal Summary

Let \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)\) be a probability space and \(X_n, X\) be real-valued random variables. The four modes order by strength:

\(X_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} X\) iff \(P(\limsup_n \{|X_n - X| > \varepsilon\}) = 0\) for all \(\varepsilon > 0\), equivalently \(P(\sup_{k \ge n} |X_k - X| > \varepsilon) \to 0\) as \(n\to\infty\).

Note: a.s. ⇏ Lp and Lp ⇏ a.s. (they are incomparable)

Proof: a.s. ⇒ in probability

Suppose \(X_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} X\). Define \(A_\varepsilon = \{\omega : X_n(\omega) \not\to X(\omega)\}\) — this has \(P(A_\varepsilon) = 0\). For any \(\varepsilon > 0\),

$$P(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon) \le P\!\left(\bigcup_{k=n}^{\infty}\{|X_k - X| > \varepsilon\}\right) \to 0$$

because the events \(\bigcup_{k \ge n}\{|X_k - X|>\varepsilon\}\) decrease to a subset of \(A_\varepsilon\), which has measure zero. \(\square\)

Proof: Lp ⇒ in probability (Markov)

By Markov's inequality applied to \(|X_n - X|^p\):

$$P(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon) = P(|X_n - X|^p > \varepsilon^p) \le \frac{\mathbb{E}[|X_n - X|^p]}{\varepsilon^p} \to 0 \quad \square$$

Proof: in probability ⇒ in distribution

For any continuity point \(x\) of \(F_X\) and \(\delta > 0\):

$$F_{X_n}(x) = P(X_n \le x) \le P(X \le x+\delta) + P(|X_n - X| > \delta) \to F_X(x+\delta)$$

Similarly \(F_{X_n}(x) \ge F_X(x-\delta) - P(|X_n-X|>\delta)\). Squeezing as \(\delta \to 0\) and using continuity of \(F_X\) at \(x\) gives \(F_{X_n}(x) \to F_X(x)\). \(\square\)

Sharp Counterexamples

In prob ⇏ a.s.: The Typewriter Sequence. Let \(\Omega = [0,1]\) with Lebesgue measure. Index \(n = 2^m + k\) (\(0 \le k < 2^m\)) and set \(X_n(\omega) = \mathbf{1}_{[k/2^m,\,(k+1)/2^m]}(\omega)\). Then \(P(|X_n| > \varepsilon) = 1/2^m \to 0\) (in prob to 0), but for any \(\omega\), there are infinitely many \(n\) with \(X_n(\omega) = 1\) (one per level \(m\)), so \(X_n(\omega) \not\to 0\) for every \(\omega\). Not a.s.

a.s. ⇏ Lp: Escaping spike. Let \(X_n(\omega) = n \cdot \mathbf{1}_{[0,1/n]}(\omega)\). Then \(X_n \to 0\) a.s. (for any \(\omega > 0\), eventually \(1/n < \omega\)). But \(\mathbb{E}[|X_n|] = n \cdot (1/n) = 1\) for all \(n\), so \(X_n \not\xrightarrow{L^1} 0\).

In dist ⇏ in prob. Let \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\), \(X_n = -X\). Then \(X_n \xrightarrow{d} X\) (same distribution by symmetry), but \(P(|X_n - X| > \varepsilon) = P(2|X| > \varepsilon) \to P(|X| > \varepsilon/2) \ne 0\).

⚠️ Exception: in dist + constant limit ⟹ in prob

If \(X_n \xrightarrow{d} c\) where \(c\) is a constant (degenerate distribution), then \(X_n \xrightarrow{P} c\). This is because \(P(|X_n - c| > \varepsilon) = 1 - F_{X_n}(c+\varepsilon) + P(X_n \le c - \varepsilon) \to 0\). The LLN uses exactly this fact.

The Dominated Convergence Bridge

a.s. convergence plus a dominating condition yields Lp: if \(X_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} X\) and \(|X_n| \le Y\) for some \(Y \in L^p\), then \(X_n \xrightarrow{L^p} X\) (Dominated Convergence Theorem). This is the key tool for upgrading a.s. to Lp.

Key Takeaways & When Each Mode Appears
▼

Where each mode appears in ML/Statistics:

  • a.s. convergence: Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN) — \(\bar{X}_n \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \mu\). Used to justify that empirical averages are reliable for every run of an experiment.
  • In probability: Weak LLN, consistency of estimators — \(\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{P} \theta_0\). The standard definition of a consistent estimator. Also used in convergence of SGD iterates.
  • In distribution: Central Limit Theorem — \(\sqrt{n}(\bar{X}_n - \mu) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)\). Bootstrap validity, asymptotic normality of MLEs, hypothesis testing. The weakest mode, but often all that's needed for inference.
  • L2 (mean square): Hilbert space methods, RKHS, spectral analysis of stochastic processes, bias-variance tradeoff (\(\text{MSE} = \mathbb{E}[(\hat\theta-\theta)^2] \to 0\)). Also used in stochastic approximation (Robbins-Monro).

💡 Practical decision guide

For consistency of estimators → in probability is the standard requirement. For LLN statements in applications → a.s. is the gold standard but in probability suffices for most purposes. For asymptotic inference (confidence intervals, hypothesis tests) → in distribution (CLT) is what you need. For mean-square error analysis and stochastic approximation → L2 convergence.
