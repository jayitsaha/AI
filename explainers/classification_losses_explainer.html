Classification Losses: BCE, Categorical Cross-Entropy, Focal Loss & Hinge Loss

How models turn "how wrong was I?" into a gradient — and why the wording of that question changes everything.

Intermediate Loss Functions Must Know Deep Learning · Classical ML
1
Understanding the Concept

A classification loss turns "how wrong was the model?" into a single number that gradient descent can shrink. Every classifier ends its forward pass with raw scores (logits), and the loss function decides exactly how harshly to punish the gap between those scores and the true label.

Think of a quiz grader who also grades confidence: a student stating "90% sure, correct" earns a tiny penalty; "90% sure, wrong" earns a huge one. Binary cross-entropy (BCE) and categorical cross-entropy (CCE) are exactly this grading scheme — they compute -log(probability assigned to the correct answer). Focal loss is the same grader, but bored of easy questions: it mutes the penalty for answers the student already nails confidently, so grading time (gradient signal) goes to the hard questions instead. Hinge loss is a different grader entirely — a pass/fail bouncer at a club: as long as you're clearly on the right side of the velvet rope (the margin), it doesn't care how far — zero penalty. Only being on the wrong side, or too close to the rope, costs you.

💡 If you remember one thing

Cross-entropy losses reward calibrated confidence and punish confident mistakes exponentially; focal loss adds a dial to stop easy examples from drowning out hard ones; hinge loss abandons probability altogether in favor of a hard geometric margin.

2
How It Works Internally

BCE takes a sigmoid-squashed probability \(\hat{p}\in(0,1)\) and true label \(y\in\{0,1\}\):

$$L_{BCE} = -\left[y\log\hat{p} + (1-y)\log(1-\hat{p})\right]$$

Only one of the two terms is ever active per example — it always reduces to -log(probability assigned to the true class). Categorical cross-entropy is the same idea generalized to \(K\) classes with a softmax output and one-hot label:

$$L_{CCE} = -\sum_{k=1}^{K} y_k\log\hat{p}_k = -\log\hat{p}_{y}$$

Focal loss multiplies cross-entropy by a modulating factor \((1-\hat{p}_t)^\gamma\), where \(\hat{p}_t\) is the probability assigned to the true class. When the model is already confident and correct (\(\hat{p}_t \to 1\)), the factor collapses toward 0 and the example's contribution to the loss — and its gradient — nearly vanishes. When the model is wrong or unsure (\(\hat{p}_t\) small), the factor stays near 1, leaving the loss almost untouched.

$$L_{focal} = -\alpha_t(1-\hat{p}_t)^{\gamma}\log(\hat{p}_t)$$

Hinge loss skips probabilities entirely. With label \(y\in\{-1,+1\}\) and raw score \(z\), it measures the signed margin \(y\cdot z\):

$$L_{hinge} = \max(0,\ 1 - y\cdot z)$$

Once a point is correctly classified with margin \(\geq 1\), loss is exactly zero — hinge loss never rewards "more correct than necessary," which is why SVM solutions depend on a sparse set of boundary-hugging support vectors.

⚠️ Design decision

Why -log(p) and not, say, (1-p)? Because -log(p) is the unique loss (up to scale) whose minimization is mathematically identical to maximum-likelihood estimation of the model's parameters — it isn't an arbitrary shape, it falls out of probability theory.

3
Why It Works

Cross-entropy's real power shows up in its gradient. With \(\hat{p}=\sigma(z)\) and \(\sigma'(z)=\sigma(1-\sigma)\), differentiating BCE with respect to the raw logit \(z\) simplifies beautifully:

$$\frac{\partial L_{BCE}}{\partial z} = \hat{p} - y$$

The gradient is just the prediction error — no saturating derivative term survives. Compare this to mean-squared-error on a sigmoid output, where a leftover \(\sigma'(z)\) factor vanishes exactly when the model is most confidently wrong, killing the learning signal precisely when it's needed most. That single cancellation is why sigmoid+BCE (and softmax+CCE) became the default pairing for classifiers.

Hinge loss earns its keep differently: its subgradient is nonzero only for margin violators (\(y\cdot z < 1\)), which is exactly the max-margin objective from statistical learning theory — it directly optimizes the geometric gap between classes rather than a likelihood.

🔑 Key invariant

Minimizing average cross-entropy over a dataset is identical to maximum likelihood estimation under a Bernoulli/Categorical model — this is why cross-entropy, not an arbitrary "distance," is the theoretically correct default for probabilistic classifiers.

Key Takeaways

Default to BCE / categorical cross-entropy whenever you need calibrated probabilities and classes are reasonably balanced. Reach for focal loss when a tiny minority of hard examples is drowned out by a flood of easy ones (object detection, extreme imbalance) — its \(\gamma\) knob trades gradient signal away from anything the model already nails confidently. Reach for hinge loss when you want a pure max-margin decision boundary and don't need probability outputs at all (SVMs, some margin-based/robust objectives).

🎯 Decision rule

Balanced + need probabilities → BCE/CCE. Severe class imbalance → focal loss. Max-margin, non-probabilistic → hinge loss.
