Decomposing signals into frequencies — the bridge between time and frequency domains

Understanding the Fourier Transform
▼

What is it? The Fourier Transform is a mathematical operation that takes a signal — anything that varies over time or space, like sound, light, or temperature — and reveals which frequencies it contains and how strongly. It answers the question: "What mixture of pure tones makes up this complex sound?"

Analogy: Imagine you drop a stone in a pond and see complex ripples. The Fourier Transform is like a prism for ripples — it separates the chaos into its pure component waves, each with a specific wavelength and height. Conversely, the Inverse Fourier Transform lets you add those pure waves back together to reconstruct the original ripple pattern exactly.

Key idea: Any reasonable signal can be decomposed into a (possibly infinite) sum of sinusoids. This is not an approximation — for smooth periodic functions it is exact. For a square wave:

$$f(t) = \frac{4}{\pi}\left(\sin t + \frac{\sin 3t}{3} + \frac{\sin 5t}{5} + \cdots\right) = \frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(2k+1)t}{2k+1}$$
If you remember one thing

The Fourier Transform \(\mathcal{F}\{f\}(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)\,e^{-i\omega t}\,dt\) is an inner product of \(f(t)\) with the complex exponential \(e^{-i\omega t}\). It measures how much of frequency \(\omega\) is present in \(f(t)\). The inverse — \(f(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} F(\omega)\,e^{i\omega t}\,d\omega\) — rebuilds \(f\) by adding weighted sinusoids back up.

How It Works Internally
▼

Step 1 — Project onto Sinusoids (the Inner Product View)

The Fourier Transform computes, for each frequency \(\omega\), the inner product of \(f(t)\) with the complex exponential \(e^{-i\omega t} = \cos(\omega t) - i\sin(\omega t)\). This is exactly like projecting a vector onto a basis. If \(f\) contains a lot of frequency \(\omega\), the product \(f(t)\cdot e^{-i\omega t}\) oscillates constructively, and the integral is large. If \(f\) has no \(\omega\) component, the product cancels and the integral is zero.

Step 2 — The Complex Exponential Basis

The functions \(\{e^{i\omega t}\}_{\omega \in \mathbb{R}}\) form an orthogonal basis for \(L^2(\mathbb{R})\) under the inner product \(\langle f, g\rangle = \int f(t)\overline{g(t)}\,dt\). Orthogonality means:

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{i\omega_1 t} \cdot e^{-i\omega_2 t}\,dt = 2\pi\,\delta(\omega_1 - \omega_2)$$

This is the reason you can invert the transform — the basis functions don't "leak" into each other.

Step 3 — What \(F(\omega)\) encodes

\(F(\omega)\) is a complex number at each frequency. Its magnitude \(|F(\omega)|\) is the amplitude of the \(\omega\) component; its phase \(\angle F(\omega)\) is the time offset of that sinusoid. The power spectrum \(|F(\omega)|^2\) tells you the energy at each frequency.

Key Property — Symmetry for Real Signals

For real-valued \(f(t)\), \(F(-\omega) = \overline{F(\omega)}\) (conjugate symmetry). This means the spectrum is symmetric: \(|F(-\omega)| = |F(\omega)|\) and \(\angle F(-\omega) = -\angle F(\omega)\). The FFT exploits this to halve the computation for real inputs.

The Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) and FFT

In practice, signals are sampled at \(N\) discrete points. The DFT computes:

$$X[k] = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\,e^{-i 2\pi kn/N}, \quad k = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$$

Naive computation is \(O(N^2)\). The Fast Fourier Transform (Cooley-Tukey, 1965) exploits the symmetry of \(e^{-i2\pi kn/N}\) to reduce this to \(O(N\log N)\) by recursively splitting even/odd indices — a factor of 1000 speedup for \(N=10^6\).

Gibbs Phenomenon

When you truncate the Fourier series at finitely many terms near a discontinuity (like the edges of a square wave), an overshoot of approximately 9% of the jump height always appears and never vanishes, no matter how many terms you add. This is the Gibbs phenomenon — visible in the animation when only a few harmonics are included.

The Mathematical Foundations
▼

The Forward Fourier Transform

Given an integrable signal \(f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{C}\), the Fourier Transform is:

$$F(\omega) = \mathcal{F}\{f\}(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)\,e^{-i\omega t}\,dt$$

The Inverse Fourier Transform recovers \(f\) from its spectrum:

$$f(t) = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\{F\}(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} F(\omega)\,e^{i\omega t}\,d\omega$$

The \(\frac{1}{2\pi}\) normalization ensures the round-trip \(\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\mathcal{F}\{f\}\} = f\).

Key Properties (with proofs)

  • Linearity: \(\mathcal{F}\{af + bg\} = a\mathcal{F}\{f\} + b\mathcal{F}\{g\}\) — follows directly from integral linearity.
  • Time-shift: \(\mathcal{F}\{f(t-t_0)\}(\omega) = e^{-i\omega t_0}F(\omega)\) — shifting in time multiplies the spectrum by a complex exponential (phase shift, not magnitude change).
  • Convolution theorem: \(\mathcal{F}\{f * g\}(\omega) = F(\omega)\cdot G(\omega)\) — convolution in time becomes pointwise multiplication in frequency. This is why FFT-based convolution is \(O(N\log N)\) instead of \(O(N^2)\).
  • Parseval's theorem: \(\int |f(t)|^2\,dt = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int |F(\omega)|^2\,d\omega\) — energy is preserved by the transform.
  • Uncertainty principle: \(\sigma_t \cdot \sigma_\omega \geq \frac{1}{2}\) — a signal cannot be simultaneously perfectly localized in both time and frequency. Sharp time localization requires broad frequency content, and vice versa.

Fourier Series (Periodic Signals)

For a \(T\)-periodic signal \(f(t)\), the Fourier series decomposes it into discrete harmonics at integer multiples of the fundamental frequency \(\omega_0 = 2\pi/T\):

$$f(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} c_n\,e^{in\omega_0 t}, \quad c_n = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T f(t)\,e^{-in\omega_0 t}\,dt$$

For a square wave (period \(2\pi\), amplitude 1), the coefficients are:

$$c_n = \begin{cases} \dfrac{2}{in\pi} & n \text{ odd} \\ 0 & n \text{ even} \end{cases}$$

So the real-valued representation is \(f(t) = \frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin((2k+1)t)}{2k+1}\), confirming only odd harmonics contribute.

Numerical Trace

At \(t = \pi/2\): \(\sin(\pi/2)=1\), \(\sin(3\pi/2)/3 = -1/3\), \(\sin(5\pi/2)/5 = 1/5\), ... Partial sum with 6 terms: \(\frac{4}{\pi}(1 - \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{7} + \frac{1}{9} - \frac{1}{11}) = \frac{4}{\pi}(0.8441) \approx 1.074\) — the Gibbs overshoot of ~7.4%. As \(n\to\infty\) this converges to 1 but the peak overshoot stays fixed at ~9%.

Key Takeaways & Connections to AI
▼

When to Apply the Fourier Transform

  • Signal processing: audio compression (MP3 uses Modified DCT), noise filtering, spectral analysis
  • Image processing: JPEG compression, edge detection via frequency-domain filters
  • Partial differential equations: the heat and wave equations become algebraic in frequency domain
  • Fast convolution: FFT-based convolution is \(O(N\log N)\) vs. \(O(N^2)\) direct — essential for CNNs and audio processing

Connections to Modern AI

  • Positional Encodings (Transformers): The sinusoidal PE in the original "Attention Is All You Need" (Vaswani 2017) — \(\text{PE}(pos, 2i) = \sin(pos/10000^{2i/d})\) — directly uses Fourier basis functions to inject position information, exploiting the Fourier shift property so relative positions can be computed via inner products.
  • Fourier Neural Operator (FNO): Learns solution operators for PDEs by performing convolution in frequency space via FFT, then truncating to the lowest-frequency modes. \(O(N\log N)\) complexity vs. \(O(N^2)\) for attention over grid points.
  • Spectral Graph Neural Networks: Graph convolution is defined as multiplication in the graph Fourier domain (eigenbasis of the graph Laplacian), extending classical convolution to irregular graphs.
  • NeRF & Fourier Features: Random Fourier features \(\phi(x) = [\cos(2\pi\mathbf{b}^\top x), \sin(2\pi\mathbf{b}^\top x)]\) map inputs to a high-frequency feature space, enabling MLPs to learn fine detail.
The Big Picture

Fourier analysis is the foundation of "signal ↔ spectrum" duality: any problem involving locality in frequency can be solved efficiently in frequency space, and any problem involving locality in space/time is solved in time space. The FFT is the algorithm that makes this switch computationally feasible, and the convolution theorem is the theoretical bridge. Virtually every efficient ML operation on structured data (images, audio, graphs, PDEs) traces back to this duality.

Code Trace

import numpy as np
# Square wave: f(t) = 1 if 0<t<pi else -1
N = 1024; t = np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, N, endpoint=False)
f = np.sign(np.sin(t)) # square wave
# Continuous Fourier Transform (via integral approximation)
def FT(f, t, omega):
dt = t[1]-t[0]
return np.sum(f * np.exp(-1j * omega * t)) * dt
# Discrete Fourier Transform (FFT — O(n log n))
F = np.fft.fft(f) # complex spectrum
freqs = np.fft.fftfreq(N, d=t[1]-t[0])
magnitude = np.abs(F) / N # normalize
phase = np.angle(F)
# Inverse Fourier Transform
f_reconstructed = np.fft.ifft(F).real
print(f"Max error: {np.max(np.abs(f - f_reconstructed)):.2e}")
# Partial reconstruction: only keep odd harmonics up to n_terms
def partial_square_wave(t, n_terms):
s = np.zeros_like(t)
for k in range(1, 2*n_terms, 2): # k=1,3,5,...
s += (4/np.pi) * np.sin(k*t) / k
return s
# Parseval's theorem: energy preserved
E_time = np.sum(np.abs(f)**2) / N
E_freq = np.sum(np.abs(F)**2) / N**2
assert np.isclose(E_time, E_freq, rtol=1e-5)
