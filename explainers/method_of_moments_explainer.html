Estimating distribution parameters by matching sample moments to theoretical moments
Intermediate Statistical Inference Parameter Estimation O(n) estimation
1
Understanding Method of Moments
▼

What is it? The Method of Moments (MOM) is one of the oldest parameter estimation techniques in statistics. The core idea is beautifully simple: a distribution's theoretical moments (mean, variance, skewness...) are functions of its unknown parameters. If you compute the same moments from your actual data (sample moments), you can set them equal and solve for the parameters.

Analogy: Imagine you've found an unmarked bag of coins. You don't know if they're fair. You flip them and compute the fraction of heads (the sample mean). You know that for a Bernoulli coin, the theoretical mean equals \(p\). So you set \(p = \text{fraction of heads}\) — that's Method of Moments. For more complex distributions with two unknowns (like shape and scale), you use two equations (mean and variance) and solve the system.

💡 If you remember one thing

MOM equates the first \(k\) sample moments \(\hat{\mu}_r = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i^r\) to the corresponding theoretical moments \(\mu_r(\theta)\), then solves for \(\theta\). With \(k\) parameters, you need \(k\) moment equations: $$\hat{\mu}_r = \mathbb{E}_\theta[X^r], \quad r = 1, 2, \ldots, k$$

The key terminology

The \(r\)-th raw moment of a random variable \(X\) is \(\mu_r = \mathbb{E}[X^r]\). The first raw moment is the mean; the second raw moment minus the squared mean gives the variance. Sample moments replace the expectation with the sample average: \(\hat{\mu}_r = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^r\). MOM says: treat your sample as if it perfectly represents the population — then the two must agree.

2
Historical Context & Core Theory
▼

Origins

Karl Pearson introduced the Method of Moments in 1894 to fit his newly proposed family of distributions (the Pearson distributions) to biological data. It predates maximum likelihood estimation by nearly 30 years — Fisher introduced MLE in 1922. For decades, MOM was the standard estimation approach, partly because it required no optimization: you just solve a (often closed-form) system of equations.

Formal Setup

Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_n \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} F_\theta\) where \(\theta = (\theta_1, \ldots, \theta_k)\) are unknown parameters. Define:

$$\hat{\mu}_r = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i^r \quad \text{(sample moment)}$$
$$\mu_r(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_\theta[X^r] \quad \text{(theoretical moment)}$$

The MOM estimator \(\hat{\theta}\) solves the system:

$$\mu_r(\hat{\theta}) = \hat{\mu}_r, \quad r = 1, 2, \ldots, k$$

Why does it work?

By the Law of Large Numbers, \(\hat{\mu}_r \xrightarrow{p} \mu_r(\theta_0)\) as \(n \to \infty\), where \(\theta_0\) is the true parameter. So setting sample moments equal to theoretical moments is a consistent approach — as data grows, our estimates converge to the truth. This is MOM's theoretical guarantee.

🔑 Consistency Property

Under mild regularity conditions (moments exist, the moment equations have a unique solution), the MOM estimator is consistent: \(\hat{\theta}_{MOM} \xrightarrow{p} \theta_0\). It is also asymptotically normal, allowing confidence intervals and hypothesis tests.

Three classic distributions worked out

Normal(\(\mu, \sigma^2\))

\(\mathbb{E}[X] = \mu\), \(\text{Var}(X) = \sigma^2\)

MOM: \(\hat{\mu} = \bar{X}\)

\(\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum(X_i - \bar{X})^2\)

= MLE

Gamma(\(\alpha, \beta\))

\(\mathbb{E}[X] = \alpha/\beta\), \(\text{Var}(X) = \alpha/\beta^2\)

MOM: \(\hat{\alpha} = \bar{X}^2/\hat{S}^2\)

\(\hat{\beta} = \bar{X}/\hat{S}^2\)

≠ MLE (less efficient)

Beta(\(\alpha, \beta\))

\(\mathbb{E}[X] = \alpha/(\alpha+\beta)\), \(\text{Var}(X) = \frac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^2(\alpha+\beta+1)}\)

Let \(c = \bar{X}(1-\bar{X})/\hat{S}^2 - 1\)

\(\hat{\alpha} = \bar{X}\cdot c\), \(\hat{\beta} = (1-\bar{X})\cdot c\)

Closed-form; MLE requires Newton

The Math: Derivations & Asymptotic Theory
▼

Full Gamma derivation (step by step)

Suppose \(X \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)\) with density \(f(x) = \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} x^{\alpha-1} e^{-\beta x}\) (rate parameterisation). The first two raw moments are:

$$\mu_1 = \mathbb{E}[X] = \frac{\alpha}{\beta}, \qquad \mu_2 = \mathbb{E}[X^2] = \frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)}{\beta^2}$$

Set sample moments equal to theoretical moments:

$$\hat{\mu}_1 = \bar{X} = \frac{\hat\alpha}{\hat\beta}, \qquad \hat{\mu}_2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum X_i^2 = \frac{\hat\alpha(\hat\alpha+1)}{\hat\beta^2}$$

From the first equation: \(\hat\alpha = \hat\beta \bar{X}\). Note that \(\hat\mu_2 - \bar{X}^2 = \hat{S}^2\) is the sample variance (biased). Then:

$$\hat{S}^2 = \hat\mu_2 - \bar{X}^2 = \frac{\hat\alpha(\hat\alpha+1)}{\hat\beta^2} - \frac{\hat\alpha^2}{\hat\beta^2} = \frac{\hat\alpha}{\hat\beta^2}$$

Solving: \(\hat\beta = \bar{X}/\hat{S}^2\) and \(\hat\alpha = \bar{X}^2/\hat{S}^2\). Both are simple closed-form expressions.

Asymptotic distribution of MOM estimators

Let \(g(\mu_1, \ldots, \mu_k) = \theta\) be the moment-inversion map (solve the system for \(\theta\)). By the delta method applied to the CLT:

$$\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_{MOM} - \theta_0) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\ \nabla g(\mu)^\top \Sigma \nabla g(\mu)\right)$$

where \(\Sigma_{rs} = \mu_{r+s} - \mu_r \mu_s\) is the covariance of the raw moment vector. This gives us standard errors and confidence intervals for MOM estimators.

MOM vs MLE: asymptotic efficiency

MLE achieves the Cramér–Rao lower bound:

$$\text{AsyVar}(\hat\theta_{MLE}) = \frac{1}{I(\theta_0)}$$

where \(I(\theta_0) = -\mathbb{E}[\partial^2 \log f / \partial\theta^2]\) is the Fisher information. MOM achieves:

$$\text{AsyVar}(\hat\theta_{MOM}) = \frac{(\partial \mu_1/\partial\theta)^{-2} \text{Var}(X)}{1} \geq \frac{1}{I(\theta_0)}$$

For the Gamma distribution, MOM is provably less efficient than MLE (larger asymptotic variance). The efficiency ratio depends on the distribution.

⚠️ Common Misconception

MOM estimators can fall outside the parameter space. For example, Gamma MOM gives \(\hat\alpha = \bar{X}^2/\hat{S}^2\), which is always positive (fine). But for some distributions, the moment equations can yield inadmissible solutions. Always check parameter constraints after solving.

🎯 Decision Guide

Use MOM when: you need a fast closed-form estimator, you have a distribution with analytically tractable moments, or you're using GMM for econometric models with endogeneity.

Use MLE when: efficiency matters, you have large samples, and you can write/evaluate the log-likelihood easily.

Use Bayesian when: small samples, prior knowledge available, or you need full uncertainty quantification.

Generalized Method of Moments (GMM) — preview

GMM extends MOM to handle situations where you have more moment conditions than parameters (over-identification). Instead of solving \(\mu_r(\theta) = \hat{\mu}_r\) exactly, GMM minimises a weighted quadratic form:

$$\hat\theta_{GMM} = \underset{\theta}{\operatorname{argmin}}\; g_n(\theta)^\top W_n\, g_n(\theta)$$

where \(g_n(\theta) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i m(X_i, \theta)\) are the sample moment conditions and \(W_n\) is a weight matrix. The optimal weight is the inverse of the moment covariance. GMM is central to econometrics (Hansen 1982, Nobel Prize 2013) and also underpins modern self-supervised learning objectives.

Interview Deep-Dive & Key Takeaways
▼

Interview Q&A

Q1 (Easy): What is the Method of Moments?
A: Set the first \(k\) sample moments \(\hat{\mu}_r = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i^r\) equal to the corresponding theoretical moments \(\mathbb{E}[X^r]\) expressed as functions of unknown parameters, then solve for the parameters. With \(k\) parameters, \(k\) equations suffice.

Q2 (Medium): Why might MOM be preferred over MLE in practice?
A: MOM often yields closed-form estimators, making it computationally trivial (no numerical optimisation). It doesn't require the full density — just the moments — which is useful when the likelihood is intractable. It's the basis for GMM in econometrics, where we have endogenous variables and can't write a clean likelihood.

Q3 (Medium): Is MOM consistent? Is it efficient?
A: Yes, consistent — by LLN, sample moments converge to true moments, and if the moment map is continuous and invertible, the estimator converges to the true parameter. Generally NOT efficient — it doesn't achieve the Cramér-Rao bound except in special cases (e.g., Normal, Exponential). MLE is asymptotically efficient under regularity conditions.

Q4 (Hard): What is the asymptotic distribution of MOM estimators?
A (PhD level): By CLT, \(\sqrt{n}(\hat{\mu} - \mu) \to \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)\) where \(\Sigma_{rs} = \mu_{r+s} - \mu_r\mu_s\). Applying the delta method to the transformation \(g: \mu \mapsto \theta\), we get \(\sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_{MOM} - \theta_0) \to \mathcal{N}(0, \nabla g^\top \Sigma \nabla g)\). The Jacobian \(\nabla g = (\partial \mu_r / \partial \theta_j)^{-1}\) captures how moment equations translate to parameter uncertainty.

Q5 (Hard): When does MOM produce inadmissible estimates, and how do you fix it?
A: When the parameter space is bounded (e.g., \(p \in [0,1]\) for Beta), the system of equations solved by MOM has no constraint that keeps the solution in-bounds. In practice, if sample moments are extreme (e.g., very low variance data), MOM can give \(\hat{p} > 1\). Fix: use constrained optimisation, or switch to the truncated moment equations, or use MLE with constraint. In GMM, projection back onto the parameter space after each step is common.

✅ Key Takeaways

1. MOM equates \(k\) sample moments to \(k\) theoretical moment equations to estimate \(k\) parameters.
2. It is consistent (LLN), asymptotically normal (CLT + delta method), but not always efficient.
3. Biggest advantage: closed-form solutions, no density needed, computationally trivial.
4. Biggest limitation: less efficient than MLE; can violate parameter constraints.
5. GMM generalises MOM to over-identified systems (more conditions than parameters).
