1
Understanding Sharp vs Flat Minima
▼

What is it? After training, a neural network sits at a minimum of the training loss landscape — a valley in a very high-dimensional surface. Two networks can achieve exactly the same training loss (same valley depth) yet generalize completely differently to new data. The key is the shape of the valley: is it narrow and steep (sharp) or wide and bowl-like (flat)?

The core intuition: When you move from training data to test data, the loss surface shifts slightly — distribution shift, finite sample noise, or even floating-point rounding changes the landscape. A model sitting in a flat valley still has low loss after this shift. A model in a sharp ravine shoots up to high loss at the slightest perturbation.

If you remember one thing

Flat minima generalize better because they are robust to the inevitable mismatch between training and test loss surfaces. The width of the minimum determines how much the loss can shift before test performance degrades. Mathematically, this is captured by the Hessian eigenvalue spectrum at the minimum — large eigenvalues mean sharp, small eigenvalues mean flat.

Real-world analogy: Imagine two hikers who both reach the lowest point of two different valleys in a foggy mountain range. Fog shifts the entire landscape by 30 cm. Hiker A is in a wide, flat meadow — a 30 cm shift changes nothing. Hiker B is balanced on a knife-edge ridge — any shift sends them tumbling. Both were at ground level during training (fog-free), but only A survives the test-time fog.

The Hessian, Sharpness Measures & the SAM Connection
▼

Hessian Eigenvalue Sharpness

The local geometry of \(L(\theta)\) around a minimum \(\theta^*\) is described by the Hessian \(H = \nabla^2_\theta L(\theta^*)\). A Taylor expansion gives:

$$L(\theta^* + \epsilon) \approx L(\theta^*) + \frac{1}{2}\epsilon^\top H \epsilon$$

The sharpness of the minimum is measured by the largest Hessian eigenvalue \(\lambda_{\max}(H)\). A sharp minimum has large \(\lambda_{\max}\); a flat minimum has small \(\lambda_{\max}\). The trace of \(H\) (sum of all eigenvalues) gives the overall curvature in all directions.

The Train-Test Loss Shift Argument

Suppose the test loss \(L_{\text{test}}\) is a perturbed version of training loss: the minimizer shifts by \(\delta\) in weight space. The test loss at the training minimum \(\theta^*\) is approximately:

$$L_{\text{test}}(\theta^*) \approx L_{\text{train}}(\theta^*) + \frac{1}{2}\delta^\top H\, \delta \leq L_{\text{train}}(\theta^*) + \frac{1}{2}\lambda_{\max} \|\delta\|^2$$

The generalization gap is bounded by \(\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda_{\max}\|\delta\|^2\). A flat minimum (\(\lambda_{\max}\) small) gives a small gap even for a sizeable shift \(\delta\). A sharp minimum amplifies any perturbation.

PAC-Bayes / Weight Perturbation View

If we add Gaussian noise \(\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)\) to weights, the expected loss increase is:

$$\mathbb{E}_\epsilon\bigl[L(\theta^* + \epsilon)\bigr] - L(\theta^*) \approx \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\operatorname{tr}(H)$$

For our toy example: \(\sigma=0.1\), sharp \(\lambda=20\) gives increase \(= 0.01 \times 20/2 = 0.10\); flat \(\lambda=1\) gives \(0.01 \times 1/2 = 0.005\). A 20× difference in robustness from the same weight noise.

Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM)

SAM (Foret et al., 2021) directly minimizes the worst-case loss within a ball of radius \(\rho\) around the current weights:

$$\min_\theta \max_{\|\epsilon\|_2 \leq \rho} L(\theta + \epsilon)$$

The inner maximizer has closed form: \(\hat{\epsilon}(\theta) = \rho \cdot \frac{\nabla_\theta L(\theta)}{\|\nabla_\theta L(\theta)\|_2}\). SAM perturbs along the gradient direction, evaluates the loss there, then steps the gradient at that perturbed point — biasing training toward flat minima by penalizing regions with steep surroundings.

Key Design Insight: Why SGD Finds Flatter Minima Than Full-Batch GD

Stochastic gradient noise acts as implicit regularization. The noise covariance \(\propto \eta/B \cdot H\) (where \(\eta\) is LR, \(B\) is batch size) means SGD naturally escapes sharp minima — the curvature \(H\) at a sharp minimum makes the noise even larger, pushing the optimizer away. Larger batches (smaller noise) tend to converge to sharper minima with worse generalization, explaining the "batch-size generalization gap" observed empirically.

Common Misconception: Flat Minima Are Not Necessarily Low-Loss

A wide, flat region of loss landscape can be at a higher training loss than a sharp minimum. The claim is not that flat minima have lower training loss — they often don't. The claim is that they have better generalization: the gap \(L_{\text{test}} - L_{\text{train}}\) is smaller. Over-training with large batches can find sharp, near-zero training loss minima that generalize poorly.

The Math — Hochreiter, PAC-Bayes & MDL
▼

Hochreiter & Schmidhuber (1997) — Original Formal Argument

Hochreiter & Schmidhuber introduced the description length perspective: a sharp minimum requires more bits to encode (since the weights must be specified to high precision to stay in the basin), while a flat minimum is robust to quantization. By MDL (Minimum Description Length), the shortest description generalizes best. Formally, if the basin has "volume" \(V\) in weight space, the number of bits needed is \(\sim -\log_2 V\). A flat basin has large \(V\), short description, better generalization.

PAC-Bayes Bound (Dziugaite & Roy, 2017)

The PAC-Bayes framework gives a rigorous generalization bound. Let \(Q\) be a distribution over weights (e.g., Gaussian centred at \(\theta^*\)), \(P\) a prior. With probability \(\geq 1 - \delta\):

$$L_{\text{test}}(\theta^*) \leq L_{\text{train}}^Q + \sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{KL}(Q \| P) + \ln(m/\delta)}{2(m-1)}}$$

where \(m\) is the number of training samples and \(L_{\text{train}}^Q = \mathbb{E}_{\theta \sim Q}[L_{\text{train}}(\theta)]\) is the expected training loss under perturbation. Choosing \(Q = \mathcal{N}(\theta^*, \sigma^2 I)\), the KL term equals \(\frac{\|\theta^*\|^2}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{p}{2}\ln(1/\sigma^2)\) where \(p\) is the parameter count. To make the bound tight, choose \(\sigma^2\) as large as possible while keeping \(L_{\text{train}}^Q \approx L_{\text{train}}(\theta^*)\) — this is exactly the condition that the minimum is flat: the loss stays low when weights are perturbed by \(\sigma\).

Flatness Measure: \(\phi_\rho\)

Keskar et al. (2017) define the flatness of a minimum \(\theta^*\) relative to a neighborhood radius \(\rho\):

$$\phi_\rho(\theta^*) = \frac{\max_{\|\epsilon\|_\infty \leq \rho} L(\theta^* + \epsilon) - L(\theta^*)}{\max(1, L(\theta^*))}$$

This is directly the SAM objective's inner maximization. A value close to 0 means flat; large values mean sharp. In their experiments, large-batch training (1024+) produced \(\phi_\rho \approx 10\times\) larger than small-batch training, with correspondingly worse test accuracy.

Numerical Example: Sharp vs Flat

Sharp: \(a = 10\), minimum at \(w^* = 0.5\). Test loss minimum shifted by \(\delta = 0.3\).
\(L_{\text{test}}(w^*) = 10 \times (0.3)^2 = 0.90\). Gap = 0.90.

Flat: \(a = 0.5\). \(L_{\text{test}}(w^*) = 0.5 \times (0.3)^2 = 0.045\). Gap = 0.045.
Same training loss (0), 20× different generalization gap.

Key Takeaways & Trade-offs
▼

Flat minima: what promotes them?

  • Small batch SGD — stochastic noise escapes sharp basins implicitly
  • Higher learning rate — larger steps bounce out of narrow valleys
  • SAM / ASAM / GSAM — explicit flatness regularization
  • Sharpness-penalized loss — add \(\lambda \cdot \lambda_{\max}(H)\) to the objective
  • Cyclical LR / SWA — averaging over trajectory finds flat regions

Caveats and open questions

  • Flatness is not scale-invariant: rescaling weights changes Hessian eigenvalues without changing predictions (for ReLU nets). The measure \(\phi_\rho\) is thus model-class-dependent.
  • Dinh et al. (2017) showed sharp minima can generalize well if measured at the right scale — the geometry is more subtle than \(\lambda_{\max}\) alone.
  • The causal direction is debated: does flatness cause generalization, or do well-generalizing solutions happen to be flat?

Practical Decision Rule

If your model overfits (train acc ≫ test acc): try smaller batch size, or add SAM. If training is unstable with small batches: use SWA (Stochastic Weight Averaging) which approximates flat minima by averaging checkpoints. For transformers, the flatness-LR interaction means a warm-up schedule is essential — it prevents early convergence to a sharp minimum.

Code Trace — SAM & Sharpness Measurement
▼

SAM in PyTorch — two-step gradient update that targets flat minima.

import torch, torch.nn as nn
class SAM(torch.optim.Optimizer):
def __init__(self, params, base_optimizer, rho=0.05):
self.base_optimizer = base_optimizer(params)
self.rho = rho # neighborhood radius
super().__init__(params, dict(rho=rho))
@torch.no_grad()
def first_step(self):
# Step 1: perturb weights toward steepest ascent
grad_norm = self._grad_norm()
for p in self.param_groups[0]['params']:
if p.grad is None: continue
eps = p.grad * self.rho / grad_norm # eps = rho * g / ||g||
p.add_(eps) # theta_adv = theta + eps
self.state[p]['eps'] = eps.clone()
@torch.no_grad()
def second_step(self):
# Step 2: restore weights, apply gradient from adv. point
for p in self.param_groups[0]['params']:
if p.grad is None: continue
p.sub_(self.state[p]['eps']) # restore theta
self.base_optimizer.step() # update at original theta
def _grad_norm(self):
norms = [p.grad.norm(2) for p in self.param_groups[0]['params']
if p.grad is not None]
return torch.stack(norms).norm(2) # global L2 norm
# Training loop (two forward+backward passes per batch)
optimizer = SAM(model.parameters(), torch.optim.SGD, rho=0.05)
for x, y in dataloader:
loss = criterion(model(x), y)
loss.backward() # compute grad at theta
optimizer.first_step() # move to theta + eps
optimizer.zero_grad()
criterion(model(x), y).backward() # grad at theta_adv
optimizer.second_step() # update using adv. grad
optimizer.zero_grad()
