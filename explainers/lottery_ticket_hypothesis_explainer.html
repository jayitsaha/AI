Dense networks secretly contain sparse "winning tickets" that, trained from the same initialisation, match full-network accuracy
Advanced Deep Learning Optimization Pruning IMP · Weight Rewinding
1
Understanding the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis
▼

What is it? When you train a large neural network, most of the weights end up unnecessary — you could have gotten the same result with a much smaller network. The Lottery Ticket Hypothesis (LTH), proposed by Frankl & Carlin (2019), says something stronger: inside that large network there already exists a small subnetwork, called a winning ticket, whose original initialisation is so fortunate that — if you train just that subnetwork from scratch — it converges to at least the same accuracy as the full network, in fewer steps.

Analogy: Imagine you buy 1 000 lottery tickets. Statistically, a handful are winners. If, before the draw, you could somehow identify only those winning tickets and throw the rest away, you'd still win the lottery — but with far fewer tickets. The large neural network is your 1 000-ticket bundle; the small subnetwork with its original initialisation is the winning ticket(s) hidden inside.

If you remember one thing

A dense, over-parameterized network contains a sparse subnetwork — the winning ticket \(\left(f(\mathbf{x}; \mathbf{m} \odot \boldsymbol{\theta}_0)\right)\) — which, when trained from its original initialisation \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0\) (not a random re-init), matches the full network's accuracy at lower parameter count. The key word is same initialisation: a randomly re-initialised subnetwork of the same structure does NOT win.

2
How IMP Finds the Winning Ticket
▼

Frankl & Carlin identified winning tickets using Iterative Magnitude Pruning (IMP) — a method that iterates three stages:

  1. Train the full network to convergence from initialisation \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0\). Record the final weights \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_T\).
  2. Prune a fraction \(p\) of weights with the smallest absolute values \(\lvert \theta_i \rvert\), creating a binary mask \(\mathbf{m} \in \{0,1\}^{|\boldsymbol{\theta}|}\).
  3. Rewind the surviving weights back to \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0\) (or an early checkpoint \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_k\), \(k \ll T\)). Repeat from step 1.

Each round reduces the network by \(p\%\). After \(n\) rounds, density is \((1-p)^n\). The subnetwork that remains and still trains well is the winning ticket.

Why weight rewinding (not random re-init)?

Frankl & Carlin's key ablation showed that resetting surviving weights to random values breaks the ticket — accuracy collapses. The specific initialisation \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0\) is essential. This strongly implies that the initialisation, not the architecture alone, is responsible for trainability. Later work (Frankl et al. 2020) showed rewinding to a very early checkpoint \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_k\) (rather than exactly \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0\)) also works and sometimes works better at scale — this is called late resetting.

The Formal Statement

A random initialisation \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0\) for a network \(f\) contains a sparse subnetwork — the winning ticket — \(f(\mathbf{x};\, \mathbf{m} \odot \boldsymbol{\theta}_0)\) such that, for sufficiently small sparsity, training the masked network from \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0\) achieves accuracy \(\geq\) training the full network, in \(\leq\) the full network's training time.

Common Misconception

LTH does NOT say "you should prune instead of training the full network." IMP requires training the full network first (possibly multiple times). Its value is theoretical insight into why over-parameterisation helps and practical guidance for efficient model design.

3
The Math — IMP Formally
▼

Notation

Let \(\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^d\) be network parameters. A binary mask \(\mathbf{m} \in \{0,1\}^d\) selects a subnetwork. The masked network is \(f(\mathbf{x};\, \mathbf{m} \odot \boldsymbol{\theta})\). Define the training objective as \(\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\).

IMP Algorithm

$$\mathbf{m}^{(0)} = \mathbf{1}, \quad \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_0$$

At round \(r\):

$$\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(r)}_T = \underset{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\; \mathcal{L}\!\left(f(\cdot;\, \mathbf{m}^{(r-1)} \odot \boldsymbol{\theta})\right), \quad \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(r)}_0 = \boldsymbol{\theta}_0$$

Prune by magnitude — remove the bottom \(p\)-fraction of surviving weights:

$$\mathbf{m}^{(r)}_i = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \left|\theta^{(r)}_{T,i}\right| \geq \tau^{(r)} \text{ and } \mathbf{m}^{(r-1)}_i = 1 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

where \(\tau^{(r)}\) is the \(p\)-th percentile of \(\{\lvert\theta^{(r)}_{T,i}\rvert : \mathbf{m}^{(r-1)}_i = 1\}\).

After \(n\) rounds, density is:

$$\rho_n = (1 - p)^n$$

Weight Rewinding vs Late Resetting

Original LTH (Frankl & Carlin 2019): rewind to \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_0\) (epoch 0 initialisation).

$$\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(r+1)}_{\text{start}} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_0$$

Late resetting (Frankl et al. 2020): rewind to \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_k\) for small \(k \ll T\):

$$\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(r+1)}_{\text{start}} = \boldsymbol{\theta}_k, \quad k \approx 0.01T$$

Late resetting is crucial for large-scale models (ResNet, Transformers) where exact init rewinding can fail.

Key Quantities

Sparsity after \(n\) rounds at pruning rate \(p\): \(\;\text{sparsity} = 1-(1-p)^n\)
Density: \(\rho_n = (1-p)^n\)
One-shot pruning: \(n=1\), remove \(1-p\) fraction in a single step (finds weaker tickets)
Iterative (IMP): \(n \gg 1\), each step prunes a small fraction — finds stronger tickets

Scope & Limitations

LTH was originally verified on small CNNs (LeNet, VGG on CIFAR-10). Scaling to large models (BERT, GPT) required late resetting and showed that tickets may not transfer across datasets/tasks without careful design.

Why over-parameterisation helps

LTH provides a compelling explanation: a large random network is likely to contain many lucky subnetworks. Gradient descent selects and amplifies the best ones. Over-parameterisation is not wasteful — it is the search budget for finding good initialisations.
