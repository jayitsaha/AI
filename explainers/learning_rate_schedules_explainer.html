Learning Rate Schedules

Fixed, Decaying, Warmup, Cyclical, Cosine Annealing & 1Cycle — the complete guide to controlling the optimizer's step size
Intermediate Optimization Theory Part I · Section 5.4 Must Know
1
Understanding the Concept
▾

When a neural network trains, it repeatedly nudges its weights in the direction that reduces the loss. The learning rate \(\eta\) is the single number that controls how big each nudge is. Think of it as the gear ratio on a bicycle: too high and you overshoot every hill (divergence); too low and you barely move (slow convergence).

The Core Analogy

Imagine hiking to a valley in fog. Early on, take big confident strides — you just need to get close. Near the bottom, take tiny careful steps — one wrong move and you climb back up. A learning rate schedule is your stride-length plan for the hike.

A fixed LR is like taking the same stride the whole way. A schedule adapts \(\eta_t\) at each step \(t\) according to a rule. Schedules matter enormously in practice: they are often the difference between a model that converges in hours versus days, or that achieves 78% accuracy versus 82%.

Why does the learning rate need to change?

  • Early training: loss landscape is steep and roughly convex → large \(\eta\) covers ground fast
  • Mid training: approaching a good region, curvature increases → medium \(\eta\) to not overshoot
  • Late training: near a sharp minimum, even small \(\eta\) can oscillate → tiny \(\eta\) to settle
  • Warmup (Transformers): random weights produce huge, chaotic gradients at step 0 → ramp \(\eta\) from near-zero to avoid corrupting early layers
Key Concept

A learning rate schedule is a function \(\eta: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}_{>0}\) that maps training step \(t\) to a positive scalar. The SGD update becomes \(\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta_t \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta_t)\).

This explainer covers six canonical schedules: fixed, step decay, exponential decay, cosine annealing, warmup+cosine, cyclical (CLR), and 1cycle — plus the math, intuition, and practical trade-offs of each.

2
The Six Schedules — Formulas & Mechanics
▾

1. Fixed Learning Rate

$$\eta_t = \eta_0 \qquad \forall\, t$$

The trivial schedule. A constant \(\eta_0\) throughout training. Simple and interpretable, but almost always suboptimal for deep networks since the same step size is too large for fine-tuning and too small for early exploration.

2. Step Decay (Staircase)

$$\eta_t = \eta_0 \cdot \gamma^{\lfloor t / s \rfloor}$$

where \(\gamma \in (0,1)\) is the decay factor (e.g. 0.1 or 0.5) and \(s\) is the step size in epochs. Every \(s\) epochs the LR is multiplied by \(\gamma\). The staircase shape holds LR steady so SGD can find the bottom of each local basin, then drops it to refine. Classic for ResNet, VGG training with \(\gamma=0.1\), \(s=30\).

3. Exponential Decay

$$\eta_t = \eta_0 \cdot e^{-\lambda t}$$

A continuous, smooth version of step decay. Mathematically clean: each step multiplies LR by \(e^{-\lambda}\). With \(\lambda=0.04\), the LR halves every \(\ln 2 / 0.04 \approx 17\) steps. Sensitive to the decay rate \(\lambda\): too large and LR hits near-zero before convergence.

4. Cosine Annealing

$$\eta_t = \eta_{\min} + \frac{1}{2}(\eta_{\max} - \eta_{\min})\left(1 + \cos\!\left(\frac{\pi\, t}{T}\right)\right)$$

Proposed by Loshchilov & Hutter (SGDR, 2017). The LR follows half a cosine wave from \(\eta_{\max}\) to \(\eta_{\min}\) over \(T\) steps. The cosine shape is naturally smooth: fast initial drop when far from optimum, slow final tail for fine-tuning. Often used with warm restarts (SGDR): after \(T\) steps, reset to \(\eta_{\max}\) and repeat with doubled period — this helps escape sharp local minima.

5. Linear Warmup → Cosine Annealing

$$\eta_t = \begin{cases} \eta_{\max} \cdot \dfrac{t}{W} & t \le W \\[8pt] \eta_{\min} + \dfrac{1}{2}(\eta_{\max}-\eta_{\min})\!\left(1 + \cos\!\dfrac{\pi(t-W)}{T-W}\right) & t > W \end{cases}$$

The de facto schedule for Transformers (BERT, GPT, T5, ViT). The warmup phase (first \(W\) steps) ramps \(\eta\) from 0 to \(\eta_{\max}\) linearly — essential when weights are random and gradients are explosive. After warmup, standard cosine annealing takes over. Warmup duration is typically 5–10% of total steps (e.g. 10k out of 100k).

Common Mistake

Skipping warmup for Transformers causes the embedding layer and layer norms to receive huge gradient updates on step 0. This can be catastrophic and irreversible — the model learns a bad representation in the first few batches and never fully recovers.

6. Cyclical Learning Rate (CLR)

$$\eta_t = \eta_{\min} + (\eta_{\max} - \eta_{\min}) \cdot \max\!\left(0,\; 1 - \left|\frac{t}{s} - 2k + 1\right|\right)$$

where \(k = \left\lfloor 1 + \frac{t}{2s} \right\rfloor\) is the cycle index. Proposed by Smith (2017). The LR oscillates between \(\eta_{\min}\) and \(\eta_{\max}\) in a triangular wave with half-period \(s\). High-LR phases let the optimizer traverse the loss landscape and escape saddle points; low-LR phases allow precise convergence. Smith showed this can achieve "super-convergence": matching or beating fixed-LR results in 10× fewer iterations.

7. 1Cycle Policy

$$\eta_t = \begin{cases} \eta_{\text{init}} + (\eta_{\max} - \eta_{\text{init}}) \cdot \dfrac{t}{W} & t \le W \\[8pt] \eta_{\text{final}} + \dfrac{1}{2}(\eta_{\max} - \eta_{\text{final}})\!\left(1 + \cos\!\dfrac{\pi(t-W)}{T-W}\right) & t > W \end{cases}$$

where \(\eta_{\text{init}} = \eta_{\max}/\text{div\_factor}\) (typically 25) and \(\eta_{\text{final}} \approx 0\). One large cycle: ~30% warmup then aggressive cosine annealing. The peak LR is much larger than you'd normally dare — the high LR acts as regularization (noise). Popularized by fast.ai; produces state-of-the-art results on ImageNet in dramatically fewer epochs than conventional schedules.

Numerical Trace (1Cycle, T=100 steps)

init_lr = 0.004, max_lr = 0.1, final_lr ≈ 0
t=0: \(\eta_0 = 0.004\)  |  t=10: \(\eta_{10} = 0.036\)  |  t=30: \(\eta_{30} = 0.100\) (peak)
t=60: \(\eta_{60} = 0.061\)  |  t=80: \(\eta_{80} = 0.019\)  |  t=100: \(\eta_{100} \approx 0\)

3
Why It Works — Optimization Theory
▾

The convergence of gradient descent is governed by the interplay between \(\eta\) and the curvature of the loss landscape. Understanding this mathematically explains why schedules help.

Gradient Descent Update Rule

$$\theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \eta_t \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta_t)$$

Convergence for Smooth Convex Functions

For a convex, \(L\)-smooth loss (Lipschitz gradient with constant \(L\)): GD converges at rate \(\mathcal{O}(1/t)\) with fixed \(\eta \le 1/L\). The upper bound on \(\eta\) is called the stability bound:

$$\eta_{\max} = \frac{2}{L_{\min} + L_{\max}}$$

where \(L_{\min}, L_{\max}\) are the minimum and maximum eigenvalues of the Hessian \(\nabla^2 \mathcal{L}\). Too large \(\eta\) ↔ divergence; too small ↔ \(\mathcal{O}(1/\eta t)\) slowdown.

Why Decreasing LR Improves Final Accuracy

Near a minimum \(\theta^*\), the loss is approximately quadratic: \(\mathcal{L}(\theta) \approx \frac{1}{2}(\theta-\theta^*)^\top H (\theta-\theta^*)\). The optimal step is \(\eta^* = 1/\lambda_{\max}(H)\). As training progresses and we get closer to \(\theta^*\), the Hessian's largest eigenvalue typically increases (sharp minima), requiring a smaller \(\eta\) to avoid oscillation.

Oscillation Condition

For quadratic loss with Hessian eigenvalue \(\lambda\): convergence iff \(\eta < 2/\lambda\). If \(\eta > 2/\lambda\), the iterate oscillates across the minimum and eventually diverges. This is why large fixed LR fails — it satisfies the condition globally but violates it locally near sharp minima.

Why the Cosine Shape is Near-Optimal

The cosine annealing LR has two desirable properties:

  • Zero slope at endpoints: \(\frac{d\eta}{dt}\big|_{t=0} = 0\) and \(\frac{d\eta}{dt}\big|_{t=T} = 0\). The LR changes slowly at the extremes — no sudden jumps that shock the optimizer.
  • Maximum rate of change at midpoint: \(\frac{d\eta}{dt}\big|_{t=T/2} = -\frac{\pi(\eta_{\max}-\eta_{\min})}{2T}\) — the sharpest drop happens in the middle of training, aligning with when the model transitions from exploration to exploitation.

Cyclical LR and Saddle Point Escaping

Saddle points — where some Hessian eigenvalues are positive and some negative — are problematic for constant-LR SGD. A cyclical schedule helps because at high LR, the noise in stochastic gradients combined with large step size can push iterates out of the saddle basin. Formally, with learning rate \(\eta_t\) and gradient noise covariance \(\Sigma\), the effective temperature of the implicit Langevin dynamics is:

$$T_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\eta_t}{|\mathcal{B}|} \cdot \Sigma(\theta_t)$$

where \(|\mathcal{B}|\) is the batch size. High-LR phases increase \(T_{\text{eff}}\), analogous to simulated annealing, allowing the optimizer to explore. Low-LR phases decrease temperature, allowing convergence.

Key Insight — Implicit Regularization

High learning rates in 1Cycle and CLR don't just help speed — they regularize. Large \(\eta\) amplifies gradient noise, which biases SGD toward flat minima (wide loss basins). Flat minima generalize better than sharp minima because small perturbations to \(\theta\) produce small changes in loss.

Warmup: Why Random Initialization is Dangerous

At step 0, weights are random (e.g., Xavier: \(\theta_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 2/(n_{\text{in}}+n_{\text{out}}))\)). The loss surface near random init is irregular and the Hessian eigenvalues can be enormous. Running at full \(\eta\) immediately produces gradient steps that are far too large, corrupting parameters irreversibly. Linear warmup from \(\eta \approx 0\) to \(\eta_{\max}\) over \(W\) steps ensures the first effective step size is \(\eta_{\max}/W\) — a factor of \(W\) smaller than the target, giving the model time to find a stable region before the full step size kicks in.

LR Range Test (Smith, 2017)

Before using CLR or 1Cycle, run a LR range test: start with \(\eta = 10^{-7}\), increase exponentially to \(\eta = 1\) over ~100 iterations, and plot loss vs LR. The optimal \(\eta_{\max}\) is just before loss starts to increase; \(\eta_{\min} \approx \eta_{\max}/10\). This is now standard practice in fast.ai and many production training pipelines.

Interaction with Batch Size

The linear scaling rule (Goyal et al., 2017): if you multiply batch size by \(k\), multiply \(\eta\) by \(k\). Intuition: with \(k\times\) more data per step, each gradient update is \(\sqrt{k}\times\) less noisy, but the effective number of update steps is \(k\times\) less — so you can afford a larger \(\eta\). This rule holds approximately for \(k \le 32\) with warmup; beyond that, the approximation breaks down.

6
Key Takeaways & Code
▾
  • The learning rate \(\eta_t\) is the most impactful single hyperparameter — schedule choice often matters more than optimizer choice (Adam vs SGD).
  • Fixed LR is fine for baselines; for production, use a schedule. Cosine or warmup+cosine are the modern defaults.
  • Warmup is mandatory for Transformers — without it, early training is catastrophically unstable.
  • Cyclical LR and 1Cycle leverage high-LR as implicit regularization toward flat minima, often enabling super-convergence.
  • Cosine annealing's smooth endpoints (zero slope at \(t=0\) and \(t=T\)) make it robust to epoch-boundary artifacts that plague step decay.

PyTorch Code — All Schedules

import torch, math
from torch.optim.lr_scheduler import (
StepLR, ExponentialLR, CosineAnnealingLR,
CyclicLR, OneCycleLR, LinearLR, SequentialLR
)
optimizer = torch.optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=0.1)
# 1. Step decay (gamma=0.1 every 30 epochs)
sched = StepLR(optimizer, step_size=30, gamma=0.1)
# 2. Exponential decay
sched = ExponentialLR(optimizer, gamma=0.97) # per epoch
# 3. Cosine annealing
sched = CosineAnnealingLR(optimizer, T_max=100, eta_min=1e-4)
# 4. Warmup + Cosine (Transformer style)
warmup = LinearLR(optimizer, start_factor=1/25, total_iters=10000)
cosine = CosineAnnealingLR(optimizer, T_max=90000, eta_min=1e-5)
sched = SequentialLR(optimizer, [warmup, cosine], milestones=[10000])
# 5. Cyclical LR (triangular)
sched = CyclicLR(optimizer, base_lr=1e-4, max_lr=1e-1,
step_size_up=2000, mode='triangular2')
# 6. 1Cycle Policy
sched = OneCycleLR(optimizer, max_lr=0.1, total_steps=10000,
pct_start=0.3, anneal_strategy='cos')
# Training loop (same for all):
for batch in dataloader:
optimizer.zero_grad()
loss = model(batch)
loss.backward()
optimizer.step()
sched.step() # call after optimizer.step()
Common Gotcha

Step vs epoch scheduling: Most PyTorch schedulers call step() once per epoch by default. For OneCycleLR and CyclicLR, call step() once per batch — the scheduler expects total_steps batches, not epochs. Mixing these up is one of the most common bugs in training pipelines.
