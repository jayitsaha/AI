K-Means: Algorithm, Convergence & k-means++

Lloyd's algorithm, monotone inertia descent, and far-point seeding for initialization
Foundational Classical ML Clustering O(nkt) per run
1
What is K-Means?
▼

Goal: Partition \(n\) data points into \(k\) clusters so that each point belongs to the cluster whose centroid (mean) it is closest to. The algorithm minimizes inertia — the total within-cluster sum of squared distances.

Analogy: Place \(k\) fire stations in a city. Assign each neighbourhood to its nearest station, then move each station to the geographic centre of its assigned neighbourhoods. Repeat. Each move is guaranteed to not make coverage worse.

💡 The One Thing to Remember

K-Means alternates between two steps that each monotonically decrease inertia \(J\). Since \(J \geq 0\) and there are finitely many assignments, convergence to a local minimum is guaranteed. Bad initialization (random) can trap it in a poor local min — k-means++ fixes this with probabilistic far-point seeding.

2
Lloyd's Algorithm — Step by Step
▼

Objective (Inertia)

$$J = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \| x_i - \mu_{c_i} \|^2$$

where \(c_i \in \{1,\ldots,k\}\) is the cluster assignment of point \(x_i\) and \(\mu_{c_i}\) is its centroid.

Two-Step Iteration

Step A — Assign: For each point \(x_i\), assign it to the nearest centroid:

$$c_i \leftarrow \arg\min_{j \in \{1,\ldots,k\}} \| x_i - \mu_j \|^2$$

This reassignment can only decrease \(J\) (or leave it unchanged) because each point moves to a cluster with strictly lower or equal squared distance.

Step B — Update: Recompute each centroid as the mean of its assigned points:

$$\mu_j \leftarrow \frac{1}{|C_j|} \sum_{x_i \in C_j} x_i$$

The mean minimizes sum of squared deviations — moving the centroid to the mean can only decrease \(J\).

🔑 Convergence Proof

Both steps monotonically decrease \(J\): Assign ↓ (nearest point rule), Update ↓ (mean minimizes SS). Since \(J \geq 0\) and there are at most \(k^n\) distinct assignment configurations, the algorithm terminates in finite iterations at a local minimum. It is not guaranteed to reach the global minimum.

Complexity

Each iteration costs \(O(nk)\) for assignment + \(O(n)\) for update = \(O(nk)\). With \(t\) iterations and \(r\) restarts: \(O(nktr)\). In practice \(t \ll n\) for well-separated clusters.

3
k-means++ Initialization
▼

Random initialization often places centroids too close together, causing convergence to poor local minima with large inertia. k-means++ seeds centroids probabilistically so they are spread out.

Algorithm

1. Choose first centroid \(\mu_1\) uniformly at random from the data.

2. For each subsequent centroid \(\mu_j\) (\(j = 2,\ldots,k\)):

$$P(\text{choose } x_i) = \frac{D(x_i)^2}{\sum_{x \in X} D(x)^2}$$

where \(D(x_i) = \min_{j' < j} \|x_i - \mu_{j'}\|^2\) is the squared distance to the nearest already-chosen centroid. Points far from all existing centroids have high probability of being picked next.

3. Run Lloyd's algorithm from this spread-out initialization.

📐 Theoretical Guarantee (Arthur & Vassilvitskii 2007)

k-means++ achieves expected inertia at most \(O(\log k)\) times the optimal inertia, compared to no guarantee for random init. Empirically, it typically needs fewer iterations to converge and lands in better local minima.

⚠️ Common Misconception

k-means++ does NOT guarantee the global optimum — it improves initialization but Lloyd's still finds only a local minimum. For hard problems, run multiple restarts (sklearn default: n_init=10) and keep the best.

5
Interview Questions
▼

Q1: Does k-means always converge? To what?

Yes — in finite iterations. Each of the two steps (Assign, Update) monotonically decreases inertia \(J \geq 0\), and there are finitely many distinct label assignments. So the sequence \(J^{(t)}\) is non-increasing and bounded below, hence convergent. The limit is a local minimum, not necessarily global.

Q2: What is the time complexity?

\(O(nktr)\): \(n\) points, \(k\) clusters, \(t\) iterations, \(r\) restarts. k-means++ initialization adds \(O(nk)\) overhead — negligible compared to total runtime.

Q3: Why is k-means++ better than random init?

Random init can cluster centroids together, leaving large regions unrepresented and trapping Lloyd's in a high-inertia local minimum. k-means++ seeds each new centroid with probability proportional to \(D(x)^2\) (squared distance to nearest existing centroid), spreading them apart. The \(O(\log k)\) approximation guarantee follows.

Q4: How do you choose k?

Elbow method: plot inertia vs. \(k\); pick the "elbow" where marginal gain flattens. Silhouette score: measures cohesion vs. separation, higher is better. BIC/AIC for Gaussian Mixture Models. Domain knowledge often trumps all.

Q5: What are K-Means' key limitations?

Assumes spherical, equally-sized clusters (uses Euclidean distance, fixed centroid). Sensitive to outliers (mean is not robust). Requires specifying \(k\). Non-deterministic (local minima vary by init). Fails on non-convex cluster shapes (use DBSCAN or spectral clustering instead).

6
K-Means vs. Alternatives
▼

K-Medoids (PAM): Uses actual data points as cluster centres (medoids), not means. More robust to outliers; \(O(n^2k)\) — much slower.

DBSCAN: Density-based; finds arbitrarily shaped clusters; no \(k\) needed; handles outliers explicitly. Fails with varying densities.

Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM): Soft (probabilistic) assignments; handles elliptical clusters; EM algorithm (similar two-step structure to Lloyd's). Slower, more parameters.

Hierarchical Clustering: No \(k\) needed upfront; produces dendrogram; \(O(n^2 \log n)\) — impractical for large \(n\).

Mini-Batch K-Means: Stochastic approximation of Lloyd's using random data subsets per iteration. \(O(b k t)\) with batch size \(b \ll n\). Scales to millions of points with minor accuracy trade-off.

📊 When to Use K-Means

Use k-means when: clusters are roughly spherical and equal-sized, \(n\) is large, you know (or can estimate) \(k\), and interpretability matters. Use DBSCAN for arbitrary shapes, GMM for soft assignments or elliptical clusters.
