Understanding the Gaussian Distribution
▼

What is it? The Gaussian (Normal) distribution is the most important probability distribution in all of statistics and machine learning. It describes the shape of data that clusters symmetrically around a central value — like heights in a population, or measurement errors in an experiment. You've seen its shape: the iconic "bell curve."

Analogy: Imagine you throw a dart at a target 1000 times, aiming for the bullseye every time. Most darts land near the center, fewer land far away, and the pattern is symmetric — missing left and missing right are equally likely. The distribution of where darts land is approximately Gaussian. Increasing σ is like having a shaky hand — the bell widens. Changing μ is like aiming at a different point on the target — the bell shifts.

💡 If you remember one thing

The Gaussian \(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\) is the maximum-entropy distribution with fixed mean and variance, the attractor of sums (CLT), and has MGF \(M(t) = \exp(\mu t + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 t^2)\) — which generates every moment cleanly. These three facts explain why Gaussians dominate statistics and ML.

🔑 Key Property

A Gaussian \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\) is fully characterized by just two numbers: mean \(\mu\) (location) and variance \(\sigma^2\) (spread). All higher moments are determined by these two — a consequence of the distribution belonging to the exponential family.

Key Properties & Why They Matter
▼

The PDF Formula

For \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\), the probability density function is:

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), \quad x \in \mathbb{R}$$

The normalizing constant \(\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\) ensures the density integrates to 1. The exponent is a negative-definite quadratic in \((x-\mu)\), which produces the symmetric bell shape.

The Standard Normal

When \(\mu = 0, \sigma^2 = 1\), we have the standard normal \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\). Its CDF \(\Phi(z) = P(Z \leq z)\) has no closed form but is tabulated; key values: \(\Phi(1.645) \approx 0.95\), \(\Phi(1.96) \approx 0.975\), \(\Phi(2.576) \approx 0.995\).

Symmetry & Moments

The distribution is symmetric around μ: \(f(\mu + x) = f(\mu - x)\) for all \(x\). This means:

  • Mean = Median = Mode = μ (all three coincide)
  • Skewness = 0 (no asymmetry)
  • Excess Kurtosis = 0 (kurtosis = 3; Gaussian is the baseline)
  • Variance = σ², Standard deviation = σ

The 68-95-99.7 (Empirical) Rule

These exact values come from integrating the standard normal CDF:

$$P(\mu - k\sigma \leq X \leq \mu + k\sigma) = \text{erf}\!\left(\frac{k}{\sqrt{2}}\right) = \begin{cases} 68.27\% & k=1 \\ 95.45\% & k=2 \\ 99.73\% & k=3 \end{cases}$$

Closure Under Linear Combinations

This is the killer property for ML. If \(X_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)\) and \(X_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)\) are independent, then:

$$aX_1 + bX_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(a\mu_1 + b\mu_2,\; a^2\sigma_1^2 + b^2\sigma_2^2)$$

Concretely: \(\mathcal{N}(1,4) + \mathcal{N}(2,9) = \mathcal{N}(3,13)\). This makes Gaussians close under the operations neural networks perform — weighted sums of Gaussian inputs stay Gaussian.

Maximum Entropy Property

Among all continuous distributions with a specified mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), the Gaussian maximizes differential entropy:

$$h(X) = \frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi e \sigma^2) \approx 1.4189 \text{ nats for } \mathcal{N}(0,1)$$

This is the information-theoretic reason Gaussians are the "least informative" prior for continuous data — they make the fewest extra assumptions beyond knowing the first two moments.

⚠️ Common Misconception

Gaussians have light tails (sub-Gaussian, exponentially decaying). Real-world phenomena like stock returns, internet traffic, and earthquake magnitudes have heavy tails. Assuming Gaussianity in these settings leads to catastrophic underestimation of extreme events (the "Black Swan" problem).

Moment Generating Function & Moments
▼

Definition & Closed Form

The Moment Generating Function (MGF) of a random variable \(X\) is \(M_X(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{tX}]\). For \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\):

$$M_X(t) = \exp\!\left(\mu t + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 t^2\right), \quad t \in \mathbb{R}$$

Derivation: Complete the square in the exponent of the expectation integral. Since \(\mathbb{E}[e^{tX}] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{tx} \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2)} dx\), combine the exponentials and complete the square to show the integral reduces to 1 (a Gaussian integral), leaving the MGF.

Extracting Moments via Differentiation

The k-th raw moment is \(\mathbb{E}[X^k] = M^{(k)}(0)\) — the k-th derivative at \(t=0\). For \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\):

  • \(M'(t) = (\mu + \sigma^2 t)\,M(t)\)  ⟹  \(\mathbb{E}[X] = M'(0) = \mu\) ✓
  • \(M''(t) = [\sigma^2 + (\mu+\sigma^2 t)^2]\,M(t)\)  ⟹  \(\mathbb{E}[X^2] = \sigma^2 + \mu^2\) ✓  (so \(\mathrm{Var}=\sigma^2\))
  • \(\mathbb{E}[X^3] = \mu^3 + 3\mu\sigma^2\) (odd function: 0 when μ = 0)
  • \(\mathbb{E}[X^4] = \mu^4 + 6\mu^2\sigma^2 + 3\sigma^4\)  ⟹  for N(0,1): \(\mathbb{E}[X^4] = 3\)

The Cumulant Generating Function (CGF)

The CGF is \(K(t) = \ln M(t) = \mu t + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 t^2\). Cumulants are the derivatives of \(K\) at 0:

$$K(t) = \mu t + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}t^2 \implies \kappa_1 = \mu,\; \kappa_2 = \sigma^2,\; \kappa_k = 0 \;\forall k \geq 3$$

This is the defining property of the Gaussian: it is the only distribution whose CGF is a polynomial (degree 2). All higher cumulants vanish, which is why the Gaussian has "no excess skewness or kurtosis." The Lévy-Cramér theorem states: if \(X_1 + X_2\) is Gaussian and \(X_1, X_2\) are independent, then both must be Gaussian.

∑ Numerical Trace — N(1, 4): MGF at t = 0.5

\(M(0.5) = \exp(1 \cdot 0.5 + \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 4 \cdot 0.25) = \exp(0.5 + 0.5) = e^1 \approx 2.7183\)
Check: \(\mathbb{E}[X] = 1\), \(\mathbb{E}[X^2] = 4 + 1 = 5\), \(\mathbb{E}[X^4] = 1 + 24 + 48 = 73\).

MGF and Characteristic Function

The characteristic function \(\varphi_X(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{itX}] = \exp(i\mu t - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 t^2)\) always exists (unlike the MGF for heavy-tailed distributions). It uniquely determines the distribution. The Gaussian characteristic function is a Gaussian itself — the Fourier transform of a Gaussian is Gaussian.

From Scratch — NumPy

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
def gaussian_pdf(x, mu=0.0, sigma=1.0):
"""Numerically stable Gaussian PDF."""
# Normalize x to avoid overflow in exp for large |x-mu|/sigma
z = (x - mu) / sigma # standardize
log_pdf = -0.5 * z**2 - np.log(sigma) - 0.5 * np.log(2 * np.pi)
return np.exp(log_pdf) # exponentiate log-pdf
def mgf(t, mu=0.0, sigma=1.0):
"""Moment generating function M(t) = exp(mu*t + 0.5*sigma^2*t^2)."""
return np.exp(mu * t + 0.5 * sigma**2 * t**2)
def moment_from_mgf(k, mu=0.0, sigma=1.0, h=1e-4):
"""Approximate k-th raw moment via numerical differentiation at t=0."""
# k-th derivative of M(t) at t=0 using finite differences
if k == 1:
return (mgf(h, mu, sigma) - mgf(-h, mu, sigma)) / (2 * h)
elif k == 2:
return (mgf(h, mu, sigma) - 2*mgf(0, mu, sigma) + mgf(-h, mu, sigma)) / h**2
return None
# Verify: N(2, 9) i.e. mu=2, sigma=3
mu, sigma = 2.0, 3.0
print(f"E[X] from MGF: {moment_from_mgf(1, mu, sigma):.4f} (expected {mu})")
print(f"E[X^2] from MGF: {moment_from_mgf(2, mu, sigma):.4f} (expected {sigma**2 + mu**2:.4f})")
def empirical_rule_check(n_samples=1_000_000, mu=0, sigma=1):
"""Verify 68-95-99.7 rule empirically."""
x = np.random.normal(mu, sigma, n_samples)
for k in [1, 2, 3]:
pct = np.mean(np.abs(x - mu) < k * sigma) * 100
exact = [68.27, 95.45, 99.73][k-1]
print(f"±{k}σ: {pct:.2f}% (exact: {exact}%)")

Production — SciPy & PyTorch

from scipy import stats
import torch
# SciPy: full distribution object
dist = stats.norm(loc=0, scale=1) # N(0, 1)
print(dist.pdf(0)) # 0.3989 (peak)
print(dist.cdf(1.96)) # 0.9750 (one-tailed 95%)
print(dist.ppf(0.975)) # 1.9600 (inverse CDF = z-score)
print(dist.moment(4)) # 3.0000 (4th raw moment)
# PyTorch: torch.distributions (used in VAEs, diffusion models)
d = torch.distributions.Normal(loc=0., scale=1.)
x = d.sample((1000,)) # sample 1000 points
log_prob = d.log_prob(x) # log PDF -- used in ELBO, NLL loss
entropy = d.entropy() # H[N(0,1)] = 0.5*ln(2*pi*e) ≈ 1.4189 nats
# ⚠️ Gotcha: torch.distributions.Normal(loc, scale) takes std (sigma), NOT variance!
# Correct: Normal(0, 1) -- sigma=1, variance=1
# Wrong: Normal(0, 4) -- if you meant variance=4, pass scale=sqrt(4)=2
# KL divergence: KL(N(mu1,s1) || N(mu2,s2)) in closed form
p = torch.distributions.Normal(0., 1.)
q = torch.distributions.Normal(2., 1.5)
kl = torch.distributions.kl_divergence(p, q) # uses closed-form formula
print(kl) # ~2.622 nats

Interview Q&A & Key Takeaways
▼

Interview Q&A

Q1 (Easy): State the PDF of N(μ, σ²) and name its two parameters.
A: \(f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)\). Parameters: μ (mean / location) and σ² (variance / scale²).

Q2 (Easy): What is the empirical rule?
A: 68.27% of mass lies within ±1σ, 95.45% within ±2σ, 99.73% within ±3σ of the mean. Exact via \(\text{erf}(k/\sqrt{2})\).

Q3 (Medium): What is the MGF of N(μ, σ²) and how do you use it to find E[X²]?
A: \(M(t) = \exp(\mu t + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 t^2)\). To find \(\mathbb{E}[X^2]\): compute \(M''(t)\) and evaluate at \(t=0\). We get \(M''(t) = [\sigma^2 + (\mu + \sigma^2 t)^2]M(t)\), so \(\mathbb{E}[X^2] = M''(0) = \sigma^2 + \mu^2\).

Q4 (Medium): Why is the sum of independent Gaussians Gaussian?
A: MGF of a sum = product of MGFs. \(M_{X_1+X_2}(t) = M_{X_1}(t)\cdot M_{X_2}(t) = \exp([\mu_1+\mu_2]t + \tfrac{1}{2}[\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2]t^2)\), which is the MGF of \(\mathcal{N}(\mu_1+\mu_2, \sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2)\).

Q5 (Medium): What does it mean for the Gaussian to be max-entropy?
A: Among all distributions on \(\mathbb{R}\) with fixed mean and variance, the Gaussian maximizes differential entropy \(h(X) = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi e\sigma^2)\). This means it encodes the minimum additional structure — it's the least informative prior consistent with knowing only first and second moments.

Q6 (Hard): State and sketch the proof of the CLT.
A: Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) be i.i.d. with mean μ, variance σ². Then \(\frac{\bar X_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0,1)\). Proof via MGFs/characteristic functions: the CGF of the standardized mean is \(n K(t/\sqrt{n}) - \mu t\sqrt{n}/\sigma\). Taylor-expanding \(K\) around 0: \(K(s) \approx \mu s + \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}s^2 + O(s^3)\). Substituting and taking \(n \to \infty\) gives \(\tfrac{t^2}{2}\) — the CGF of \(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\).

Q7 (Hard): Why do all cumulants of order ≥ 3 vanish for Gaussians?
A: The cumulant generating function is \(K(t) = \log M(t) = \mu t + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2 t^2\). This polynomial is degree 2 exactly, so all derivatives of order ≥ 3 at \(t=0\) are zero. By the Marcinkiewicz theorem, the Gaussian is the only distribution with a CGF that is a polynomial of finite degree.

🎯 Key Takeaways
  • Gaussian = max-entropy distribution with fixed μ, σ² — minimal assumptions
  • MGF: \(M(t) = e^{\mu t + \sigma^2 t^2/2}\) — closed-form, generates all moments cleanly
  • CGF is degree-2 polynomial — only distribution with this property
  • Closure: linear combos of independent Gaussians are Gaussian
  • CLT: standardized sums converge to N(0,1) — explains ubiquity
  • Standardize via \(Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma\); use \(\Phi\) table for probabilities
