1
Understanding ScaNN

What is ScaNN? When you search for "similar products" or ask a recommendation system for the nearest match, the system must compare your query against millions or billions of vectors. Comparing every vector is too slow. ScaNN is Google's system that finds the approximate nearest neighbors at blazing speed — with accuracy rivalling exact search — by compressing vectors in a mathematically principled way.

Core Insight

Standard vector quantization minimizes reconstruction error uniformly across all data. ScaNN's key insight: not all reconstruction errors are equal. An error that changes the dot-product ranking of the top-k items is catastrophic; an error on vectors with low inner product with the query is harmless. ScaNN minimizes a score-aware loss that penalizes errors proportionally to how much they affect the MIPS result.

The Anisotropic Loss

ScaNN decomposes the quantization residual into two components: the parallel component (along the query direction, affecting dot-product score directly) and the perpendicular component (orthogonal, mostly harmless). It then applies a higher penalty weight \(\eta > 1\) to the parallel component, forcing the codebook to preserve inner products that matter for retrieval.

In the 2020 Google ANN benchmark, ScaNN achieved the highest queries-per-second at 90% recall@10 — beating FAISS, HNSW, and all other systems at the time.

2
ScaNN's Three-Stage Pipeline

ScaNN combines three techniques into a pipeline. Each stage filters and refines candidates, trading recall for speed at a tunable operating point.

  1. Partitioning — The database is partitioned into \(P\) clusters (via k-means or spherical k-means). At query time, only the top-\(k_p\) closest partitions are searched, pruning most of the dataset immediately.
  2. Anisotropic Quantization (AQ) — Vectors within each partition are compressed using a product quantizer trained with the score-aware (anisotropic) loss. Each sub-vector is mapped to one of \(2^{b}\) codewords. Dot-product scores are approximated by table lookups — extremely fast with SIMD.
  3. Re-scoring — The top candidates from AQ are re-ranked using the original float32 vectors (optionally with additional quantization). This rescoring step recovers recall lost during compression.
Objective Function (Anisotropic Loss)

Given query \(\mathbf{q}\) and database vector \(\mathbf{x}\), let \(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\) be the quantized version. ScaNN minimizes: $$L(\mathbf{x}, \tilde{\mathbf{x}}) = \eta \cdot \underbrace{\left(\frac{(\mathbf{x} - \tilde{\mathbf{x}}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{q}}}{\|\mathbf{q}\|}\right)^2}_{\text{parallel error}} + (1 - \eta) \cdot \underbrace{\left\|\mathbf{x} - \tilde{\mathbf{x}} - \frac{(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\cdot\hat{\mathbf{q}}}{\|\mathbf{q}\|^2}\mathbf{q}\right\|^2}_{\text{perpendicular error}}$$ where \(\hat{\mathbf{q}} = \mathbf{q}/\|\mathbf{q}\|\) and \(\eta > 1\) upweights the parallel (score-affecting) component.

Why not just minimize reconstruction MSE?

Standard Product Quantization minimizes \(\|\mathbf{x} - \tilde{\mathbf{x}}\|^2\) equally in all directions. For MIPS, what matters is the dot product \(\mathbf{q} \cdot \tilde{\mathbf{x}} \approx \mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{x}\). The perpendicular reconstruction error has zero first-order effect on the dot product, so penalizing it wastes codebook capacity that could be used to reduce the parallel error.

3
Mathematical Deep Dive

Maximum Inner Product Search (MIPS). Given a query \(\mathbf{q} \in \mathbb{R}^d\) and database \(\mathcal{X} = \{\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_n\} \subset \mathbb{R}^d\), find:

$$\mathbf{x}^* = \underset{\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{X}}{\operatorname{argmax}} \;\; \mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{x}$$

Score approximation error. Let \(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\) be the quantized approximation. The score error is:

$$\delta(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{q} \cdot \tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{q} \cdot (\mathbf{x} - \tilde{\mathbf{x}})$$

Decompose the residual \(\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{x} - \tilde{\mathbf{x}}\) into parallel and perpendicular components relative to the query direction \(\hat{\mathbf{q}}\):

$$\mathbf{r}_\parallel = (\mathbf{r} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{q}})\hat{\mathbf{q}}, \qquad \mathbf{r}_\perp = \mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}_\parallel$$

Then the score error is purely determined by the parallel component: \(\delta = \mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{r} = \|\mathbf{q}\| \cdot (\mathbf{r} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{q}}) = \|\mathbf{q}\| \|\mathbf{r}_\parallel\|\cos\theta\). The perpendicular component contributes zero to score error for any fixed query direction.

Anisotropic Loss Derivation. To train a codebook \(\mathcal{C} = \{c_1, \ldots, c_M\}\) that minimises expected MIPS ranking error, ScaNN (Guo et al. 2020) show that the following surrogate loss is a valid upper bound on the probability of a ranking error:

$$L_\text{AQ}(\mathbf{x}, \tilde{\mathbf{x}}) = \eta \|\mathbf{r}_\parallel\|^2 + \|\mathbf{r}_\perp\|^2, \quad \eta \geq 1$$

Setting \(\eta=1\) recovers isotropic (standard) reconstruction loss. As \(\eta \to \infty\), the loss collapses to \(\|\mathbf{r}_\parallel\|^2\) — pure score-preservation with no perpendicular penalty.

Product Quantization with AQ Loss. Vectors are split into \(M\) sub-spaces of dimension \(d/M\). Each sub-vector is quantized independently. The AQ loss couples the sub-spaces through the query direction decomposition, so codebook training uses a modified E-step/M-step:

$$c_j^* = \underset{c}{\operatorname{argmin}} \sum_{\mathbf{x}: \text{assigned to } j} L_\text{AQ}(\mathbf{x}^{(m)}, c)$$

Inner product approximation at query time. After quantization, \(\mathbf{x} \approx \tilde{\mathbf{x}} = [c_{i_1}^{(1)}, \ldots, c_{i_M}^{(M)}]\). The dot product becomes:

$$\mathbf{q} \cdot \tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \mathbf{q}^{(m)} \cdot c_{i_m}^{(m)}$$

This sum decomposes into \(M\) table lookups. With \(d=128\), \(M=8\), each sub-space has dimension 16, and \(2^8=256\) codewords per sub-space — scores from all 256 possibilities are precomputed in a lookup table of 256 floats, one per sub-space. The inner product for any database vector then costs just 8 table lookups + 8 additions.

Numerical Trace Example

Let \(\mathbf{q} = [1, 0]\), \(\mathbf{x} = [0.9, 0.5]\), two candidate codebook centers \(c_A = [0.85, 0.0]\), \(c_B = [0.7, 0.7]\).
Standard MSE: \(\|x - c_A\|^2 = 0.0025 + 0.25 = 0.2525\), \(\|x - c_B\|^2 = 0.04 + 0.04 = 0.08\) → picks \(c_B\).
Dot-product score: \(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{x}=0.9\). \(\mathbf{q}\cdot c_A=0.85\), \(\mathbf{q}\cdot c_B=0.7\). Error: \(c_A\) gives 0.05, \(c_B\) gives 0.20.
AQ loss (\(\eta=4\)): parallel residual for \(c_A\): \(|0.9-0.85|^2\cdot 4 = 0.01\); for \(c_B\): \(|0.9-0.7|^2\cdot 4 = 0.16\) → picks \(c_A\). Correct!

When to Use ScaNN

Use ScaNN when: (1) your metric is inner product / cosine similarity (not L2), (2) you need billion-scale throughput, (3) you can afford offline index training time, and (4) memory is a constraint (AQ compresses 32x vs full float). Use HNSW when you need insertions at runtime. Use DiskANN when your index doesn't fit in RAM.

6
Key Takeaways
  • MIPS ≠ L2 search. Most ANN research targets L2. ScaNN is specifically optimized for Maximum Inner Product Search — the setting used in embedding-based retrieval (transformers, recommendation).
  • Anisotropic loss is the key novelty. By upweighting the parallel (score-affecting) reconstruction error during quantization training, ScaNN preserves the ranking of top-scoring items far better than standard PQ at the same compression ratio.
  • Partition + AQ + Rescore is the recipe. Partitioning prunes 90%+ of the dataset. AQ compresses what remains. Rescoring with raw vectors recovers recall. The three stages are independently tunable.
  • SIMD-accelerated lookup. AQ dot products reduce to integer table lookups computable at ~10 ns per vector on modern CPUs with AVX2/AVX-512 instructions.
  • Hyperparameter: \(\eta\). The anisotropy parameter \(\eta\) trades off perpendicular vs parallel error. Empirically, \(\eta \in [2, 10]\) works well; higher values help at larger compression ratios.
