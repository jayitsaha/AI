1
Understanding the Taylor Expansion
▼

What is it? The Taylor expansion lets you approximate a complicated function near a known point using simpler polynomial terms. In multiple dimensions, the "polynomial" uses the gradient vector (first-order) and the Hessian matrix (second-order) — the multivariable generalizations of the first and second derivatives you know from calculus.

Analogy: Imagine standing on a hilly landscape and taking a step in direction \(\Delta\). Before stepping, you can predict your new height pretty well: "the slope tells me how fast I rise (first order), and the curvature tells me if the ground is concave or convex (second order)." The Taylor expansion formalizes this prediction, and it becomes exact as \(\Delta \to 0\).

Why it matters in ML: Almost every optimization algorithm in deep learning — gradient descent, Newton's method, trust-region methods, natural gradient — is derived by reasoning about a Taylor approximation of the loss surface. Understanding the matrix form is the gateway to understanding second-order methods.

If you remember one thing

For \(f:\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}\), the second-order Taylor expansion around \(x\) is: $$f(x + \Delta) \approx f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top \Delta + \tfrac{1}{2}\, \Delta^\top H(x)\, \Delta$$ where \(\nabla f\) is the gradient (slope in every direction) and \(H\) is the Hessian (curvature matrix). The first-order term is a dot product; the second-order term is a quadratic form.

From 1D to Matrix Form — Internal Mechanics
▼

Starting from 1D

In one dimension, Taylor's theorem gives \(f(x + \delta) = f(x) + f'(x)\delta + \frac{1}{2}f''(x)\delta^2 + \cdots\). Each coefficient is a derivative; each power of \(\delta\) captures finer structure of the function.

Extending to n Dimensions

For \(f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}\), replace scalar \(\delta\) with a vector \(\Delta \in \mathbb{R}^n\). The first-order term \(f'(x)\delta\) becomes the dot product \(\nabla f(x)^\top \Delta\). The second-order term \(\frac{1}{2}f''(x)\delta^2\) becomes the quadratic form \(\frac{1}{2}\Delta^\top H(x)\Delta\), where the Hessian matrix \(H_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i \partial x_j}\) captures all mixed second partial derivatives.

What the Hessian Tells You

The Hessian is symmetric (by Schwarz's theorem, assuming continuous second derivatives). Its eigenvalues encode the principal curvatures of the function:

  • All eigenvalues positive → positive definite → local minimum (bowl shape)
  • All eigenvalues negative → negative definite → local maximum
  • Mixed eigenvalue signs → indefinite → saddle point

The quadratic term \(\frac{1}{2}\Delta^\top H \Delta\) is the "curvature correction" — it tells you how much the tangent plane (first-order) overestimates or underestimates \(f(x+\Delta)\).

Key Design Decision — Why Quadratic?

We stop at second order for a practical reason: the second-order expansion is the lowest-degree polynomial that can detect curvature (and therefore minima/maxima). Going to third order requires a rank-3 tensor, which is \(O(n^3)\) — prohibitively expensive for large \(n\). Most ML uses only first or second order.

3
Why It Works — The Math
▼

Formal Statement (Multivariate Taylor)

Let \(f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}\) be twice continuously differentiable. Then for any \(\Delta \in \mathbb{R}^n\):

$$f(x + \Delta) = f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top \Delta + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\Delta^\top H(x)\,\Delta + O(\|\Delta\|^3)$$

The remainder \(O(\|\Delta\|^3)\) vanishes faster than \(\|\Delta\|^2\) as \(\Delta \to 0\).

Gradient and Hessian Definitions

$$\nabla f(x) = \begin{bmatrix} \partial f/\partial x_1 \\ \vdots \\ \partial f/\partial x_n \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^n, \qquad H(x) = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1^2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1 \partial x_n} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_n \partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_n^2} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$$

Deriving the First-Order Term

Define \(g(t) = f(x + t\Delta)\). By the chain rule, \(g'(t) = \nabla f(x + t\Delta)^\top \Delta\). Evaluating at \(t=0\): \(g'(0) = \nabla f(x)^\top \Delta\). This is the directional derivative of \(f\) at \(x\) in direction \(\Delta\).

Deriving the Second-Order Term

Differentiating again: \(g''(t) = \Delta^\top H(x + t\Delta)\Delta\). At \(t=0\): \(g''(0) = \Delta^\top H(x)\Delta\). The standard 1D Taylor expansion of \(g(1)\) then gives the matrix result: $$g(1) = g(0) + g'(0) + \tfrac{1}{2}g''(0) + \cdots = f(x) + \nabla f(x)^\top\Delta + \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta^\top H(x)\Delta + \cdots$$

Connection to Newton's Method

Newton's method for minimizing \(f\) finds the exact minimum of the second-order Taylor approximation. Setting the gradient of the approximation to zero:

$$\nabla_\Delta\bigl[\nabla f^\top\Delta + \tfrac{1}{2}\Delta^\top H\Delta\bigr] = \nabla f + H\Delta = 0 \implies \Delta_\text{Newton} = -H^{-1}\nabla f$$
Common Pitfall — The Approximation Only Holds Locally

The Taylor expansion is local: it is accurate only for small \(\Delta\). For large steps, the true function can diverge badly from the quadratic approximation. Trust-region methods explicitly constrain \(\|\Delta\| \le r\) to stay within the region where the approximation is valid.

Key Applications in ML
▼

Newton's Method

Minimize the second-order approximation at each step: \(\Delta = -H^{-1}\nabla f\). Converges quadratically near a minimum, but costs \(O(n^3)\) per step to invert \(H\). Practical variants (L-BFGS, Gauss-Newton) approximate \(H^{-1}\) cheaply.

Trust-Region Methods

Instead of constraining the step size indirectly (via learning rate), trust regions explicitly ask: "minimize the second-order approximation subject to \(\|\Delta\| \le r\)." The radius \(r\) is adapted based on how well the quadratic matches the true function — a direct use of Taylor remainder theory.

Natural Gradient

In probabilistic models, the Fisher information matrix plays the role of \(H\), measuring curvature in the space of probability distributions. The natural gradient step is \(\Delta = -F^{-1}\nabla \mathcal{L}\) — a second-order Taylor method in distribution space.

The Hierarchy of Approximations

Zeroth order: \(f(x+\Delta) \approx f(x)\) — constant. First order: \(+\,\nabla f^\top\Delta\) — adds direction (gradient descent). Second order: \(+\,\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta^\top H\Delta\) — adds curvature (Newton, trust region). Each order adds a new power of \(\|\Delta\|\) of accuracy.

Code Trace — Numerically Correct
▼
import numpy as np
# Function: f(x1, x2) = sin(x1)*cos(x2) + 0.3*(x1**2 + x2**2)
def f(x): return np.sin(x[0])*np.cos(x[1]) + 0.3*(x[0]**2 + x[1]**2)
def grad_f(x): return np.array([np.cos(x[0])*np.cos(x[1]) + 0.6*x[0],
-np.sin(x[0])*np.sin(x[1]) + 0.6*x[1]])
def hess_f(x):
H11 = -np.sin(x[0])*np.cos(x[1]) + 0.6
H12 = -np.cos(x[0])*np.sin(x[1])
H22 = -np.sin(x[0])*np.cos(x[1]) + 0.6
return np.array([[H11, H12],[H12, H22]])
x0 = np.array([0.6, 0.4]) # expansion point
Delta = np.array([0.5, 0.3]) # step vector
f0 = f(x0) # 0.6761
g = grad_f(x0) # [1.1202, 0.0201]
H = hess_f(x0) # [[0.0799, -0.3214],[-0.3214, 0.0799]]
# First-order approximation
f1 = f0 + g @ Delta # 0.6761 + 0.5661 = 1.2422
# Second-order approximation
f2 = f0 + g @ Delta + 0.5 * Delta @ H @ Delta # 1.2422 - 0.0346 = 1.2076
# True value
f_true = f(x0 + Delta) # 1.1916
print(f"f(x0) = {f0:.4f}") # 0.6761
print(f"First-order = {f1:.4f}") # 1.2422 (error: 0.0506)
print(f"Second-order = {f2:.4f}") # 1.2076 (error: 0.0159)
print(f"True f(x0+Δ) = {f_true:.4f}")# 1.1916
# Newton step: minimize the second-order approximation
delta_newton = -np.linalg.solve(H, g) # -H^{-1} @ grad
print(f"Newton step: {delta_newton}") # [-0.99, -3.73] -- descends to min
