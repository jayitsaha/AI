Angle-Based Outlier Detection (ABOD)

Why distances fail in high dimensions — and how angles save the day

Intermediate Classical ML Anomaly Detection O(n³) naive · O(n² log n) approx
1
Understanding the Concept

The core idea: An outlier is a point that "sticks out." In 2D you might spot it visually. But how do you find outliers in 100 or 1000 dimensions?

Distance-based methods break down in high dimensions — a phenomenon called the curse of dimensionality: all pairwise distances converge to the same value, making it impossible to distinguish near from far.

ABOD sidesteps this by using angles instead of distances. For a candidate point \(A\), consider every pair of other points \((B, C)\). Compute the angle \(\angle BAC\). An inlier (normal point) sits in a cluster and sees pairs of neighbours all around it — angles vary a lot, giving high variance. An outlier stands apart from the cloud; all other points look roughly in the same direction from its vantage — angles are concentrated, giving low variance.

Key Insight

The Angle-Based Outlier Factor (ABOF) of point \(A\) is the variance of the weighted angle \(\widehat{\angle BAC}\) over all pairs \((B,C)\). Low ABOF → outlier. High ABOF → inlier.

Everyday analogy: Imagine you're standing in a busy city square (inlier). People are all around you, so when you look at pairs of people, the angles between them span the full 360°. Now imagine you're standing alone in a field far from town (outlier). All the buildings and people are in the same direction — angles between pairs are small and similar. ABOD measures this spread of angles.

If you remember one thing

Outliers have low ABOF (all other points look the same direction). Inliers have high ABOF (other points span all directions). ABOF = variance of weighted angles over all point pairs.

2
How It Works Internally

For each candidate point \(A\) in a dataset of \(n\) points:

  1. Enumerate all pairs \((B, C)\) with \(B \neq A\), \(C \neq A\), \(B \neq C\). There are \(\binom{n-1}{2}\) such pairs.
  2. Compute the weighted cosine of the angle at \(A\):
    $$\text{wcos}(A;B,C) = \frac{\vec{AB} \cdot \vec{AC}}{\|\vec{AB}\|^2 \|\vec{AC}\|^2}$$
    The denominator squares the distances (one factor per edge) — this down-weights pairs where \(B\) or \(C\) is very far from \(A\), because distant points contribute less information about the local neighbourhood.
  3. Compute the ABOF score as the variance of all these weighted cosine values:
    $$\text{ABOF}(A) = \operatorname{Var}_{B,C \neq A}\!\left[\frac{\vec{AB}\cdot\vec{AC}}{\|\vec{AB}\|^2\|\vec{AC}\|^2}\right]$$
  4. Rank all points by ABOF ascending — the lowest scores are the outliers.
Why Weight by Distance Squared?

Without weighting, a single pair \((B,C)\) very close to \(A\) could dominate. The \(\|\vec{AB}\|^2\|\vec{AC}\|^2\) denominator dampens contributions from distant pairs, making the score focus on the angular structure of the nearest neighbourhood. It also makes the formula scale-invariant.

Approximation (FastABOD): Computing over all \(\binom{n-1}{2}\) pairs is \(O(n^3)\) — expensive for large \(n\). FastABOD uses only the \(k\) nearest neighbours of each point, reducing complexity to roughly \(O(n \cdot k^2)\). A small \(k\) (e.g., 10–30) preserves accuracy because distant pairs carry little weight anyway.

Common Misconception

The "angle" is not computed as \(\arccos(\cdot)\). The formula uses the cosine of the angle — no inverse trig needed, cheaper to compute, and preserves the monotone relationship: small angle → cosine near 1 → low variance → outlier.

3
Why It Works — The Math

Let \(\mathbf{x}_A, \mathbf{x}_B, \mathbf{x}_C \in \mathbb{R}^d\). Define difference vectors:

$$\vec{AB} = \mathbf{x}_B - \mathbf{x}_A, \quad \vec{AC} = \mathbf{x}_C - \mathbf{x}_A$$

The unnormalised cosine (the ABOD weighting kernel) is:

$$\kappa(A;B,C) = \frac{\vec{AB}\cdot\vec{AC}}{\|\vec{AB}\|^2\|\vec{AC}\|^2}$$

Note \(\frac{\vec{AB}\cdot\vec{AC}}{\|\vec{AB}\|\|\vec{AC}\|} = \cos\theta_{BAC}\), so \(\kappa = \frac{\cos\theta_{BAC}}{\|\vec{AB}\|\|\vec{AC}\|}\). The extra distance factors in the denominator provide the distance weighting.

Intuition in high dimensions: As \(d \to \infty\), distances concentrate (the relative difference between the nearest and farthest neighbour shrinks). However, the angular structure is preserved if the data has genuine geometric structure. Outliers truly do lie in directions not well-covered by the data manifold, so their angle variance is distinctively low even in high \(d\).

Dimension-Robustness

Kriegel et al. (2008) empirically showed that ABOD maintains discriminative power in \(d = 100, 1000\) where LOF and distance-based detectors degrade to near-random ranking. The angle variance concentrates more slowly than distances as \(d\) grows.

  • Use ABOD when: dimensionality is moderate-to-high (\(d \geq 10\)), clusters exist, and you want geometry-grounded scores.
  • Use FastABOD (k-NN approximation) for \(n > 10{,}000\).
  • Avoid ABOD for very large \(n\) without approximation, or when data is truly uniform (no cluster structure).
  • Implemented in: PyOD library (ABOD class), with both exact and FastABOD variants.
