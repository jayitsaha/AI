Understanding SfM & SLAM
▼

What is it? Multi-view geometry is the study of how 3D structures and camera motion relate to their 2D projections in multiple images. Structure from Motion (SfM) is the process of reconstructing a 3D scene (its "structure") and the camera poses (its "motion") from a set of unordered 2D images. Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) builds on this, adding real-time requirements and loop closure to create a consistent map while simultaneously tracking the camera's position within it.

Analogy: Imagine you're a detective trying to reconstruct a crime scene using only photos taken by tourists. You have many photos from different angles, but no direct measurements. SfM is like piecing together the relative positions of objects and where each photo was taken. SLAM is like doing this in real-time as you walk through the scene with your own camera, building the map as you go and knowing exactly where you are at all times.

💡 If you remember one thing

At its core, SfM/SLAM relies on matching features (distinct points) across multiple images. From these 2D correspondences, fundamental geometric constraints (like epipolar geometry) allow us to infer the 3D position of points and the 6-DoF pose (position + orientation) of each camera. This is often refined through a global optimization called Bundle Adjustment.

How SfM Works Internally
▼

The SfM Pipeline

A typical SfM pipeline involves several key steps:

  1. Feature Detection & Description: Identify salient points (e.g., corners, blobs) in each image that are robust to viewpoint changes (e.g., SIFT, SURF, ORB).
  2. Feature Matching: Find corresponding features between pairs of images. This creates 2D-2D correspondences.
  3. Geometric Verification (Epipolar Geometry): Filter out incorrect matches using geometric constraints. For any two views, corresponding points and the two camera centers lie on a plane (the epipolar plane). This defines epipolar lines.
  4. Camera Pose Estimation: From geometrically verified matches, estimate the relative rotation and translation between camera pairs. This is often done using the Essential or Fundamental matrix.
  5. Triangulation: Given two camera poses and two corresponding 2D points, compute the 3D position of the scene point.
  6. Bundle Adjustment: A large-scale non-linear optimization that simultaneously refines all camera poses and all 3D scene points to minimize the total reprojection error (the difference between observed 2D points and reprojected 3D points).

SfM vs. SLAM

While both aim to reconstruct 3D structure and camera motion, their primary goals and constraints differ:

  • SfM: Typically an offline process, taking a collection of images and producing a 3D model and camera trajectory. It can afford more robust (and slower) global optimization.
  • SLAM: An online, real-time process. It needs to localize the camera and build/update the map continuously. Key challenges include maintaining accuracy over time (drift) and detecting when the camera revisits a known area (loop closure) to correct accumulated errors.
🔑 Key Property

Both SfM and SLAM are inherently susceptible to scale ambiguity. Without a known reference length, the reconstructed 3D scene and camera trajectory can only be determined up to an unknown scale factor. For SLAM, this is often resolved by integrating IMU data or using a known initial motion.

Why It Works — The Math
▼

Epipolar Geometry: The Fundamental Constraint

For any two images of a 3D point \(X\) from camera centers \(C_1\) and \(C_2\), the point \(X\), and the two camera centers \(C_1\) and \(C_2\), all lie on a single plane called the epipolar plane. This constraint is expressed by the Essential Matrix (\(E\)) for calibrated cameras (known intrinsics) or the Fundamental Matrix (\(F\)) for uncalibrated cameras.

Given corresponding image points \(x_1\) in image 1 and \(x_2\) in image 2 (both in normalized coordinates for \(E\), or pixel coordinates for \(F\)), the epipolar constraint is:

$$x_2^\top E x_1 = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad x_2^\top F x_1 = 0$$

where \(E = [t]_\times R\) (with \(R\) being the relative rotation and \(t\) the relative translation between cameras, and \([t]_\times\) the skew-symmetric matrix of \(t\)). The Fundamental matrix \(F = K_2^{-\top} E K_1^{-1}\) relates to \(E\) via the camera intrinsic matrices \(K_1, K_2\).

This equation provides a powerful way to verify matches (a point \(x_2\) must lie on the epipolar line \(F x_1\)) and to estimate \(E\) or \(F\) itself from a sufficient number of matches (e.g., 8-point algorithm).

Triangulation: From 2D to 3D

Once the relative camera poses (\(R, t\)) are known, a 3D point \(X\) can be reconstructed from its 2D projections \(x_1\) and \(x_2\). This is called triangulation. Each 2D point \(x_i\) defines a ray from its camera center \(C_i\) through \(x_i\). The 3D point \(X\) is the intersection of these two rays. In practice, due to noise, the rays rarely intersect perfectly, so we seek the point \(X\) that minimizes the distance to both rays.

A common approach is to solve a linear system. For two cameras with projection matrices \(P_1\) and \(P_2\), a 3D point \(X\) projects to \(x_1\) and \(x_2\). This gives rise to the equations:

$$x_1 \times (P_1 X) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad x_2 \times (P_2 X) = 0$$

These can be stacked into an overdetermined linear system \(A X = 0\), which can be solved for \(X\) using SVD (Singular Value Decomposition).

Bundle Adjustment: Global Optimization

The estimated camera poses and 3D points from pairwise estimation and triangulation are often noisy and inconsistent. Bundle adjustment refines all parameters simultaneously by minimizing the total reprojection error over all points and all images:

$$\min_{R_j, t_j, X_i} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{j=1}^M \rho( \| x_{ij} - \pi(R_j, t_j, X_i) \|^2 )$$

where \(x_{ij}\) is the observed 2D projection of 3D point \(X_i\) in image \(j\), \(\pi(R_j, t_j, X_i)\) is the reprojection of \(X_i\) into image \(j\) using camera pose \((R_j, t_j)\), and \(\rho\) is a robust cost function (e.g., Huber loss) to mitigate the effect of outliers. This is a large, sparse non-linear least squares problem, typically solved with Levenberg-Marquardt or Gauss-Newton algorithms.

⚠️ Common Pitfall

Bundle Adjustment is a non-convex optimization problem. Its success heavily depends on a good initialization. Poor initial estimates of camera poses or 3D points can lead to convergence to a local minimum, resulting in an inaccurate reconstruction.

Key Takeaways
▼

When to use Multi-view Geometry, SfM, or SLAM:

  • 3D Reconstruction: Creating detailed 3D models of objects or environments from images (e.g., cultural heritage, digital twins, photogrammetry).
  • Augmented Reality (AR) / Virtual Reality (VR): Tracking user's head/hand pose in real-time to overlay virtual content correctly.
  • Robotics & Autonomous Driving: Enabling robots and self-driving cars to localize themselves within an environment and build maps for navigation.
  • Motion Capture: Tracking human or object motion from multiple camera feeds.
  • Surveillance: Reconstructing trajectories of objects or people across multiple cameras.

When NOT to use / Key Challenges:

  • Textureless Scenes: Lack of distinct features makes matching impossible (e.g., a blank white wall).
  • Purely Rotational Motion: Without translation, there's no baseline for triangulation, leading to scale ambiguity and poor 3D reconstruction.
  • Dynamic Environments: For SfM, moving objects in the scene complicate feature matching and can lead to incorrect 3D points. SLAM needs robust mechanisms to handle dynamic elements.
  • Computational Cost: Bundle adjustment can be very expensive for large-scale reconstructions, requiring efficient sparse solvers.
  • Drift: For SLAM, small errors in pose estimation accumulate over time, causing the map and localization to drift. Loop closure is crucial to correct this.
💡 Future Directions

Modern approaches increasingly integrate deep learning for robust feature detection, matching, and even direct pose regression or depth estimation, often combining traditional geometric methods with neural networks for improved performance in challenging conditions.
