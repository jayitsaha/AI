Understanding Conditional Probability
▼

What does "conditional" mean? An ordinary probability asks: "What fraction of all possible worlds have outcome A?" A conditional probability asks a narrower question: "Restricting attention to worlds where B already happened — what fraction of those have A?"

Formally, \(P(A \mid B)\) is read "the probability of A given B." The vertical bar means "given that we know." The defining formula is simply an area restriction:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \quad P(B) > 0$$

Think of it geometrically: the entire sample space shrinks to just the B region, and we ask what fraction of B is also A.

Real-World Analogy

You're in a room of 100 people. 40 play guitar. 15 play guitar and sing. What fraction of guitar players also sing? You naturally restrict to the 40 guitarists and count 15 singers among them: 15/40 = 37.5%. That's \(P(\text{sings} \mid \text{guitar}) = P(\text{sings} \cap \text{guitar}) / P(\text{guitar})\).

The One Thing to Remember

Conditioning = restricting the sample space. \(P(A \mid B)\) zooms in so B becomes the new universe, then measures A's share of that universe. Bayes' theorem is just this same idea rearranged.

2
Bayes' Theorem — The Prior-to-Posterior Update
▼

Bayes' theorem follows directly from the definition of conditional probability. Starting from:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \quad \text{and} \quad P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)}$$

Both share the same numerator \(P(A \cap B)\). Solve the second equation for \(P(A \cap B) = P(B \mid A) \cdot P(A)\) and substitute into the first:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A) \cdot P(A)}{P(B)}$$

In Bayesian language, we rename the terms to highlight the belief-updating interpretation:

$$\underbrace{P(H \mid E)}_{\text{posterior}} = \frac{\underbrace{P(E \mid H)}_{\text{likelihood}} \cdot \underbrace{P(H)}_{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{P(E)}_{\text{normaliser}}}$$

The Law of Total Probability (expanding the denominator)

When the hypothesis \(H\) can be true or false, we expand \(P(E)\) using the law of total probability:

$$P(E) = P(E \mid H) \cdot P(H) + P(E \mid \neg H) \cdot P(\neg H)$$

So the full Bayes' theorem with an explicit denominator is:

$$P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H) \cdot P(H)}{P(E \mid H) \cdot P(H) + P(E \mid \neg H) \cdot P(\neg H)}$$
Intuitive Reading

Prior \(P(H)\): your belief before seeing evidence. Likelihood \(P(E \mid H)\): how well the evidence fits the hypothesis. Posterior \(P(H \mid E)\): updated belief after seeing evidence. Bayes is the recipe for rational belief revision.

Common Mistake — Confusing P(E|H) with P(H|E)

This is called the prosecutor's fallacy. "The evidence is rare given innocence" [small \(P(E \mid H_\text{innocent})\)] does NOT mean "the defendant is probably guilty" [large \(P(H_\text{guilty} \mid E)\)]. You must account for the prior probability of guilt.

The Classic Medical Test: Why P(Disease | Positive) Is Low
▼

This is the canonical Bayes' theorem example, because the answer shocks almost everyone — and reveals why base-rate neglect is one of humanity's most common reasoning errors.

Setup

  • Prevalence (prior): \(P(\text{Disease}) = 0.01\) — 1% of the population has the disease.
  • Sensitivity (true positive rate): \(P(\text{Pos} \mid \text{Disease}) = 0.99\)
  • Specificity: \(P(\text{Neg} \mid \text{No Disease}) = 0.95\), so the false positive rate is \(P(\text{Pos} \mid \text{No Disease}) = 0.05\)

Computing P(Disease | Positive)

First, the total probability of a positive test:

$$P(\text{Pos}) = \underbrace{0.99 \times 0.01}_{\text{TP: }0.0099} + \underbrace{0.05 \times 0.99}_{\text{FP: }0.0495} = 0.0594$$

Then apply Bayes:

$$P(\text{Disease} \mid \text{Pos}) = \frac{0.99 \times 0.01}{0.0594} = \frac{0.0099}{0.0594} \approx \mathbf{16.67\%}$$
Counterintuitive Result

Even with a 99% accurate test, if you test positive you're still only ~17% likely to actually have the disease! This is because the disease is rare (1%). The false positives from the healthy 99% swamp the true positives from the sick 1%.

Frequency Tree (out of 10,000 people)

The tree makes it visceral: the false positives (495) vastly outnumber true positives (99) because the healthy pool is 99× larger.

Key Takeaways & Related Concepts
▼

When to think conditionally

  • Any ML classifier outputs \(P(\text{class} \mid \text{features})\) — a conditional probability.
  • Naive Bayes classifiers apply Bayes' theorem directly, assuming feature independence.
  • Bayesian neural networks maintain posterior distributions over weights.
  • A/B testing — updating beliefs about which variant is better given observed conversion data.

Three Failure Modes in Practice

FailureWhat HappensFix
Base-rate neglectIgnoring the prior \(P(H)\) — over-weighting a single piece of evidenceAlways multiply likelihood × prior
Prosecutor's fallacyConfusing \(P(E \mid H)\) with \(P(H \mid E)\)Use Bayes explicitly
Wrong reference classUsing the wrong prior for the populationDefine the event space carefully
Connection to Machine Learning

Every supervised learning model estimates some form of \(P(y \mid x)\) — the conditional probability of label \(y\) given features \(x\). Generative models additionally learn \(P(x \mid y)\) and \(P(y)\), then use Bayes' theorem to classify. The entire probabilistic ML toolkit is built on these two pages of probability theory.
