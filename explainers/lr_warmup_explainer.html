Learning Rate Warmup Strategies

Ramp the learning rate from near-zero before decaying — stabilizing early training for Transformers and large-batch settings

Understanding LR Warmup
▼

What is it? When training a neural network, the learning rate (LR) controls how large each parameter update is. Learning rate warmup starts training with a very small LR — near zero — and gradually ramps it up to the target value over the first few hundred or thousand steps, before any further decay. This brief ramp-up phase, known as warmup, stabilizes early training and prevents the optimizer from making destructively large updates before it has seen enough data to form reliable gradient estimates.

Analogy: Think of it like warming up a car engine on a cold morning. If you immediately floor the accelerator, you risk damaging the cold engine. Instead, you let it idle for a moment so the oil circulates and temperatures equalize. Warmup does the same for your optimizer: it lets the adaptive statistics (like Adam's second-moment estimates) stabilize before committing to large parameter updates.

💡 If you remember one thing

Warmup prevents catastrophically large early updates from adaptive optimizers like Adam, where the second-moment estimate \(\hat{v}_t\) is unreliable (near-zero) during the first few steps, making the effective step size \(\alpha / \sqrt{\hat{v}_t}\) dangerously large. Warmup keeps \(\alpha\) small until these statistics stabilize.

The Three Classic Schedules

Linear warmup + constant: LR ramps from 0 to \(\alpha_{\max}\) linearly over \(T_w\) steps, then stays fixed. Simple and effective for many settings.

Linear warmup + cosine decay: LR ramps linearly to \(\alpha_{\max}\), then decays following a half-cosine curve to near-zero. Widely used in vision and language model pre-training (GPT, BERT, ViT).

Noam schedule (Transformer paper): A single closed-form formula — no separate phases — that automatically produces warmup then decay: \(\alpha_t = d_{\text{model}}^{-0.5} \cdot \min(t^{-0.5},\, t \cdot T_w^{-1.5})\). Peak occurs exactly at step \(T_w\).

Why Warmup Is Necessary — The Adam Variance Problem
▼

The Root Cause: Underestimated Second Moments

Adam maintains a running estimate of the squared gradient (the second moment \(v_t\)) via exponential moving average with decay \(\beta_2 \approx 0.999\). At step \(t=1\), there is only one gradient observation, so:

$$v_1 = (1 - \beta_2)\,g_1^2 = 0.001\,g_1^2$$

The bias-corrected estimate is \(\hat{v}_1 = v_1 / (1-\beta_2^1) = g_1^2\), which seems fine — but in practice, a single noisy gradient gives a very unreliable estimate of the true gradient variance. The effective per-parameter step size in Adam is:

$$\Delta\theta_t = -\frac{\alpha \cdot \hat{m}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \epsilon}$$

If \(\hat{v}_t\) is small (underestimated) due to insufficient data, the denominator is tiny and \(|\Delta\theta_t|\) can be enormous, pushing parameters far from their initialization — often into bad loss landscape regions from which recovery takes many more steps.

Large-Batch Training Makes It Worse

With very large batches, each gradient update covers more data, so gradients in early steps carry higher variance and may be systematically misaligned with the true gradient direction (due to large gradient noise at initialization). Linear warmup provides a principled damping: during the warmup phase, even if \(\hat{v}_t\) is small, the low \(\alpha\) keeps updates small.

🔑 Key Property: The Bias-Correction / Warmup Duality

Adam's bias correction \((1-\beta_2^t)^{-1}\) partially compensates for early underestimation, but it inflates \(\hat{v}_t\) to a single-step estimate which has high variance. Warmup provides a complementary safety net: even if \(\hat{v}_t\) is wrong, a small \(\alpha\) limits damage. Together they ensure stable early training.

The Math — Three Exact Schedules
▼

Schedule 1: Linear Warmup + Cosine Decay

Let \(T_w\) be warmup steps, \(T\) total steps, \(\alpha_{\max}\) peak LR. The schedule is piecewise:

$$\alpha_t = \begin{cases} \dfrac{t}{T_w}\,\alpha_{\max} & t \le T_w \\ \dfrac{\alpha_{\max}}{2}\!\left(1 + \cos\!\left(\pi\,\dfrac{t - T_w}{T - T_w}\right)\right) & t > T_w \end{cases}$$

At \(t = T_w\) both branches give \(\alpha_{\max}\), so the schedule is continuous (though not differentiable at the junction). At \(t = T\) it reaches zero (or a small \(\alpha_{\min}\) if added).

Schedule 2: The Noam (Transformer) Schedule

Vaswani et al. (2017) introduced a single unified formula, parameterized by model dimension \(d_{\text{model}}\) and warmup steps \(T_w\):

$$\alpha_t = d_{\text{model}}^{-1/2} \cdot \min\!\left(t^{-1/2},\; t \cdot T_w^{-3/2}\right)$$

During warmup (\(t \le T_w\)): the second argument is smaller, giving \(\alpha_t = d_{\text{model}}^{-1/2} \cdot t \cdot T_w^{-3/2}\) — linear in \(t\). After warmup (\(t > T_w\)): the first argument is smaller, giving \(\alpha_t = d_{\text{model}}^{-1/2} \cdot t^{-1/2}\) — inverse-square-root decay. The peak occurs at \(t = T_w\):

$$\alpha_{\max}^{\text{Noam}} = d_{\text{model}}^{-1/2} \cdot T_w^{-1/2}$$

For \(d_{\text{model}} = 512\), \(T_w = 4000\): \(\alpha_{\max} = 512^{-0.5} \cdot 4000^{-0.5} = 0.0442 \cdot 0.0158 \approx 6.99\times10^{-4}\).

Derivation: Why Peak at \(t = T_w\)?

Set the two branches equal: \(t^{-1/2} = t \cdot T_w^{-3/2}\), which gives \(t^{-3/2} = T_w^{-3/2}\), i.e., \(t = T_w\). For \(t < T_w\) the ramp slope \(T_w^{-3/2}\) dominates; for \(t > T_w\) the decay \(t^{-1/2}\) dominates. The \(\min\) automatically switches between them. This elegant single-expression design was chosen to avoid a manually tuned \(\alpha_{\max}\) — the peak LR is fully determined by \(d_{\text{model}}\) and \(T_w\).

⚠️ Common Misconception

Warmup does NOT guarantee convergence and does NOT replace proper LR tuning. If \(\alpha_{\max}\) is already too large, warmup merely delays the explosion. Warmup addresses the initialization instability of adaptive optimizers — it is not a substitute for choosing a sensible peak LR. Additionally, for SGD (no adaptive moments), warmup is less critical but still benefits large-batch regimes.

Key Takeaways & When to Use
▼

When warmup is essential

  • Training Transformers with Adam (original paper used \(T_w = 4000\), Noam schedule)
  • Large-batch training (batch sizes > 1024) where early gradient estimates are noisy
  • Fine-tuning pre-trained models (prevents immediate destruction of learned representations)
  • Any setting where Adam diverges in the first few hundred steps

Choosing warmup length \(T_w\)

A common heuristic: \(T_w \approx 0.01\text{–}0.05 \times T_{\text{total}}\). For BERT pre-training, 10,000 warmup steps out of ~1M (1%). For GPT-2, ~2,000 warmup. For ViT, typically 10,000. Too short and Adam instability persists; too long and you waste training budget on sub-optimal LR.

💡 Practitioner Tip

If training loss spikes or diverges in the first few steps, the first fix to try is increasing warmup length. If loss is stable early but plateaus too quickly, reduce warmup length or increase \(\alpha_{\max}\). Warmup is typically the most robust intervention against early training instability.
