Understanding Spectral Clustering
▼

What is it? Imagine you have a group of people and want to split them into friend groups. K-Means would group people by how physically close they are. Spectral clustering instead says: "Build a graph where friends are connected, then find natural communities in that graph — even if the communities are tangled in space." It clusters based on connectivity, not distance.

Analogy: Two interleaved spirals of string lying on a table. K-Means sees a jumble and cuts through the middle. Spectral clustering traces each spiral end-to-end and says "this strand and that strand are different objects." It discovers shape, not proximity.

The key idea: Map each data point to a new coordinate given by its position in the eigenvector space of the graph Laplacian. In this new space, clusters that were geometrically entangled become linearly separable — standard K-Means then finishes the job.

💡 If you remember one thing

Spectral clustering replaces the original coordinates with the eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian \(L = D - W\). These eigenvectors encode connectivity structure, transforming non-convex, entangled clusters into linearly separable blobs in a new space where K-Means works perfectly.

🔑 Prerequisites

To fully understand this topic, you should know: K-Means clustering, graph theory basics (adjacency matrix, degree), eigenvalue decomposition, and the concept of positive semi-definite matrices. Familiarity with the normalized cut criterion and spectral graph theory strengthens the intuition significantly.

2
Graph Laplacian & Its Eigenstructure
▼

Step 1: Build the Similarity Graph

Given \(n\) data points, form the symmetric affinity (weight) matrix \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\) using a Gaussian kernel:

$$W_{ij} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\|x_i - x_j\|^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), \quad W_{ii} = 0$$

The bandwidth \(\sigma\) controls locality. Small \(\sigma\) makes the graph sparse; large \(\sigma\) connects everything. Alternative: \(\epsilon\)-neighborhood graph (binary edges if distance < \(\epsilon\)) or \(k\)-NN graph.

Step 2: Degree Matrix D

The degree of node \(i\) is \(d_i = \sum_j W_{ij}\) — the total affinity weight of all its connections. The diagonal degree matrix is \(D = \operatorname{diag}(d_1, \ldots, d_n)\).

Step 3: Unnormalized Laplacian L = D − W

$$L = D - W$$

Properties of \(L\): (1) symmetric, (2) positive semi-definite (\(v^\top L v = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{ij} W_{ij}(v_i - v_j)^2 \geq 0\)), (3) has eigenvalue 0 with eigenvector \(\mathbf{1}\) (constant vector). The multiplicity of eigenvalue 0 equals the number of connected components in the graph. This is the central spectral fact: if two moons are nearly disconnected, there are two near-zero eigenvalues.

∑ Quadratic Form Interpretation

The quadratic form \(f^\top L f = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j} W_{ij}(f_i - f_j)^2\) measures the smoothness of function \(f\) on the graph. Minimizing \(f^\top L f\) subject to constraints finds functions that vary slowly between connected nodes — exactly what cluster indicator functions should do.

Eigenvalue Structure on Two Moons

With \(\sigma=0.3\) on 30 points (15 per moon), the normalized Laplacian \(L_\text{sym}\) has eigenvalues approximately: \(\lambda_0 \approx 0.000\), \(\lambda_1 \approx 0.030\), \(\lambda_2 \approx 0.077\), \(\lambda_3 \approx 0.139\), … The spectral gap between \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\) reveals \(k=2\) clusters. The eigenvector for \(\lambda_0\) is nearly constant; the eigenvector for \(\lambda_1\) assigns opposite signs to the two moons.

⚠️ Common Misconception

The Laplacian's eigenvalues do NOT depend on the coordinate positions of the points directly — only on the pairwise similarities \(W_{ij}\). Spectral clustering is invariant to geometric transformations that preserve distances but naturally adapts to the topology of the data manifold.

3
Normalized Cut & Algorithm Variants
▼

The Normalized Cut (N-Cut) Objective

For a partition of nodes into sets \(A\) and \(B = V \setminus A\), define: \(\operatorname{cut}(A,B) = \sum_{i \in A, j \in B} W_{ij}\) and \(\operatorname{assoc}(A,V) = \sum_{i \in A, j \in V} W_{ij} = \sum_{i \in A} d_i\). The normalized cut is:

$$\operatorname{N-Cut}(A,B) = \frac{\operatorname{cut}(A,B)}{\operatorname{assoc}(A,V)} + \frac{\operatorname{cut}(A,B)}{\operatorname{assoc}(B,V)}$$

This penalizes partitions that cut many edges and discourages tiny, unbalanced partitions. N-Cut minimization is NP-hard in general, but Shi & Malik (2000) showed that the continuous relaxation is solved by the second-smallest eigenvector of \(L_\text{rw} = D^{-1}L\) (the Fiedler vector of the random-walk Laplacian).

Shi-Malik Algorithm (L_rw)

Formulate the generalized eigenproblem:

$$L \mathbf{v} = \lambda D \mathbf{v} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad D^{-1}L \mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v}$$

Take the eigenvectors \([\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k]\) corresponding to the \(k\) smallest eigenvalues. Threshold \(\mathbf{v}_1\) (the Fiedler vector) at 0 to get a binary partition, or stack into a matrix and apply K-Means for \(k>2\). The random-walk interpretation: \(L_\text{rw}\) generates a Markov chain; eigenvectors reveal slowly-mixing (i.e., clustered) states.

Ng-Jordan-Weiss Algorithm (L_sym)

Instead of the generalized eigenproblem, form the symmetric normalized Laplacian:

$$L_\text{sym} = D^{-1/2} L D^{-1/2} = I - D^{-1/2} W D^{-1/2}$$

Compute the \(k\) smallest eigenvectors \([\mathbf{u}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{u}_k]\) of \(L_\text{sym}\) (standard symmetric eigendecomposition). Stack into matrix \(U \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k}\), then normalize each row to unit length: \(T_{ij} = U_{ij} / \|U_i\|\). Run K-Means on the rows of \(T\). Assign original point \(i\) to the cluster of row \(i\).

🔑 Connection Between L_sym and L_rw

If \(\mathbf{u}\) is an eigenvector of \(L_\text{sym}\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda\), then \(\mathbf{v} = D^{-1/2}\mathbf{u}\) is an eigenvector of \(L_\text{rw}\) with the same \(\lambda\). The algorithms produce the same clustering; \(L_\text{sym}\) is numerically preferable (symmetric → stable eigensolvers), while \(L_\text{rw}\) has the cleaner probabilistic interpretation.

⚠️ Choosing k

The number of clusters \(k\) should be chosen by the eigengap heuristic: find the largest gap \(\lambda_{k+1} - \lambda_k\) in the sorted eigenvalue sequence. A large gap after the \(k\)-th eigenvalue indicates \(k\) well-separated clusters. This is equivalent to looking for the "elbow" in the eigenvalue plot.

From-Scratch Implementation
▼

A minimal, numerically correct implementation of the Ng-Jordan-Weiss algorithm in NumPy:

import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import eigh
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans
def spectral_clustering_ngw(X, k, sigma=0.5):
"""Ng-Jordan-Weiss spectral clustering."""
n = X.shape[0]
# Step 1: Build Gaussian affinity matrix W
diffs = X[:, None, :] - X[None, :, :] # (n, n, d)
sq_dists = (diffs ** 2).sum(-1) # (n, n)
W = np.exp(-sq_dists / (2 * sigma**2)) # Gaussian kernel
np.fill_diagonal(W, 0) # no self-loops
# Step 2: Degree matrix and unnormalized Laplacian
d = W.sum(axis=1) # degree vector
D_invsqrt = np.diag(1.0 / np.sqrt(d)) # D^{-1/2}
L = np.diag(d) - W # L = D - W
# Step 3: Symmetric normalized Laplacian
L_sym = D_invsqrt @ L @ D_invsqrt # D^{-1/2} L D^{-1/2}
# Equivalently: L_sym = I - D^{-1/2} W D^{-1/2}
# Step 4: k smallest eigenvectors (eigh returns sorted ascending)
eigenvalues, U = eigh(L_sym, subset_by_index=[0, k-1])
# U[:, 0] = eigenvector for lambda_0 ≈ 0 (constant, encodes membership)
# U[:, 1] = Fiedler vector (separates clusters)
# Step 5: Row-normalize embedding matrix
norms = np.linalg.norm(U, axis=1, keepdims=True)
T = U / np.maximum(norms, 1e-10) # unit-norm rows
# Step 6: K-Means on T
labels = KMeans(n_clusters=k, n_init=10).fit_predict(T)
return labels
# Usage:
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons
X, y_true = make_moons(n_samples=200, noise=0.05, random_state=42)
labels = spectral_clustering_ngw(X, k=2, sigma=0.3)
# Achieves ~100% accuracy vs K-Means which fails on moons
⚠️ Production Gotchas

sklearn.cluster.SpectralClustering provides a production-grade implementation. Key hyperparameters: n_clusters (k), gamma (= 1/2σ² in the kernel), affinity ('rbf', 'nearest_neighbors', or precomputed). For large n, use eigen_solver='amg' or 'lobpcg' — full eigendecomposition is O(n³) and infeasible for n>10,000. The n_components parameter controls embedding dimension separately from k.

Key Takeaways & When to Use
▼

When to Use Spectral Clustering

  • Non-convex, non-globular clusters — rings, spirals, moons, manifolds
  • Clusters defined by connectivity not centroid proximity
  • Small to medium datasets (n < 5000; eigendecomposition scales as O(n³))
  • You can construct a meaningful similarity graph (domain knowledge for σ)
  • Number of clusters k is known (or discoverable via eigengap)

When NOT to Use

  • Large datasets (n > 10,000) — use approximate spectral methods or UMAP+K-Means
  • High-dimensional data without a good similarity measure — curse of dimensionality kills the affinity matrix
  • Clusters that are well-separated spheres — K-Means is simpler and faster
  • When σ tuning is impractical — spectral clustering is sensitive to bandwidth
