What Is the Kronecker Product?
▼

What is the Kronecker product? Given an \(m \times n\) matrix \(A\) and a \(p \times q\) matrix \(B\), their Kronecker product \(A \otimes B\) is the \(mp \times nq\) block matrix formed by replacing each entry \(a_{ij}\) with the scaled block \(a_{ij} B\):

$$A \otimes B = \begin{pmatrix} a_{11}B & a_{12}B & \cdots & a_{1n}B \\ a_{21}B & a_{22}B & \cdots & a_{2n}B \\ \vdots & & \ddots & \vdots \\ a_{m1}B & a_{m2}B & \cdots & a_{mn}B \end{pmatrix}$$

It is not commutative: \(A \otimes B \neq B \otimes A\) in general. But it is bilinear, associative, and compatible with transposes: \((A \otimes B)^\top = A^\top \otimes B^\top\).

💡 One-sentence intuition

The Kronecker product is a "distributed outer product" — it creates every pairwise scaling of the blocks of \(B\) by the entries of \(A\), yielding a systematically structured large matrix.

Key Algebraic Properties

These are used constantly in deriving matrix derivatives:

$$(A \otimes B)(C \otimes D) = (AC) \otimes (BD) \quad \text{(mixed-product property)}$$
$$(A \otimes B)^{-1} = A^{-1} \otimes B^{-1}, \quad (A \otimes B)^\top = A^\top \otimes B^\top$$
$$\operatorname{rank}(A \otimes B) = \operatorname{rank}(A)\cdot\operatorname{rank}(B)$$

The mixed-product property is the workhorse: it says that composing two Kronecker-structured linear maps gives another Kronecker-structured map, which is why they close under composition.

🔑 Why it appears in calculus

When a matrix expression is linear in a matrix variable \(X\), vectorizing via the vec operator turns matrix equations into standard linear-algebra equations — and the coefficient matrix is always a Kronecker product. The derivative \(\partial\,\mathrm{vec}(AXB)/\partial\,\mathrm{vec}(X)\) is exactly \(B^\top \otimes A\).

2
The vec Operator & Vectorization
▼

The vec operator converts a matrix into a column vector by stacking its columns top-to-bottom (column-major order). For \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}\):

$$\mathrm{vec}(X) = \begin{pmatrix} X_{:,1} \\ X_{:,2} \\ \vdots \\ X_{:,p} \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{np}$$

For example, with \(X = \begin{pmatrix}5&6\\7&8\end{pmatrix}\), we get \(\mathrm{vec}(X) = [5,7,6,8]^\top\) — column 1 first, then column 2.

⚠️ Column-major vs row-major

NumPy's default is row-major (C order: flatten('C')). For the vec-trick you must use column-major: flatten('F') or reshape(-1, order='F'). Mixing conventions breaks the identity \(\mathrm{vec}(AXB)=(B^\top\otimes A)\,\mathrm{vec}(X)\).

Why vectorize matrices?

Matrix equations like \(AXB = C\) cannot directly use standard linear algebra solvers because \(X\) appears inside a product. Vectorizing converts this to a standard linear system:

$$\mathrm{vec}(AXB) = (B^\top \otimes A)\,\mathrm{vec}(X) = \mathrm{vec}(C)$$

This is now a system \(Kv = c\) with \(K = B^\top \otimes A \in \mathbb{R}^{mq \times np}\), \(v = \mathrm{vec}(X)\), \(c = \mathrm{vec}(C)\). Solvable with standard tools — this is the core power of vectorization.

Other vec identities

$$\mathrm{vec}(ABC) = (C^\top \otimes A)\,\mathrm{vec}(B), \qquad \mathrm{tr}(A^\top B) = \mathrm{vec}(A)^\top \mathrm{vec}(B)$$

The trace identity shows that inner products of matrices correspond to dot products of their vectorizations — used heavily in deriving gradients of loss functions involving traces (like \(\mathcal{L} = \mathrm{tr}(A^\top B)\)).

📐 General Bilinear Form

For \(f(X) = a^\top X b\) where \(a \in \mathbb{R}^m, b \in \mathbb{R}^p\): the differential is \(df = a^\top\,dX\,b = \mathrm{tr}(b a^\top dX) = (b \otimes a)^\top \mathrm{vec}(dX)\). So \(\partial f/\partial\,\mathrm{vec}(X) = b \otimes a = a b^\top\) (as a reshaped matrix). This is the outer-product gradient.

3
The Vec-Trick: Deriving ∂vec(AXB)/∂vec(X)
▼

Setup

Let \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\), \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}\) (variable), \(B \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times q}\). Define \(F(X) = AXB \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times q}\). We seek the Jacobian \(\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{vec}(F)}{\partial\,\mathrm{vec}(X)} \in \mathbb{R}^{mq \times np}\).

Step 1 — Work column-by-column

Let \(e_j\) be the \(j\)-th standard basis vector. The \(j\)-th column of \(AXB\) is:

$$(AXB)_{:,j} = AX(B_{:,j}) = A(Xb_j) \quad \text{where } b_j = B e_j$$

So the \(j\)-th column of \(AXB\) depends on \(X\) only through \(Xb_j\).

Step 2 — Differential

Take the matrix differential (treating \(A, B\) as constants):

$$d(AXB) = A\,(dX)\,B$$

Vectorize both sides. The key identity \(\mathrm{vec}(M N P) = (P^\top \otimes M)\,\mathrm{vec}(N)\) applies with \(M=A, N=dX, P=B\):

$$d\,\mathrm{vec}(AXB) = d\,\mathrm{vec}(A\,(dX)\,B) = (B^\top \otimes A)\,d\,\mathrm{vec}(X)$$

Step 3 — Read off the Jacobian

Since \(d\,\mathrm{vec}(F) = J\,d\,\mathrm{vec}(X)\) and the map is linear, the Jacobian is the constant matrix:

$$\boxed{\frac{\partial\,\mathrm{vec}(AXB)}{\partial\,\mathrm{vec}(X)} = B^\top \otimes A \in \mathbb{R}^{mq \times np}}$$

💡 Why Bᵀ and not B?

The transpose appears because of column-major vec. When you vectorize \(A(dX)B\), the right factor \(B\) gets transposed to route the column of \(dX\) to the correct position in the output. Intuitively: \(B\) mixes the output columns of \(AXB\), so its action on the \(\mathrm{vec}\) index space is its transpose (the adjoint of the column-mixing operation).

Derivative of a scalar loss involving \(AXB\)

If \(\mathcal{L} = \ell(\mathrm{vec}(AXB))\) for some scalar \(\ell\), then by the chain rule:

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial\,\mathrm{vec}(X)} = (B^\top \otimes A)^\top \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial\,\mathrm{vec}(AXB)} = (B \otimes A^\top)\,\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial\,\mathrm{vec}(AXB)}$$

Unvectorized (matrix form), this is the familiar: \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial X = A^\top (\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial(AXB)) B^\top\), consistent with standard backprop.

K-FAC: Kronecker-Factored Curvature in Deep Learning
▼

The Kronecker product is not just theoretical — it appears at the heart of K-FAC (Kronecker-Factored Approximate Curvature), a practical second-order optimizer used to train large neural networks faster.

The Natural Gradient and the Fisher Information Matrix

Gradient descent moves in parameter space. The natural gradient moves in function space by pre-multiplying the gradient by \(F^{-1}\), where \(F\) is the Fisher information matrix:

$$\tilde{\nabla}_\theta \mathcal{L} = F(\theta)^{-1} \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}, \qquad F(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\partial \log p(y|x,\theta)}{\partial \theta}\frac{\partial \log p(y|x,\theta)}{\partial \theta}^\top\right]$$

The problem: for a network with \(d\) parameters, \(F \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}\) is enormous. For \(d = 10^8\), storing \(F\) is impossible.

K-FAC's Kronecker Approximation

For a linear layer \(h = W a\) (where \(a\) is the input activation, \(h\) the pre-activation output), the gradient is \(\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial W = g\,a^\top\) where \(g = \partial\mathcal{L}/\partial h\). The Fisher block for layer \(W\) is:

$$F_W = \mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{vec}(g a^\top)\,\mathrm{vec}(g a^\top)^\top\right] = \mathbb{E}\left[(a \otimes g)(a \otimes g)^\top\right]$$

K-FAC approximates this by assuming \(a\) and \(g\) are statistically independent (they are not, but close in practice):

$$F_W \approx \mathbb{E}[a a^\top] \otimes \mathbb{E}[g g^\top] = A \otimes G$$

where \(A = \mathbb{E}[aa^\top]\) (activation covariance) and \(G = \mathbb{E}[gg^\top]\) (gradient covariance). The Kronecker factorization immediately gives the inverse:

$$(A \otimes G)^{-1} = A^{-1} \otimes G^{-1}$$
⚡ Computational Savings

If \(W \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{out} \times n_{in}}\), inverting the full \(F_W \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{in}n_{out} \times n_{in}n_{out}}\) costs \(O(n_{in}^3 n_{out}^3)\). With K-FAC: invert \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{in}\times n_{in}}\) and \(G \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{out}\times n_{out}}\) separately — cost \(O(n_{in}^3 + n_{out}^3)\). For large layers this is a million-fold speedup.

Applying the K-FAC Natural Gradient Update

The natural gradient update for \(W\) becomes (using the Kronecker identity):

$$\Delta W = \mathrm{unvec}\!\left((A^{-1}\otimes G^{-1})\,\mathrm{vec}(\nabla_W \mathcal{L})\right) = G^{-1}\,(\nabla_W \mathcal{L})\,A^{-1}$$

The last step uses the vec-trick in reverse: \(\mathrm{unvec}((C^\top\otimes D)\,\mathrm{vec}(M)) = D\,M\,C\). So the update is just two matrix multiplications — no explicit Kronecker product needed! K-FAC stores only \(A\) and \(G\) (periodically updated from mini-batches), amortizing the curvature computation over many steps.

📐 Connection to vec-trick

The K-FAC update \(G^{-1} \Delta W A^{-1}\) is precisely \(AXB\) form with \(A \leftarrow G^{-1}\), \(X \leftarrow \nabla_W\mathcal{L}\), \(B \leftarrow A^{-1}\). So the whole natural gradient step for a layer is one instance of the vec-trick running in reverse.

Interview Q&A & Comparisons
▼

Interview Questions

Q1. What is \(A \otimes B\) if \(A = \begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix}\) and \(B = I_2\)?

Answer: \(A \otimes I_2 = \begin{pmatrix}I_2 & 2I_2 \\ 3I_2 & 4I_2\end{pmatrix}\), a 4×4 block-diagonal-like matrix. Note \(A \otimes I_n\) and \(I_m \otimes B\) commute: \((A\otimes I)(I\otimes B) = A\otimes B\).

Q2. What is the derivative \(\partial\,\mathrm{vec}(X^2)/\partial\,\mathrm{vec}(X)\)?

Answer: \(X^2 = X \cdot X\), so by the product rule in the differential: \(d(X^2) = (dX)X + X(dX)\). Vectorizing: \((I\otimes X + X^\top\otimes I)\,\mathrm{vec}(dX)\). So the Jacobian is \(I_n \otimes X + X^\top \otimes I_n\). (For general analytic functions of matrices, this generalizes via the Fréchet derivative.)

Q3. Why does K-FAC work well despite the independence approximation?

Empirically, activations and gradients are weakly correlated in well-trained networks. The Kronecker factorization captures the dominant block structure of the Fisher. The approximation improves as layers become wider. Moreover, K-FAC's update direction is still a descent direction even when the approximation is imperfect.

Q4. What is the commutation matrix and how does it relate to Kronecker products?

The commutation matrix \(K_{m,n}\) is the permutation matrix such that \(K_{m,n}\,\mathrm{vec}(X) = \mathrm{vec}(X^\top)\). It satisfies \(K_{m,n}(A\otimes B)K_{p,q}^\top = B\otimes A\), showing that swapping Kronecker factors is a similarity via commutation matrices. Key identity: \(A\otimes B = K_{m,p}(B\otimes A)K_{n,q}\).
