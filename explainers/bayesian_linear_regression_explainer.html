Understanding the Concept
▾

Standard linear regression finds a single best set of weights \(\mathbf{w}\) — a point estimate. Bayesian linear regression treats \(\mathbf{w}\) as a random variable with a prior distribution. After observing data, we update that prior into a posterior distribution. Instead of one line, we have a distribution over lines.

The Core Bayesian Loop

Prior belief about weights \(\mathbf{w}\) + observed data \(\mathcal{D}\) → posterior belief about \(\mathbf{w}\). For new input \(\mathbf{x}_*\), we integrate over all plausible weight vectors to get a predictive distribution — not just a prediction, but a prediction with calibrated uncertainty.

Key Insight

With a Gaussian prior and Gaussian noise, the posterior is also Gaussian — the model is analytically tractable. No MCMC needed. Uncertainty is exact, not approximate.

Real-World Analogy

Imagine estimating how much coffee improves productivity. Before any experiment you have a vague belief (prior). Each data point narrows your belief, but you never become infinitely certain — you always maintain a distribution. In regions where you have no data, your uncertainty is high; near many observations, it is low. This is Bayesian linear regression: principled uncertainty tracking from prior to posterior to prediction.

If You Remember One Thing

MAP estimation with a zero-mean Gaussian prior on \(\mathbf{w}\) is exactly equivalent to ridge regression. The prior precision \(\alpha\) plays the role of the ridge penalty \(\lambda\). Bayes gives you ridge plus full uncertainty quantification for free.
