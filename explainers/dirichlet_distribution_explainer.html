The 30-Second Version
▼

What is it? Imagine you're trying to decide how much of a pizza to assign to three toppings — cheese, pepperoni, and mushroom — where the fractions must sum to 1. The Dirichlet distribution tells you: "here are all the plausible ways to split the pizza." It is a probability distribution whose outputs are themselves probability vectors (like [0.5, 0.3, 0.2]). This makes it the natural tool for modeling uncertainty about category proportions.

Real-world analogy: Think of a language model predicting the next word. The model outputs a probability vector over its entire vocabulary. The Dirichlet says: "I'm uncertain about what that probability vector should be — here's my prior belief over all possible vectors." Before seeing any text, you might use a flat Dirichlet (all options equally plausible). After reading thousands of documents, the Dirichlet sharpens toward the vectors actually observed.

One-sentence summary: The Dirichlet is a probability distribution over probability distributions — it lives on the simplex, its parameter \(\alpha\) controls sparsity vs. uniformity, and it is the conjugate prior for the Multinomial (Bayesian updates are just addition: posterior \(= \text{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha} + \mathbf{c})\)).

If you remember one thing

\(\alpha_k < 1\) → sparse (corners of the simplex, spiky distributions). \(\alpha_k = 1\) → uniform (flat prior, maximum ignorance). \(\alpha_k > 1\) → smooth (concentrated near the center, all categories share probability). And after observing multinomial counts \(\mathbf{c}\), the posterior is literally just \\(\text{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha} + \mathbf{c})\\).

Historical Context & Motivation
▼

The Problem Being Solved

By the mid-20th century, probability theory had powerful tools for scalar random variables and Gaussian vectors. But a crucial gap remained: how do you place a probability distribution over probability vectors themselves? This is exactly what Bayesian inference demands when the unknown is a set of category proportions (e.g., "what fraction of my coin tosses are heads?" extended to many outcomes).

Johann Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet (1805–1859)

The Dirichlet distribution is named after the German mathematician famous for Dirichlet series and number theory. The distribution bears his name because of its connection to the Dirichlet integral, which evaluates the normalizing constant. However, the explicit statistical distribution was systematically studied in the 20th century.

Conjugate Priors: The Key Insight (1950s–1960s)

Harold Jeffreys, Dennis Lindley, and Howard Raiffa & Robert Schlaifer's 1961 book Applied Statistical Decision Theory formalized the concept of conjugate priors — priors that produce posteriors in the same distribution family. The Dirichlet is the conjugate prior for the Categorical and Multinomial likelihood. This means the Bayesian posterior update is closed-form and instant: no MCMC needed. For applications requiring thousands or millions of Bayesian updates (like document modeling), this efficiency is essential.

Latent Dirichlet Allocation (2003)

The field transformed when David Blei, Andrew Ng, and Michael Jordan introduced LDA in Journal of Machine Learning Research (2003). By placing Dirichlet priors on both document-topic and topic-word distributions, LDA provided the first scalable unsupervised framework for discovering latent topics in large text corpora. This directly drove renewed interest in Dirichlet-process methods and Bayesian nonparametrics throughout the 2000s–2010s.

Why It Matters Now

Dirichlet priors appear throughout modern ML: in LDA-style topic models, as regularizers in Bayesian neural networks, in Dirichlet-process mixture models for infinite clustering, in concentration parameters for attention distributions, and in meta-learning (Bayesian few-shot classification). The "softmax + temperature" in language models is the frequentist analog — the Dirichlet provides the Bayesian version with principled uncertainty.

Core Concepts & Mathematics
▼

The Support: The Probability Simplex

A \(K\)-dimensional Dirichlet lives on the standard \((K-1)\)-simplex: $$\Delta^{K-1} = \left\{ \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^K : x_k \ge 0,\; \sum_{k=1}^K x_k = 1 \right\}$$ For \(K=3\) this is a triangle (visualized in Card 4). Each interior point is a valid categorical distribution over \(K\) outcomes.

The PDF

For \(\mathbf{x} \in \Delta^{K-1}\) and concentration parameter \(\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_K)\) with all \(\alpha_k > 0\):

$$p(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \frac{1}{B(\boldsymbol{\alpha})} \prod_{k=1}^K x_k^{\alpha_k - 1}$$

where the normalizing constant is the multivariate Beta function:

$$B(\boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \frac{\prod_{k=1}^K \Gamma(\alpha_k)}{\Gamma\!\left(\sum_{k=1}^K \alpha_k\right)}$$

Moments

Let \(\alpha_0 = \sum_{k=1}^K \alpha_k\) (the "concentration"). Then:

$$\mathbb{E}[x_k] = \frac{\alpha_k}{\alpha_0}, \qquad \text{Var}(x_k) = \frac{\alpha_k(\alpha_0-\alpha_k)}{\alpha_0^2(\alpha_0+1)}, \qquad \text{Cov}(x_j,x_k) = \frac{-\alpha_j\alpha_k}{\alpha_0^2(\alpha_0+1)} \;\; (j\neq k)$$

All categories are negatively correlated (if one goes up, others must go down — they share the probability budget). As \(\alpha_0 \to \infty\), variance → 0 and samples collapse to the mean \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}/\alpha_0\).

Conjugate Posterior Derivation

Suppose \(\mathbf{c} = (c_1,\ldots,c_K)\) are observed counts from \(\text{Multinomial}(n, \mathbf{p})\). The likelihood is:

$$p(\mathbf{c} \mid \mathbf{p}) \propto \prod_{k=1}^K p_k^{c_k}$$

With prior \(\mathbf{p} \sim \text{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\), the posterior is:

$$p(\mathbf{p} \mid \mathbf{c}) \propto \prod_{k=1}^K p_k^{c_k} \cdot \prod_{k=1}^K p_k^{\alpha_k - 1} = \prod_{k=1}^K p_k^{(\alpha_k + c_k) - 1} \implies \mathbf{p} \mid \mathbf{c} \sim \text{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha} + \mathbf{c})$$

This is conjugacy: the posterior is the same distribution family as the prior, with parameters updated by simple addition. No integrals, no MCMC — just add the counts to \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\).

Sampling Algorithm (Gamma Trick)

To sample \(\mathbf{x} \sim \text{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\): draw independent \(y_k \sim \text{Gamma}(\alpha_k, 1)\) for each \(k\), then normalize:

$$x_k = \frac{y_k}{\sum_{j=1}^K y_j}$$

Proof: the ratio of independent Gamma variables with the same rate is Dirichlet-distributed. This is the standard implementation in NumPy and SciPy.

Common Confusion: Dirichlet vs Dirichlet Process

The Dirichlet distribution (this topic) is finite-dimensional: a distribution over \(K\)-length probability vectors. The Dirichlet Process (DP) is its infinite-dimensional generalization: a distribution over infinite discrete probability measures. DP is used in Bayesian nonparametrics when \(K\) is unknown. They share the math of the Beta function but are conceptually distinct.

Applications: LDA & Topic Models
▼

LDA: Two Dirichlet Priors Working Together

Latent Dirichlet Allocation is the canonical application. The generative story for document \(d\):

  1. Draw document-topic proportions: \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_d \sim \text{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\) — a point on the topic simplex.
  2. For each word position \(i\) in the document:
    • Draw topic: \(z_i \sim \text{Categorical}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_d)\)
    • Draw word: \(w_i \sim \text{Categorical}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{z_i})\), where \(\boldsymbol{\phi}_k \sim \text{Dir}(\boldsymbol{\beta})\)

The sparse Dirichlet prior with \(\alpha \ll 1\) is crucial: it enforces the inductive bias that documents focus on few topics and topics focus on few words — a linguistically realistic assumption.

Pseudocounts Interpretation

The \(\alpha_k\) parameters act as pseudocounts: virtual observations added before seeing real data. Prior \(\text{Dir}(1,1,1)\) means "I've virtually observed each of the 3 categories exactly once." \(\text{Dir}(0.1, 0.1, 0.1)\) means "I've barely seen anything — any single dominant category is plausible." This is the Laplace smoothing (add-1 smoothing) of language models, extended to continuous distributions.

Other Key Applications

  • Bayesian Naive Bayes: Dirichlet prior over class-conditional word probabilities, avoiding zero-count problems.
  • Dirichlet-Multinomial regression: Model compositional data (e.g., microbiome relative abundances).
  • Reinforcement learning: Dirichlet prior over transition probabilities for model-based RL under uncertainty.
  • Mixture models: Dirichlet as prior on mixing proportions \(\boldsymbol{\pi}\) in a Gaussian Mixture Model.
  • Attention calibration: Dirichlet distributions used to model uncertainty over attention weights in Bayesian neural networks.
Interview: Marginal Likelihood

Integrating out \(\mathbf{p}\) from the Dirichlet-Multinomial model gives the Dirichlet-Multinomial (Pólya) distribution: $$p(\mathbf{c} \mid \boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha_0)}{\Gamma(n+\alpha_0)} \prod_{k=1}^K \frac{\Gamma(c_k + \alpha_k)}{\Gamma(\alpha_k)}$$ This closed-form marginal likelihood is why Bayesian LDA inference is tractable via variational EM or collapsed Gibbs sampling — no need to explicitly maintain samples of \(\boldsymbol{\theta}_d\).

Code Trace — From Scratch & Production
▼
import numpy as np
from scipy.special import gammaln, digamma
# ── From scratch: sample Dirichlet via Gamma trick ──
def dirichlet_sample(alpha, n_samples=1):
"""Sample x ~ Dir(alpha) using Gamma(alpha_k, 1) draws."""
gammas = np.random.gamma(alpha, 1.0, size=(n_samples, len(alpha)))
return gammas / gammas.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
# ── Log-probability of a sample ──
def dirichlet_logpdf(x, alpha):
"""log p(x|alpha) = log B(alpha)^{-1} + sum((alpha_k-1)*log(x_k))"""
log_norm = gammaln(alpha.sum()) - gammaln(alpha).sum()
return log_norm + np.sum((alpha - 1) * np.log(x))
# ── Bayesian update: conjugate posterior ──
def dirichlet_posterior(alpha_prior, counts):
"""After observing 'counts', posterior = Dir(alpha + counts)."""
return alpha_prior + counts
# ── Demo ──
alpha = np.array([1.0, 1.0, 1.0]) # flat prior over 3 categories
samples = dirichlet_sample(alpha, 100) # 100 probability vectors
print("Sample:", samples[0].round(3)) # e.g. [0.31, 0.47, 0.22]
counts = np.array([3, 5, 2]) # observed: cat A=3, B=5, C=2
alpha_post = dirichlet_posterior(alpha, counts) # [4, 6, 3]
post_mean = alpha_post / alpha_post.sum() # [0.308, 0.462, 0.231]
print("Posterior:", alpha_post) # Dir([4, 6, 3])
print("Post mean:", post_mean.round(3)) # [0.308, 0.462, 0.231]
# ── scipy production usage ──
from scipy.stats import dirichlet as sp_dir
alpha_lda = np.full(10, 0.1) # sparse prior for 10 topics
theta = sp_dir.rvs(alpha_lda) # topic mixture for one document
print("LDA doc-topic:", theta.round(3)) # sparse: ~1-2 topics dominate
