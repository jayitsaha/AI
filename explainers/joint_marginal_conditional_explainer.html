Joint, Marginal & Conditional Distributions

How probability is structured when two random variables interact
Intermediate Probability & Statistics Part I · Section 3.2
1
Understanding the Concept
▼

Imagine you're studying how age and income relate in a population. Each person has both an age group and an income bracket. The joint distribution tells you the fraction of people in every (age, income) combination simultaneously. The marginal distribution asks: "Ignoring income, what fraction are in each age group?" And the conditional distribution asks: "Among people aged 30–40, what does the income breakdown look like?"

Real-World Analogy

Think of a spreadsheet: rows are one variable, columns are another, and each cell holds a count (or probability). The row totals are the marginal distribution of rows. The column totals are the marginal distribution of columns. A single row, renormalized, is a conditional distribution.

These three concepts are foundational to virtually all of probability and statistics. Bayesian inference, naïve Bayes classifiers, probabilistic graphical models, latent variable models (VAEs, HMMs), and causal reasoning all hinge on being able to decompose, marginalize, and condition on probability distributions.

If You Remember One Thing

Marginals come from summing (integrating) out variables; conditionals come from slicing then renormalizing; independence means the joint factors into the product of marginals.

2
Three Distribution Types — Definitions & Relationships
▼

1. Joint Distribution

For two discrete random variables \(X\) and \(Y\), the joint PMF is:

$$P(X = x, Y = y) = p_{XY}(x, y) \quad \forall\, x \in \mathcal{X},\, y \in \mathcal{Y}$$

It must satisfy: (a) \(p_{XY}(x,y) \geq 0\) for all \(x,y\), and (b) \(\sum_x \sum_y p_{XY}(x,y) = 1\). For continuous variables, replace the PMF with a joint PDF \(f_{XY}(x,y)\) integrating to 1 over \(\mathbb{R}^2\).

2. Marginal Distribution

The marginal PMF of X collapses the joint by summing out Y:

$$P(X = x) = \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} P(X=x,\, Y=y)$$

This is the law of total probability in disguise. For the continuous case: \(f_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{XY}(x,y)\,dy\). The marginal "projects" the 2D joint onto one axis, discarding all information about the other variable.

3. Conditional Distribution

Given that \(Y = y\) (with \(P(Y=y) > 0\)), the conditional PMF of X is:

$$P(X = x \mid Y = y) = \frac{P(X=x,\, Y=y)}{P(Y=y)}$$

This is Bayes' rule in its simplest form. Geometrically, it extracts a single row (or column) of the joint table and renormalizes it so the values sum to 1. The denominator \(P(Y=y)\) acts as a normalizing constant.

4. Independence

X and Y are independent iff for all \(x, y\):

$$P(X=x,\, Y=y) = P(X=x)\cdot P(Y=y)$$
Common Misconception

Uncorrelated ≠ Independent. Correlation is a linear measure. Two variables can be uncorrelated (zero covariance) yet strongly dependent (e.g., \(Y = X^2\) with \(X\) symmetric around 0). Independence is a strictly stronger condition that governs the entire joint structure, not just second moments.

3
The Mathematics — Laws & Key Identities
▼

The Multiplication Rule (Chain Rule of Probability)

The joint distribution can always be factored using the definition of conditional probability:

$$P(X=x,\, Y=y) = P(Y=y \mid X=x)\cdot P(X=x) = P(X=x \mid Y=y)\cdot P(Y=y)$$

For \(n\) variables, this generalizes to the chain rule:

$$P(X_1,X_2,\ldots,X_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(X_i \mid X_1,\ldots,X_{i-1})$$

Bayes' Theorem

Equating the two factorizations of the joint and rearranging gives Bayes' theorem:

$$P(X=x \mid Y=y) = \frac{P(Y=y \mid X=x)\cdot P(X=x)}{P(Y=y)}$$

Numerical check with our table — \(P(X=2 \mid Y=0)\):

$$P(X=2 \mid Y=0) = \frac{P(Y=0 \mid X=2)\cdot P(X=2)}{P(Y=0)} = \frac{(0.14/0.39)\times 0.39}{0.39} = \frac{0.14}{0.39} \approx 0.359$$

Law of Total Probability

The denominator in Bayes' theorem expands via marginalization:

$$P(Y=y) = \sum_{x} P(Y=y \mid X=x)\cdot P(X=x)$$

Independence Criterion — Equivalent Forms

The following statements are all equivalent:

  • \(P(X,Y) = P(X)\cdot P(Y)\)  (joint factors)
  • \(P(X \mid Y) = P(X)\)  (conditioning on Y gives no information about X)
  • \(P(Y \mid X) = P(Y)\)  (conditioning on X gives no information about Y)
  • \(\text{Cov}(f(X), g(Y)) = 0\) for all measurable \(f, g\)  (zero correlation across all transformations)

Covariance and Correlation

$$\text{Cov}(X,Y) = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}[X]\,\mathbb{E}[Y]$$

For our table: \(\mathbb{E}[X]=1.15\), \(\mathbb{E}[Y]=0.91\), \(\mathbb{E}[XY]=1.10\), so \(\text{Cov}(X,Y) \approx 0.054 \neq 0\) — confirming dependence. Independence would require \(\text{Cov}(X,Y)=0\) (necessary but not sufficient).

Key Identity — Conditional Expectation

The law of iterated expectations connects expectations across the joint: $$\mathbb{E}[X] = \mathbb{E}_Y\bigl[\mathbb{E}[X \mid Y]\bigr] = \sum_y \mathbb{E}[X \mid Y=y]\cdot P(Y=y)$$ This is the continuous-variable analogue of marginalizing the joint.

Key Takeaways & When to Use Each
▼
You want to…Use…Operation
Know the full probability landscape over two variablesJoint \(P(X,Y)\)Given directly
Know the distribution of X regardless of YMarginal \(P(X)\)Sum/integrate out Y
Update beliefs about X given new evidence Y=yConditional \(P(X|Y=y)\)Slice + renormalize
Check if two variables carry information about each otherIndependence testCheck \(P(X,Y)=P(X)P(Y)\)
Propagate uncertainty through a modelLaw of total probabilityMarginalize out hidden variables

Where These Appear in ML

  • Naïve Bayes: assumes conditional independence \(P(X_1,\ldots,X_n \mid Y) = \prod_i P(X_i \mid Y)\)
  • EM Algorithm: the E-step computes the conditional \(P(\text{latent} \mid \text{observed}, \theta)\)
  • VAEs: encoder approximates posterior \(q_\phi(z|x)\); decoder models \(p_\theta(x|z)\); marginal \(p_\theta(x) = \int p_\theta(x|z)p(z)\,dz\) is intractable
  • Causal inference: distinguishes \(P(Y|X=x)\) (observational) from \(P(Y|\text{do}(X=x))\) (interventional)
  • Attention mechanisms: attention weights are conditional probabilities \(P(\text{query attends to key}_i)\)
Golden Rule

Any joint distribution can always be written as a product of conditionals (chain rule). Any marginal can be recovered from the joint. But going the other direction — reconstructing the joint from marginals alone — is impossible without additional assumptions. The joint contains strictly more information than either marginal alone.

6
From-Scratch Code — NumPy Implementation
▼

A complete NumPy implementation of joint/marginal/conditional operations with verification:

import numpy as np
# 1. Define the joint distribution as a 2D array
# joint[i, j] = P(X=i, Y=j)
joint = np.array([
[0.10, 0.08, 0.06], # X=0
[0.15, 0.12, 0.10], # X=1
[0.14, 0.11, 0.14], # X=2
])
assert np.isclose(joint.sum(), 1.0), "Must sum to 1"
# 2. Marginal distributions
px = joint.sum(axis=1) # sum over Y (columns) → shape (3,)
py = joint.sum(axis=0) # sum over X (rows) → shape (3,)
print("P(X):", px) # [0.24, 0.37, 0.39]
print("P(Y):", py) # [0.39, 0.31, 0.30]
# 3. Conditional P(Y | X=x) — one row, renormalized
def conditional_y_given_x(joint, x_val):
row = joint[x_val, :] # P(X=x, Y=*)
return row / row.sum() # divide by P(X=x)
print("P(Y|X=1):", conditional_y_given_x(joint, 1))
# → [0.405, 0.324, 0.270] (sums to 1.0)
# 4. Conditional P(X | Y=y) — one column, renormalized
def conditional_x_given_y(joint, y_val):
col = joint[:, y_val] # P(X=*, Y=y)
return col / col.sum() # divide by P(Y=y)
print("P(X|Y=0):", conditional_x_given_y(joint, 0))
# → [0.256, 0.385, 0.359]
# 5. Test for independence
def is_independent(joint, tol=1e-6):
px = joint.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True) # shape (n,1)
py = joint.sum(axis=0, keepdims=True) # shape (1,m)
product = px * py # outer product = shape (n,m)
return np.allclose(joint, product, atol=tol)
print("Independent?", is_independent(joint)) # False
# 6. Build an independent joint from given marginals
def make_independent_joint(px, py):
return np.outer(px, py) # P(X=i)*P(Y=j)
indep = make_independent_joint(px, py)
print("Is indep itself independent?", is_independent(indep)) # True
# 7. Mutual information I(X;Y)
def mutual_information(joint):
px = joint.sum(axis=1);
py = joint.sum(axis=0)
mi = 0.0
for i in range(joint.shape[0]):
for j in range(joint.shape[1]):
if joint[i, j] > 0:
mi += joint[i,j] * np.log2(joint[i,j] / (px[i] * py[j]))
return mi # 0 iff independent
print("MI:", round(mutual_information(joint), 4)) # 0.0078 bits
Production Gotcha

In practice you often work with log-probabilities to avoid underflow. Use np.log(joint) - np.log(joint.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)) for conditional log-probs. Libraries like scipy.stats and torch.distributions handle the continuous case.
